Unit 5: Introduction to Production and Cost
• This chapter introduces fundamental concepts in economics related to production and cost.
• It is divided into two main sections: 
  ◦ The first covers the basics of production, including production functions, short-run production, and its stages.
  ◦ The second focuses on costs, differentiating between economic and accounting costs, and exploring short-run cost functions and their relationship with production functions.

1. Production and Production Functions
1.1 Definition of Production, Inputs, and Outputs

• Production:
  ◦ Definition: The process of combining various inputs to produce an output for consumption.
  ◦ It's the act of creating output in the form of a commodity or service that adds to the utility of individuals.
  ◦ Essentially, it's a process where inputs are converted into outputs.
  ◦ Example: Producing wheat on land using labor, capital, and seeds is an example of production.

• Inputs:
  ◦ Definition: Economic resources used in the production of goods and services.
  ◦ There are four basic inputs commonly used:
    ▪ Labour: Human effort used in production.
    ▪ Capital: Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services (e.g., machinery, buildings).
    ▪ Land: Natural resources used in production.
    ▪ Entrepreneurial Ability: The skill and willingness to combine the other factors of production, take risks, and innovate.

• Outputs:
  ◦ Definition: The result of the production process, where inputs are transformed into a final usable form.
  ◦ Outputs provide satisfaction to consumers.
  ◦ Outputs can be classified into two types:
    ▪ Tangible Outputs:
      - Characteristics: Physical products that can be touched and have a long-term physical existence.
      - Examples: Buildings, machinery, equipment, cars.
    ▪ Intangible Outputs:
      - Characteristics: Products that have value but are not physical objects; they cannot be seen or touched. They cannot often be tried out, inspected, or tested in advance.
      - Examples: Insurance coverage, consultancy services, computer software, educational training, health care services.

1.2 Types of Inputs
• Inputs are broadly categorized into two main groups:
  ◦ Fixed Inputs:
    ▪ Definition: An input whose quantity cannot be varied during the period under consideration.
    ▪ Examples: Building, machinery. These are typically fixed in the short run.
  ◦ Variable Inputs:
    ▪ Definition: An input whose quantity can be changed during the period under consideration.
    ▪ Examples: Raw materials, labor. These can be increased or decreased relatively quickly.

1.3 Periods of Production: Short Run vs. Long Run
• The classification of production periods is based on the flexibility of economic resources to respond to changes in the business environment.
  ◦ Short Run:
    ▪ Definition: A period of production in which at least one input is fixed, while the remaining inputs are variable.
    ▪ Implication: An increase in output in the short run can only be achieved by increasing the quantities of the variable inputs.
    ▪ Example: A producer can increase output by hiring more labor or using more raw materials, but cannot immediately build a new factory.
  ◦ Long Run:
    ▪ Definition: A period of production in which all inputs are variable; there are no fixed inputs.
    ▪ Implication: A firm can change the scale of its operations, such as installing a new plant or constructing a new factory building. All factors of production can be adjusted.
  ◦ Important Note: The terms "short run" and "long run" do not necessarily refer to specific calendar time periods (like months or years). They describe the nature of the economic arrangement of inputs and their ability to be changed in response to economic conditions.

1.4 Productivity Measures in the Short Run
• Productivity refers to the measure of output per unit input. In the short run, with one variable input (commonly labor) and fixed inputs, productivity can be measured in three key ways:
  ◦ Total Product (TP):
    ▪ Definition: The overall amount of output produced by all the factors of production employed over a given period.
    ▪ It is the total output achieved by using a combination of variable inputs with a specific amount of fixed inputs.
    ▪ Expressed in terms of quantity (Q).
  ◦ Average Product (AP):
    ▪ Definition: The output produced per unit of the variable input.
    ▪ Calculation: AP = TP / L (where L is the quantity of the variable input, typically labor).
    ▪ Average Product of Labour (APL): APL = TP / L
    ▪ Significance: A good indicator of the productivity of the variable input.
  ◦ Marginal Product (MP):
    ▪ Definition: The additional output produced by employing one extra unit of a single variable input, assuming all other inputs are held constant.
    ▪ Calculation: MP = ΔTP / ΔL (where ΔTP is the change in total product and ΔL is the change in the variable input).
    ▪ Significance: Shows the contribution of the last unit of the variable input.
  ◦ Interrelationship: The three measures - TP, AP, and MP - are derived from each other and are closely interrelated.

• Stage I: Increasing Returns
  ◦ Characterized by: TP increases at an increasing rate, AP increases, and MP increases.
  ◦ Occurs when adding more variable input leads to increasing efficiency and output.
  ◦ Ends when AP reaches its maximum.
• Stage II: Diminishing Returns
  ◦ Characterized by: TP continues to increase but at a diminishing rate, AP decreases, and MP decreases but remains positive.
  ◦ Occurs due to the law of diminishing marginal returns, where adding more of a variable input to fixed inputs eventually leads to smaller increases in output.
  ◦ This stage is considered the rational stage of production because output is increasing, and resources are being used efficiently.
  ◦ Ends when MP becomes zero (TP is at its maximum).
• Stage III: Negative Returns
  ◦ Characterized by: TP starts to decline, AP continues to decrease, and MP becomes negative.
  ◦ Occurs when the variable input becomes excessive relative to the fixed inputs, leading to inefficiency and a decrease in total output.
  ◦ A rational producer would never operate in this stage.


2. Cost of Production
2.1 Definition of Cost of Production
• Cost of Production:
  ◦ Definition: Generally refers to the monetary outlays associated with the production activity.
  ◦ It encompasses the total expenditures and sacrifices made throughout the entire process of producing and distributing goods and services.

2.2 Types of Costs of Production
• Costs can be classified in several ways, differentiating between explicit/implicit, economic/accounting, and fixed/variable costs.

2.2.1 Explicit and Implicit Costs
• Explicit Costs:
  ◦ Definition: Actual monetary payments or cash outlays made by business firms to outsiders who supply inputs or resources.
  ◦ These are tangible, out-of-pocket expenses.
  ◦ Also known as accounting costs.
  ◦ Examples: Wages paid to labor, rent for leased property, cost of raw materials, fuel, transport, power, interest on borrowed funds.
• Implicit Costs:
  ◦ Definition: Costs representing the value of non-purchased resources that are owned and used by the firm in its own production activities.
  ◦ These are opportunity costs - what the firm's own resources could have earned in their best alternative use.
  ◦ Examples: The salary the owner-manager could earn working elsewhere, the estimated rent of a building owned by the firm, the return on capital invested by the owner.

2.2.2 Economic Cost and Accounting Costs
• This distinction highlights how economists and accountants view and calculate costs.
  ◦ Economic Cost:
    ▪ Definition: Encompasses both explicit and implicit costs. Economists consider the opportunity cost of all resources used, including those owned by the firm.
    ▪ Formula: Economic Cost = Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs
    ▪ Significance: Provides a more comprehensive view of the true cost of production, essential for decision-making.
  ◦ Accounting Cost:
    ▪ Definition: Refers only to the monetary value of all purchased inputs used in production. It ignores the cost of non-purchased (self-owned) inputs.
    ▪ Considers only direct, out-of-pocket expenses.
    ▪ Equivalent to: Explicit costs.
    ▪ Examples: Wages, raw materials, depreciation, interest on borrowed funds, utility expenses.

2.2.3 Fixed and Variable Costs (in the Short Run)
• This classification is crucial for understanding short-run cost behavior.
  ◦ Fixed Costs (FC or TFC - Total Fixed Cost):
    ▪ Definition: Costs that do not vary with the level of output produced. They are incurred even if the firm produces nothing (output is zero).
    ▪ These are the costs associated with fixed inputs.
    ▪ Examples: Rent on property, interest on borrowed funds, salaries of administrative staff, depreciation of machinery.
    ▪ The Total Fixed Cost (TFC) curve is a horizontal line at a certain cost level.
  ◦ Variable Costs (VC or TVC - Total Variable Cost):
    ▪ Definition: Costs that directly vary with the level of output. When output is zero, variable costs are also zero.
    ▪ These costs increase as the firm expands its output.
    ▪ These are the costs associated with variable inputs.
    ▪ Examples: Wages of production workers, cost of raw materials, fuel, power.
  ◦ Total Cost (TC):
    ▪ Definition: The sum of total fixed cost and total variable cost at any given level of output.
    ▪ Formula: TC = TFC + TVC

2.3 Short-Run Cost Functions and Curves
• Analyzing cost behavior at different output levels reveals specific cost curves.
  ◦ Total Fixed Cost (TFC): Remains constant regardless of output. Represented by a horizontal line.
  ◦ Total Variable Cost (TVC): Starts at zero when output is zero. Initially rises at a decreasing rate (due to increasing returns from variable inputs), then rises at an increasing rate (due to diminishing returns).
  ◦ Total Cost (TC): The vertical sum of TFC and TVC. It has the same shape as TVC but is shifted upwards by the amount of TFC.

2.3.1 Average Cost Measures
• These are per-unit cost calculations.
  ◦ Average Fixed Cost (AFC):
    ▪ Definition: Total fixed cost divided by the quantity of output.
    ▪ Formula: AFC = TFC / Q
    ▪ Behavior: Declines continuously as output increases because the fixed cost is spread over more units.
  ◦ Average Variable Cost (AVC):
    ▪ Definition: Total variable cost divided by the quantity of output.
    ▪ Formula: AVC = TVC / Q
    ▪ Behavior: Typically U-shaped. It initially falls as output increases (due to increasing returns) and then rises as output further increases (due to diminishing returns).
  ◦ Average Total Cost (ATC):
    ▪ Definition: Total cost divided by the quantity of output.
    ▪ Formula: ATC = TC / Q
    ▪ Relationship: ATC = AFC + AVC
    ▪ Behavior: Also U-shaped. It is the sum of AFC and AVC. Its minimum point occurs at a higher output level than the minimum of AVC.

2.3.2 Marginal Cost (MC)
• Marginal Cost (MC):
  ◦ Definition: The additional cost incurred from producing one more unit of output. It represents the change in total cost (or total variable cost) resulting from a one-unit change in output.
  ◦ Formula: MC = ΔTC / ΔQ or MC = ΔTVC / ΔQ (since ΔTFC = 0 in the short run).
  ◦ Behavior: Typically U-shaped. It initially falls as output increases (due to increasing efficiency) and then rises sharply as output further increases (due to diminishing returns and increasing variable costs).
• Relationship between MC and AP/MP:
  ◦ MC is closely related to MP. MC falls when MP is rising, and MC rises when MP is falling.
  ◦ MC reaches its minimum at the output level where MP is at its maximum.
• Relationship between MC and ACs:
  ◦ MC intersects both AVC and ATC at their minimum points.
  ◦ When MC is below AVC or ATC, it pulls them down.
  ◦ When MC is above AVC or ATC, it pulls them up.
  ◦ When MC equals AVC or ATC, they are at their minimum.

Unit Summary
• Production is the process of transforming inputs into outputs.
• Inputs are resources like labor, capital, land, and entrepreneurial ability. Outputs are the goods and services produced.
• Fixed inputs cannot be varied in the short run, while variable inputs can be.
• The short run has at least one fixed input, while the long run has all variable inputs.
• Productivity is measured by Total Product (TP), Average Product (AP), and Marginal Product (MP).
• Production exhibits stages of increasing, diminishing, and negative returns. The rational firm operates in the stage of diminishing returns.
• Cost of production involves monetary outlays. Explicit costs are cash payments (accounting costs), while implicit costs are opportunity costs of self-owned resources (economic costs).
• Economic cost equals explicit plus implicit costs.
• Fixed costs (TFC) do not change with output, while variable costs (TVC) do.
• Total Cost (TC) is the sum of TFC and TVC.
• Average costs include AFC (TFC/Q), AVC (TVC/Q), and ATC (TC/Q). All are typically U-shaped except AFC, which continuously falls.
• Marginal Cost (MC) is the cost of producing one additional unit (ΔTC/ΔQ). MC is also typically U-shaped and intersects AVC and ATC at their minimum points.
