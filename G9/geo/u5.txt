Major Economic and Cultural Activities in Ethiopia

    This unit provides a comprehensive overview of the economic landscape of Ethiopia, exploring the classification of economic activities, the critical role of agriculture, the dynamics of trade and transport, road safety challenges, and the richness of Ethiopia's cultural landscapes and tourism.

1. Major Economic Activities in Ethiopia
1.1 Introduction to Economic Activity

Economic Activity: The production, distribution, and exchange of goods and services.

Examples of economic activities include:

    Hunting and fishing
    Farming and grazing
    Mining and manufacturing
    Transportation and trade

1.2 Classification of Economic Activities

Geographers group economic activities into five distinct sectors. The distribution of jobs across these sectors often indicates a country's level of development.
A. Primary Economic Activities

Primary Economic Activities: Activities that focus directly on the extraction of resources from the environment, producing foodstuffs and raw materials.

These activities occur at the beginning of the production cycle and are characterized by:

    Direct dependence on the natural environment.
    Exploitation of the earth's resources to produce raw materials.
    Heavy influence from the conditions of the physical environment.

Key Primary Activities:

    Agriculture: The science and art of soil cultivation and livestock rearing for local consumption or commercial purposes.
        Crop Production (Arable Farming): The cultivation of plants for food, feed, fiber, medicine, or industrial use.
            Seed (Grain) Production: Common in Weina Dega and Dega zones.
                Cereals: Teff, barley, wheat, maize, sorghum, millet, and oats.
                Pulses: Horse beans, chickpeas, haricot beans, field peas, lentils, and vetch.
                Oilseeds: Oilniger, linseed, fenugreek, rapeseed, sunflower, groundnuts, and sesame.
            Permanent (Perennial) Crop Cultivation: Found in WoinaDega areas of southern and southwestern Ethiopia (e.g., coffee, cotton, and enset). Enset is the staple food in these regions.
            Shifting Cultivation: A "cut-and-burn" system practiced in low population density areas like Benishangul-Gumuz, Gambella, and Southern Regions.
            Plantation Agriculture: Focuses on cash/industrial crops (sugarcane, cotton, coffee, tea, rubber) in areas with high rainfall or irrigation.
        Livestock Raising: Keeping animals for food, power, raw materials, or market. Ethiopia ranks 1st in Africa and 10th globally in cattle population.
    Forestry: The extraction of forest products for various purposes.
        Forests contribute approximately 6.10% to Ethiopia's GDP.
        Primary domestic uses include fuel wood, timber for furniture, and building/construction.
    Fishery: The catching and harvesting of fish and other marine creatures.
        In Ethiopia, this is primarily a freshwater activity taking place in rivers, lakes, and ponds.
    Mining: The extraction of mineral-bearing substances from the earth's crust.
        Mineral: An inorganic chemical element or compound found naturally in the earth's crust.
        Mining currently contributes 1.5% to Ethiopia's GDP.

B. Secondary Economic Activities

Secondary Economic Activities: Activities that turn raw materials into finished products using labor, energy, and equipment.

    Manufacturing: The actual process of turning raw materials into products in factories.
    Industry: The place where manufacturing takes place.

Types of Manufacturing in Ethiopia:

    Cottage (Traditional) Industries: Small-scale activities like weaving, wood carving, pottery, metal works, and basketry. These have faced slow development due to negative traditional outlooks and low social status associated with the work.
    Modern Manufacturing Industries: Currently at a low level in Ethiopia, consisting largely of light industries producing consumer goods.

Other Secondary Activities: Construction, power production, metalworking, smelting, automobile and textile production, and breweries.
C. Tertiary Economic Activities

Tertiary Economic Activities: The provision of services to other businesses and final consumers.

Examples include:

    Trade and transportation
    Tourism
    Medical and legal services

D. Quaternary Economic Activities

Quaternary Economic Activities: An advanced form of service activity requiring specialized knowledge, technical skills, or administrative competence.

These are primarily concentrated in urban areas and require higher education. Examples include:

    Research and financial services
    Government activities
    Education (classrooms) and healthcare (doctors' offices)
    The entertainment industry

E. Quinary Economic Activities

Quinary Economic Activities: A subset of quaternary activities involving high-level decision-making and top-tier scientific research.

These involve highly paid skills of:

    Top business executives
    Government officials
    Research scientists
    Financial and legal consultants

2. Contribution of Farming to the Ethiopian Economy

    Agriculture is the pillar of the Ethiopian national economy, serving as the primary source of income and employment.

2.1 General Importance of Agriculture

The dominance of agriculture is evidenced by the following statistics:

    Employment: Employs 80% of the total population.
    Exports: Supplies more than 90% of export commodities.
    GDP Contribution: Contributed 32.7% of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) as of 2019/20.

2.2 Subsistence Farming

Subsistence Farming: A method of farming where nearly all crops or livestock are used to maintain the farmer and their family, leaving little to no surplus for sale.

Characteristics:

    Common in developing countries; "hand-to-mouth" in nature.
    Uses traditional tools and techniques.
    Highly dependent on rainfall.
    Focuses on self-sufficiency and reducing vulnerability for food-insecure rural households.

2.3 Cash Crop Production (Commercial Farming)

Commercial Farming: Farming concerned with producing crops specifically for sale to make a profit.

Contributions to the Economy:

    Source of Food and Raw Materials: Provides food grains, dairy, and meat to consumers, and raw materials to agro-industries.
    Source of Capital:
        Agricultural Taxation: Taxes paid by commercial farmers contribute to the economy.
        Export Earnings: Major exports include coffee, oilseeds, flowers, chat, and pulses.
            Coffee: The most important export (28.6% of all export value in 2019/20).
            Other shares (2019/20): Oilseeds (11.5%), Chat (10.9%), Pulses (7.9%).
    Employment: Provides substantial wage and employment opportunities in rural areas.

2.4 Problems of Agriculture in Ethiopia

Despite its importance, Ethiopian agriculture faces several obstacles:

    Land Degradation: 50% of cultivable land is exposed to soil erosion, reducing fertility and water-holding capacity.
    Variable Rainfall: Heavy dependence on unreliable rainfall that is often untimely or inconsistent in amount.
    Fragmentation of Farm Plots: Due to inheritance, land is divided into smaller, fragmented plots.
        Stat: 38% of households access less than 0.5 hectares.
    Backward Technology: Dependence on traditional tools (oxen-drawn ploughs, manual broadcasting for planting, and sickles for harvesting).
    Poor Rural Infrastructure: Poor roads and lack of irrigation, electricity, and markets increase transport costs and reduce farmer income.

3. Trade and Transport in Ethiopia
3.1 Trade

Trade: The buying and selling of goods and services, involving compensation paid by a buyer to a seller, or the exchange of goods/services between parties.
Types of Trade

    Internal (Domestic) Trade: Exchange of goods and services within the country. This serves as the base for foreign trade.
    External (Foreign) Trade: Exchange of commodities between different countries.

The Structure of External Trade

    Export Trade: Dominated by agricultural raw materials. Coffee remains the leading export, followed by flowers (14.1%) and oilseeds (11.5%) as of 2019/20.
    Import Trade: Ethiopia mainly imports finished products, including machinery, transport equipment, electrical/electronic goods, and fuel.

Trade Balance

Trade Balance: The yearly difference between the value of a country's exports and its imports.

    Positive (Surplus): Exports > Imports.
    Negative (Deficit): Imports > Exports.
    Ethiopia's Status: Experiences a trade deficit because import values exceed export values.

3.2 Transportation

Transportation: The movement of materials, goods, or people from one place to another for a specified objective.
Modes of Transport

I. Traditional Modes:

    Pack animals
    Human porterage

II. Modern Modes:

    Road Transport: The most widespread and flexible mode; critical for accessing rural and urban areas.
    Railway Transport: Used for transporting bulky products.
        Addis Ababa-Djibouti Railway: A new standard gauge international railway (752.7 km) providing sea access via the Port of Doraleh. Over 95% of Ethiopia's trade passes through Djibouti.
        Addis Ababa Light Rail Transit (AA-LRT): The first light rail in Sub-Saharan Africa, reducing traffic congestion in the capital.
    Inland Waterways: Limited due to rugged topography and seasonal rainfall.
        Navigable River: Baro.
        Other use: Small-scale transport on lakes Tana, Abbaya, and Ziway.
    Air Transport: Crucial due to difficult topography.
        Ethiopian Airlines (EAL): Established in 1945; leading and most profitable airline in Africa.
        Impact: Contributes significantly to GDP through the airline industry and foreign tourism.

4. Road Safety in Ethiopia

Road Safety: Safety in using roads, involving rules (speed limits, anti-drunk driving) and control systems (traffic lights, crossings) to protect motorists, cyclists, and pedestrians.
4.1 Accident Severity Definitions

    Fatal Accident: One or more individuals die within 30 days of the accident.
    Serious Injury: Victim requires hospital admission as an "in-patient" for severe cuts or breaks.
    Slight Injury: Victim is treated as an "out-patient" for small cuts or scratches.
    Property Damage Only: No persons are injured, but one or more vehicles are damaged.

4.2 Road Traffic Accidents in Ethiopia

    Trends: Deaths more than doubled between 2007 (2,161) and 2018 (4,597).
    Frequency: Approximately 13 people die per day in road crashes.
    Geography: Fatalities are concentrated in central regions; Oromia, Amhara, SNNPR, and Addis Ababa account for nearly 85% of fatalities.
    Management: The National Road Safety Council (NRSC) oversees road safety, comprising various ministries (Transport, Finance, Education, Health), the Federal Police, and the Ethiopian Roads Authority.

4.3 Causes and Factors of Accidents

General Influencing Factors:

    Vehicle-related
    Road-related
    Road user-related
    Environmental-related

Specific Causes:

    Driver Distraction: Using technical devices, eating, grooming, or talking to passengers.
    Driver Impairment: Illness, alcohol, drugs, and sleepiness (causes more than half of all crashes).
    Mechanical Failure: Brake failure, flat tires, or steering mechanism failure.
    Road Conditions: Potholes or slick surfaces due to foreign substances.
    Speeding: Exceeding limits designed for the road, weather, or surrounding traffic.

4.4 Consequences and Safety Rules

Consequences:

    Loss of productivity for victims.
    Costs associated with the legal system.
    Physical pain, suffering, and loss of quality of life for victims and families.

Safety Rules:
For Pedestrians 	For Drivers
Use sidewalks when available 	Never drink and drive
Walk facing traffic if no sidewalk exists 	Always wear a seat belt
Obey all signs and signals 	Keep a safe distance from the vehicle ahead
Cross only at designated areas 	Avoid distractions and drowsiness
Wear bright colors (day) or reflective tape (night) 	Drive within the speed limit
5. Cultural Landscapes and Tourism in Ethiopia
5.1 Cultural Landscapes

Cultural Landscapes: Geographic areas (including natural and cultural resources) associated with historic events, activities, or persons, representing the "combined works of nature and man."

Example: Konso Cultural Landscape

    Location: Southern Ethiopia, edge of the Rift Valley.
    Key Features:
        Dry stone terrace agriculture (up to 5 meters high) to prevent soil erosion in rocky, barren environments.
        Walled town settlements.
        Indigenous adaptation practiced for approximately 400 years.
    Recognition: Officially recognized by UNESCO in 2011.

5.2 Tourism in Ethiopia

Tourism: A collection of activities and services (transport, accommodation, retail, entertainment) that deliver a travel experience. It is often called a "smokeless industry."

Tourist Attraction Sites:

    Natural Features:
        National Parks: Simien Mountains, Bale Mountains, Awash, NechSar, Omo, Mago, and Gambella.
        Water/Geological: Blue Nile Falls (Tis isat), Lake Tana, Sof Omar Cave, Abijata-Shalla National Park, and the Rift Valley.
    Human-made Features:
        Lalibela, Axum, Harar, Gonder Castle, and Tiya.
