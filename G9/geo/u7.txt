Unit Seven: Contemporary Geographic Issues and Public Concerns in Ethiopia

    This unit explores significant geographic issues in Ethiopia, focusing on the degradation of natural resources such as soil, vegetation, and water. It also examines the nation's proactive "Green Legacy" movement aimed at addressing these challenges and promoting sustainable development.

7.1 Natural Resource Degradation

This section describes the causes, effects, and conservation measures for the degradation of Ethiopia's natural resources.
7.1.1 Soil Degradation

    Definition: Soil degradation is a change in the state of soil caused by increased erosion, leaching, or both.
    Primary Problem in Ethiopia: Erosion by running water is identified as the most serious problem for soil resources in Ethiopia.

Causes of Soil Erosion

Soil erosion is a natural process, but human activities often accelerate it. Factors are divided into natural and human-made.

I. Natural Factors: * Topography: Steepness of the slope. * Rainfall Characteristics: Intensity, duration, and seasonality of rainfall. * Soil Type: Including soil texture. * Torrent Rivers: Particularly during the summer season. * Vegetation Cover: The presence or absence of vegetation influences erosion rates.

II. Human-made Factors: * Deforestation: * Consequences of Forests: Forests in Ethiopia's highlands historically provided habitats, livelihoods, watershed protection, soil erosion prevention, and climate change mitigation. * Current Situation: Deforestation is occurring at a rapid rate, significantly contributing to Ethiopia's accelerated erosion. * Overgrazing: * Definition: Keeping too many livestock in a small area. * Mechanism: Animals consume vegetation faster than it can regenerate, leaving the land bare and vulnerable to erosion. * Specific Impact: Goats are particularly damaging as they consume all types of vegetation, including roots. * Bad Cultivation Practices: These practices make soil more susceptible to natural erosive forces. * Overcropping: * Definition: Planting an area too densely. * Effect: Depletes soil nutrients faster than they can be replenished, reducing land fertility. * Overcultivation: * Definition: Tilling land every year, often driven by increasing food demands. * Effect: Frequent tilling removes nutrients faster than natural replenishment. * Slash and Burn: * Definition: Cutting down and burning vegetation to clear land. * Use in Farming: Sometimes used periodically to prepare land for cultivation or to allow it to lie fallow and regain nutrients. * Effect: Leaves the soil bare and unprotected, making it highly vulnerable to erosion. It is also used to clear forestland for farming, exposing the soil.
Impact of Soil Erosion in Ethiopia

    Quantity of Topsoil Loss: Approximately 1.9 billion tons of topsoil are washed away from the highlands annually.
    River Abbay's Contribution: Carries away 3000-4000 million cubic meters of soil annually.
    Economic Cost: The loss of topsoil costs billions of Ethiopian birr per year.
    Irreplaceability: Lost topsoil is essentially irreplaceable due to slow natural production rates.

Conservation Measures to Combat Soil Erosion

    Reforestation: Planting trees on land that was previously forested but has been cleared.
    Afforestation: Planting trees on land not previously covered by forests to establish new forests.
    Terracing: Building level surfaces at right angles to the slope to retain water and reduce erosion.
    Strip Cultivation: Planting different crops in alternating strips to help retain water and soil.
    Controlling Livestock Population: Managing the number of livestock to prevent overgrazing.
    Intercropping: Growing two or more crops in the same field simultaneously to keep the land covered.
    Contour Ploughing: Ploughing across slopes, rather than up and down, to create barriers against water runoff.
    Developing Improved Grass Types: Creating grass varieties that can support more livestock without excessive degradation.
    Proper Construction of Tracks and Routes: Designing roads and paths to prevent drainage from washing them away.
    Constructing Check Dams: Building small dams to slow down water flow and trap sediment.
    Installing Shelter-belts and Windbreaks: Planting rows of trees and shrubs in arid and semi-arid areas to protect against wind erosion.

    Key Takeaway: Soil is a renewable resource, but its conservation requires appropriate and sometimes expensive measures. Conservation means using resources wisely for long-term societal benefit.

7.1.2 Vegetation Degradation (Deforestation)

The most significant evidence of vegetation degradation in Ethiopia is growing deforestation.
Major Causes of Deforestation

    Demand for Fuelwood: The need for firewood for heating and cooking.
    Demand for Construction Material: Timber and other forest products for building.
    Expansion of Agricultural Lands: Clearing forests to create more land for farming.
    Slash-and-Burn Practices: Used for clearing land for agriculture.
    Overgrazing: Converting forest land into pasture.
    Forest Fires: Both natural and human-induced fires.
    Expansion of Settlements: Clearing forests for housing, industries, and recreational areas due to a growing population.

Mitigation Measures to Combat Deforestation

The following approaches are suggested for slowing down and mitigating deforestation in Ethiopia. These approaches alone are not sufficient and require sustained effort and additional ideas.

I. Conservation Measures: * Reforestation: Planting trees after harvesting or to replace removed trees. * Afforestation: Planting trees on bare and unproductive lands. * Controlling Burning Practices: Regulating slash-and-burn activities. * Practicing Agro-forestry: Integrating trees with crops and livestock. * Using Alternative Energy Sources: Shifting to non-wood-based energy for household consumption. * Using Alternative Construction Materials: Finding substitutes for wood in building and furniture. * Controlling Overgrazing: Managing livestock numbers and grazing practices. * Controlling Population Growth Rate: Addressing the underlying demographic pressure.

II. Capacity Building and Institutional Development: * Environmental Education: Enhancing public awareness about natural vegetation use and management. * Developing Forest Curricula: Creating and implementing educational programs in schools and institutions. * Supporting Community Forests: Protecting community-managed forests with legal measures. * Moderating Population Growth: Implementing strategies to slow down rapid population increase. * Facilitating Community Participation: Encouraging active involvement of communities in combating deforestation. * Changing Lifestyles: Promoting behavioral changes related to deforestation activities. * Vigorous Law Enforcement: Ensuring that anti-deforestation rules and laws are actively enforced by relevant agencies.

    Key Takeaway: Combating deforestation requires a multi-pronged approach involving direct conservation actions, educational initiatives, and institutional strengthening, alongside community participation and strong legal frameworks.

7.1.3 Water Resource Issues

Ethiopia is known as the "Water Tower of East Africa" and is rich in water resources, second only to the Democratic Republic of Congo in Africa. However, it faces significant challenges.
Challenges Facing Water Resources

    Recurring Droughts: Droughts occur every 3 to 5 years.
    Disappearing Lakes: Some rift valley lakes, like Lake Abijata and Lake Cheleklektu, are disappearing or on the verge of disappearing.
    Decreasing River Volume and Purity: The volume and purity of Ethiopia's rivers are declining.
        Pollutants: Sewage, organic wastes, and fertilizers are contaminating water resources.
    Growing Demand for Potable Water: Rapid population growth leads to a critical and increasing demand for clean drinking water.

National Strategy for Water Resource Conservation and Management

The Ethiopian government has adopted a national strategy to address these issues. Key components include:

    Integrating Ecosystem Protection: Major conservation, development, and management projects must include the costs and benefits of protecting watershed forests, wetlands, and other key ecosystems.
    Recognizing Ecosystem Importance: Natural ecosystems, especially wetlands and upstream forests, are crucial for regulating water quality and quantity. Their rehabilitation and protection should be integrated into water resource projects.
    Environmental Health Hazards: Controlling environmental health hazards is a necessary condition in the design, construction, and use of dams and irrigation systems.
    Introducing Exotic Species: Any proposed introduction of non-native species into water ecosystems must undergo detailed studies and environmental impact assessments.
    Protecting Water-Land Interfaces: Promoting the protection of areas where water bodies meet land (e.g., lake shores, river banks, wetlands).
    Involving Water Users: Engaging water-resource users, especially women and animal herders, in local planning, design, and follow-up of water policies and projects to ensure ecological balance.
    Recycling Wastewater: Reusing wastewater when it is deemed safe for health and the environment.
    Artificial Recharge: Promoting viable measures to artificially replenish ground and surface water resources.
    Farm-Level Water Management: Promoting effective water management techniques at the farm level for better performance of irrigation schemes.
    Private Sector Support: Providing technical and credit support to the private sector for water resource development.

    Key Takeaway: Despite its vast water potential, Ethiopia faces significant water resource degradation due to drought, pollution, and increasing demand. A national strategy is in place to promote conservation, management, and sustainable use of these vital resources.

7.2 The Ethiopian "Green Legacy" Movement

This section describes the concept and effects of Ethiopia's "Green Legacy" movement.

    Definition: The "Green Legacy" Initiative is a tree-planting campaign launched in 2019 by Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed.
    Primary Goal: To curb the effects of climate change and deforestation.

Components of the Movement

The Green Legacy movement integrates several key areas:

    Agroforestry: Combining trees with agricultural practices.
    Forest Sector Development: Enhancing the management and growth of forests.
    Greening and Renewal of Urban Areas: Increasing vegetation and green spaces in cities.
    Integrated Water and Soil Resources Management: Coordinated efforts to manage water and soil resources.

Ambitious Planting Goals

    Target: To plant 20 billion seedlings across the country over four years (2020-2023).
    Post-Planting Care: Emphasis on watering, weeding, and replacement planting to ensure seedling survival.

Contribution to Development and International Commitments

The initiative aims to contribute to:

    Long-term Social, Economic, and Environmental Development Goals of Ethiopia.
    International Commitments:
        Paris Climate Change Agreement.
        2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (UN).
        African Agenda 2063.

Guiding Strategic Documents

Efforts are guided by strategic documents like:

    Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) Strategy.
    Ten-Year National Forest Sector Development Program.
    Bamboo Development Strategy and Action Plan.

These strategies align with global goals and provide direction for afforestation, restoration, urban greening, forest protection, and sustainable forest resource use.
Overall "Green Legacy" Movement Goals

    Curb the effects of climate change and deforestation.
    Intensify climate-smart agriculture to increase productivity.
    Improve food security.
    Prevent environment-related conflicts.
    Battle desertification and soil erosion.
    Build a climate-resilient green economy.
    Ensure gender equality.

Unit Summary

    Ethiopia possesses abundant natural resources but faces degradation due to population pressure, agricultural expansion, urbanization, climate change, and pollution.
    Soil erosion by running water is the main soil problem, driven by both natural and human factors. Conservation efforts include reforestation, afforestation, terracing, and contour ploughing.
    Long-term human activities and population pressure have led to heavy deforestation, drastically reducing forest cover from 40% to less than 3% historically. However, initiatives like the "Green Legacy" movement have helped increase forest coverage.
    The Ethiopian government is implementing various strategies, including soil and water conservation campaigns, tree planting programs, and developing water resource management policies. Public awareness is being raised through educational systems and extension services.
