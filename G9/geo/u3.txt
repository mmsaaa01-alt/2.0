UNIT THREE: NATURAL RESOURCE BASE OF ETHIOPIA

• This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of Ethiopia's natural resources, including their meaning, classification, distribution, characteristics, and significance. It covers drainage systems, water resources (rivers and lakes), major soil types, mineral resources, and biotic resources (vegetation and wildlife).

3.1 Meaning of Natural Resources

• This section introduces the concept of natural resources and classifies them into renewable and non-renewable types, highlighting their significance and the factors influencing their sustainability in Ethiopia.

  • Natural Resources: Natural assets occurring in nature that can be used for economic production or consumption.

3.1.1 Classification of Natural Resources

Natural resources are broadly classified into two main categories:

  • Renewable Resources:
    ◦ Definition: Resources which can be replenished by nature over a relatively short period.
    ◦ Examples: Plants, animals, soil, water, geothermal energy, wind energy, solar radiation.
  • Non-renewable Resources:
    ◦ Definition: Resources that cannot be regenerated by nature or regenerate over extremely long geological timescales, and exist in limited amounts.
    ◦ Examples: All minerals, coal, crude oil, natural gas.

3.1.2 Factors Influencing Natural Resources in Ethiopia

The natural resources in Ethiopia are affected by various interconnected factors, leading to their degradation:

  • Population pressure: A huge population places a significant burden on the sustainability of almost all types of natural resources.
  • Agricultural expansion: Leads to conversion of natural habitats and increased demand on resources.
  • Migration and rapid urbanization: Concentrates population and resource demand in specific areas.
  • Resettlement: Can disrupt existing ecological balances.
  • Climate change: Alters environmental conditions and resource availability.
  • Environmental pollution: Degrades the quality of natural resources.

• These factors contribute to serious degradation of land, water, forest, rangeland, and wildlife resources, often in a cycle where they feed off each other.

3.2 Drainage Systems of Ethiopia

• This section elaborates on the major drainage systems of Ethiopia, describing their flow direction, destination, and the natural factors that influence them.

  • Drainage Systems: Refer to the flow direction and destination of rivers.

• The drainage patterns and systems of Ethiopia are primarily the results of various structural events that took place in the Cenozoic era.

• The drainage systems of Ethiopia are classified into three major groups:

3.2.1 The Western (Mediterranean Sea) Drainage System

  • Characteristics:
    ◦ Largest of the three systems in terms of catchment area and volume of water.
    ◦ Contributes 60% of the country's total annual water discharge.
  • Major Rivers and Tributaries:
    ◦ River Tekezze: Drains the Massif of western Lasta, northern Gondar/Semein, and southwestern, western, and central Tigray.
    ◦ River Abbay: Originates in the Gojjam plateau. Joined by streams from western Shewa, southwestern Wollo, northern Wollega, and northern Illubabor. It has a semicircular course from Lake Tana.
    ◦ River Baro-Akobo: Drains the wettest highlands of the southwest.
  • Destination: These rivers join the Nile in Sudan and eventually flow into the Mediterranean Sea.

3.2.2 The Southeastern (Indian Ocean) Drainage System

  • Characteristics:
    ◦ Second largest drainage system.
    ◦ Flows southeast, across the Somali arid and semi-arid areas.
    ◦ Contributes about 32% of the country's total annual water flow.
  • Major Rivers and Tributaries:
    ◦ Wabe Shebelle:
      ▪ The longest river in the country.
      ▪ Collects waters from the highlands of Hararghe, Sidamo, Bale, and Arsi.
      ▪ Does not reach the Indian Ocean; ends at the Benadir coast of Somalia.
    ◦ Genale:
      ▪ Collects waters from the highlands of Hararghe, Sidamo, Bale, and Arsi.
      ▪ Reaches the Indian Ocean.
      ▪ Joins the Dawa River at the Ethio-Somalia border, where it acquires the name Juba.

3.2.3 The Inland (Rift Valley) Drainage System

  • Characteristics:
    ◦ Smallest of the three systems in terms of catchment area, discharge, and volume of water.
    ◦ Comprises rivers and streams that flow into lakes within the Rift Valley, and two major rivers that terminate internally.
  • Lakes and Smaller Streams:
    ◦ Bilate and Gedabo: Flow into Lake Abbaya.
    ◦ Segan: Flows into Chew Bahir.
    ◦ Meki and Katar: Flow into Lake Ziway.
  • Major Rivers:
    ◦ Awash River:
      ▪ Most utilized basin in the Rift Valley.
      ▪ Covers a total area of 110,000 km².
      ▪ Rises from the Shewan plateau near Ginchi town.
      ▪ Flows along the Rift Valley.
      ▪ Terminates in the salty Lake Abbe on the border with Djibouti.
      ▪ The middle and lower courses are part of the Great Rift Valley system (the upper course is not).
      ▪ Lower Awash River basin includes deltaic alluvial plains of Tendaho, Assaita, Dit Behri areas, and terminal lakes area.
    ◦ Omo-Gibe River.

3.3 Water Resources of Ethiopia

• This section describes Ethiopia's rich water resources, including its major rivers and lakes, their characteristics, and their significant roles in the country's socio-economic development.

• Ethiopia is known as the "Water Tower of Northeastern Africa" and is the second richest African country in water resource potential, after the Democratic Republic of Congo. This is due to adequate average annual rainfall in most areas, resulting in several major rivers, lakes, and significant groundwater potential.

3.3.2 Characteristics of Ethiopian Rivers

• The most important characteristics of Ethiopian rivers include:

  • Seasonal fluctuation in water volume: Their flow changes significantly between wet and dry seasons.
  • Steep profiles: They arise from very high places and flow across lowlands to the country's borders, resulting in rapid descent.
  • Rapids and waterfalls along their courses: Due to the steep terrain.
  • Running through steep-sided river valleys and gorges: Carved out by their energetic flow.
  • Serve as boundaries: Both international and domestic borders.

• Note: Some Ethiopian rivers change names after crossing the country's borders:

  • River Abbay becomes Blue Nile in Sudan.
  • River Tekezze becomes River Athbara in Sudan.
  • River Genale becomes River Juba in Somalia.
  • River Baro becomes River Sobat in Sudan.

3.3.3 Lakes of Ethiopia

• Ethiopia is rich in lakes, which are found dispersed on the plateaus and clustered in the Rift Valley. Most of these lakes formed during the Quaternary Period due to structural events, rather than climate.

Highland Lakes

  • Location: Situated on the plateaus.
  • Formation: Resulted from different types of structural formations.
    ◦ Crater Lake: Formed after an explosive volcano breaks a mountain open, leaving a deep mouth that fills with water from streams or subterranean sources.
      ▪ Examples: Haik (near Dessie), Hashenge (near Korum), Bishoftu, Kuriftu, Babbo Gaya, Arsedi (in and around Bishoftu), Wonchi, Dendi (around Ambo, Ginchi, Wellisso), Ziquala.
    ◦ Watershed Lake: Formed when a sheet of lava dams up a shallow surface depression.
      ▪ Example: Lake Tana, formed when flowing lava dammed the depression between the Gojjam and Gondar Massif during the Quaternary Period.

Rift Valley Lakes

  • Location: Clustered and found in a linear pattern along the floor of the Rift Valley.
  • Formation: Formed in the depressions and basins caused by tectonic activities.

3.3.4 Significance of Ethiopian Lakes and Rivers

• Ethiopian rivers and lakes are vital natural resources for the country's socio-economic and aesthetic development:

  • Hydroelectric Power (H.E.P.) Generation: Rivers are the main source of H.E.P.
    ◦ Examples: River Gibe (Gilgel Gibbe - 1, 2, and 3 H.E.P. plants), River Awash (Awash 1, 2, and 3 H.E.P. project), River Fincha (Fincha H.E.P. project), Tekezze (Tekezze H.E.P plant), Melka Wakena (Wabe Shebelle H.E.P), Koysha (under construction), Great Ethiopian Renaissance Dam H.E.P plant (under construction).
  • Fishery: Main source of fish.
    ◦ Examples: Lake Chamo, Lake Abbaya, Lake Tana, River Baro.
  • Major Irrigation Schemes: Important water sources for irrigation.
    ◦ Example: River Awash is most utilized due to its flat plains.
  • Navigation: Used for water transport.
    ◦ Examples: River Baro (only river in Ethiopia for transport), Lake Tana, Lake Ziway, Lake Abbaya (important inland waterways).
  • Tourism and Recreation: Provide recreation resorts and aesthetic value.
    ◦ Their scenic beauty comes from a rich variety of birds, fish, wildlife, deep gorges, waterfalls, and mists. These features attract both Ethiopians and tourists. The economic potential is enormous but not yet fully developed.

3.4 Major Soils Types of Ethiopia

• This section examines the formation and various types of soils found in Ethiopia, along with their characteristics and agricultural suitability.

  • Soil: The loose material overlying the crust of the earth consisting mainly of very small particles.
  • Major Components of Soil: Water, air, organic minerals, and inorganic minerals. Soil is a dynamic, natural, and complex substance that supports animal and plant life.

3.4.1 Formation of Soils in Ethiopia

• Soils in Ethiopia are formed as a result of several interacting factors:

  • Parent Rock Material: Broken into small particles through weathering and natural decomposition.
  • Climatic Conditions: Largely determine the speed and nature of soil formation processes (e.g., extreme heat/cold affecting bacterial activity, moisture influencing soil aspects).
  • Vegetation Cover: Adds humus (organic matter) to the soils and supports soil-making animals and bacteria.
  • Topography: Soils on hillsides tend to be shallow due to erosional losses.

• Specific Soil Formation Examples in Ethiopia:

  • Plateaus (North, Northwest, Southwest, Central, Southeast): Where parent rocks are of volcanic origin and experience sufficient rainfall, red basaltic and black basaltic soils are formed. Black soils have high clay content, making farming difficult.
  • Hararghe Plateaus and Borena Lowlands: Where hard crystalline rocks are the parent material, soils are poor, thin, rocky, acidic, and range from grey to brown.
  • Afar Region: Due to recent volcanic deposits and dried-out lake deposits, very shallow and saline-dominated soils are formed. These are not deep or fertile due to time and climatic effects.
  • Southeastern Lowlands: Where sedimentary rocks are the parent rocks, soils lack humus but are rich in phosphorus and potash, though low in nitrogen content.
  • Lower Course of Major Rivers: Transported soils (alluvial soils) are dominant. These are fertile due to their volcanic origin and continuous nourishment from water.

• Soils are a fundamental basis of life, and their quality depends on wise land use, particularly in agrarian economies like Ethiopia.

3.4.2 Types of Soil in Ethiopia

• Ethiopian soils are primarily derived from volcanic, metamorphic, and sedimentary rocks. According to FAO, there are eighteen classes of soil in Ethiopia, but the following cover more than 85% of the country:

  • Nithosols (Red Basaltic Soils):
    ◦ Cover about 12% of the country.
    ◦ Associated with high rainfall and found in areas previously covered with forest.
    ◦ Predominant in Western Highlands (Wollega, Kafa, Illubabor), Southern Highlands (Sidama), Central and Western Highlands (Shewa), Highlands of Gojjam, and Eastern Highlands (Hararghe).
    ◦ Matured soils with deep profiles, highly leached, lack soluble minerals (Sodium, Calcium), but rich in iron and aluminum.
    ◦ Potentially good for farming; friable and have a stable structure.
    ◦ Most widely cultivated soil type, best for coffee, inset, and cereals.
  • Vertisols (Black Basaltic Soils):
    ◦ Cover about 10% of total land.
    ◦ High clay content, making them sticky and difficult for farming due to poor drainage.
    ◦ Have excellent nutrients that could support agriculture, but drainage issues limit their use, often to grazing.
    ◦ Largely found in Arsi, Bale, and central Hararghe, areas with pronounced wet and dry seasons.
  • Cambisoils:
    ◦ Developed from recent lava deposits of the Quaternary Period.
    ◦ Young and shallow.
    ◦ Found on the rugged and sloping terrain of the Plateau of Shewa (eastern escarpment) and Chercher Highlands.
  • Regosols:
    ◦ Like Cambisols, they are shallow and young, but with a coarse texture.
    ◦ Low agricultural value.
    ◦ Found in the Danakil and Ogaden plains.
  • Xerosols:
    ◦ Generally young and shallow, found in arid and semi-arid regions with a weakly developed profile.
    ◦ Extensively found in the Northeastern escarpment, Northwestern and Southeastern Lowlands.
    ◦ Characterized by high salt content and humus deficiencies.
    ◦ Little significance for agriculture except where they can be irrigated.
  • Luvisols:
    ◦ Well-developed in areas with clearly marked wet and dry seasons and low leaching.
    ◦ Among the best soils due to good chemical nutrients.
    ◦ Intensively cultivated, except in steeply sloped or water-logged areas.
    ◦ Found around Lake Tana, eastern Northern Central Highlands, and Southern Lowlands.
  • Lithosols:
    ◦ Similar to Cambisols and Regosols in their poor maturity and location on steep slopes.
    ◦ Found in areas of low precipitation.
    ◦ Cover the escarpments of the Northeastern and Chercher Highlands.
  • Fluvisols:
    ◦ Transported soils carried by rivers from highlands to lowlands.
    ◦ Cover about 10% of the country's total area.
    ◦ Associated with river, sea, and lake deposits.
    ◦ Have very good agricultural potential.
    ◦ Found extensively in the lower regions of the Omo, Awash, Abbay, and Baro-Akobo Rivers.

3.5 Major Mineral Resources and their Distribution in Ethiopia

• This section details the major metallic and non-metallic mineral resources identified in Ethiopia and their geographical distribution.

• Preliminary geological surveys indicate that Ethiopia has many different types of untapped mineral deposits. Currently, none are extracted on a large scale for commercial or industrial use. Non-metallic minerals are produced annually more than metallic ones, though most productions (except common salt) are small scale. Some amounts of these minerals are exported.

3.5.1 Metallic Minerals

  • Gold: 
    ◦ Extracted in Ethiopia since ancient times from alluvial deposits. 
    ◦ Geological Environments: Volcanic veins in Precambrian rocks and alluvial deposits. 
    ◦ Largest Active Mines: Adola, Bule Hora, Arero, Moyale, Akobo, Lega Dembi, Sakaro, and various Tigray regions.
  • Platinum: 
    ◦ Key input for manufacturing electronic equipment, including mobile phones. 
    ◦ Deposits Identified In: Western parts of Ethiopia (northeast Yubdo, north of Gimbi, and Akobo area of Gambella region).
  • Tantalum: 
    ◦ Used for manufacturing electronic equipment, including mobile phones. 
    ◦ Identified Rich Area: Kenticha, 50 km southeast of Shakiso in Adola. 
    ◦ Other tantalum-bearing minerals have been found in Adola, but not yet explored in detail.

3.5.2 Non-Metallic Minerals

  • Potash and Salt: 
    ◦ Location: Danakil depression (Dallol) area of the Northern Rift Valley, known for rich deposits.
  • Soda Ash: 
    ◦ Reserves: Geological surveys indicate some Rift Valley lakes (Abijata, Shalla, etc.) contain 460 million metric tons of Sodium Carbonate (Na₂CO₃). 
    ◦ Potential: High salt concentration and favorable climate make this area potentially one of the best locations for soda ash production.
  • Limestone: 
    ◦ Importance: Important mineral resource for building and construction. 
    ◦ Uses: Production of cement and chalk. 
    ◦ Good Reserves Found In: Tigray, Shewa, and Harerghe.
  • Clay: 
    ◦ Distribution: Found in many parts of Ethiopia. 
    ◦ Uses: Pottery and brick industries.
  • Silica: 
    ◦ Importance: Important raw material for glassy industries. 
    ◦ Reserves Found In: Harer, Shewa, Gondar, Gojjam, Sidamo, Arsi, Tigray, and Wollega.
  • Crude Oil and Natural Gas: 
    ◦ Reserves Believed To Be Found In: The Ogaden basin.
  • Coal: 
    ◦ Lignite Coal Deposits Proved To Exist In: Shewa (Debre Brihan-Dessie road, Sululta, and near Mojo), Sidama, Wollega.

3.6 Biotic Resources of Ethiopia

• This section describes Ethiopia's biotic resources, covering the major types of natural vegetation influenced by altitude and climate, and the diverse wildlife, including endemic species.

  • Biotic Resources: Living organisms in an ecosystem.
  • Main Categories:
    ◦ Producers: Include all green plants.
    ◦ Consumers: Include all animals.
    ◦ Decomposers: Include bacteria and fungi.

3.6.1 Major Types of Natural Vegetation of Ethiopia

  • Natural Vegetation: Any original plant grown in and covering an area.
  • Influencing Factors: The distribution of natural vegetation is influenced by:
    ◦ Altitude
    ◦ Climate
    ◦ Soil type
    ◦ Drainage

• In Ethiopia, the types of natural vegetation are highly correlated with altitude and rainfall, and also with temperature. Lowlands have harsh environments with low rainfall, characterized by xerophytic plants, while highlands feature various tree stands and forests.

• The natural vegetation of Ethiopia is classified into four major types, largely based on altitude and climate:

A. Afroalpine and Sub-Afroalpine

  • Description: Often known as high mountain vegetation, similar to European Alpine vegetation.
  • Altitude:
    ◦ Afroalpine: Above 3300 m.a.m.s.l.
    ◦ Sub-Afroalpine: Between 3000 - 3300 m.a.m.s.l.
  • Locations in Ethiopia: Highlands of Semein and Highlands of Bale.
  • Vegetation:
    ◦ Afroalpine: Consists of tussock grasslands, serules, scattered mosses, and lichens.
    ◦ Sub-Afroalpine: Dominated by woodland scrubs.
  • Dominant Plant Species: Gibra (Lobelia rhynchopetalum) and Asta (Erica arborea).

B. Forests

  • Altitude Range: 450 - 3300 m.a.m.s.l.
  • Annual Rainfall: 200 - 2200 mm.
  • This wide variation results in highland and lowland forests with very different characteristics due to altitudinal zonation.

i. Highland Forests

  • Altitude: Grow between 1500 - 3300 m.a.m.s.l.
  • Consist of:
    ◦ Kerkha (Arundinarial): At an altitude of 2800 - 3000 m.a.m.s.l.
    ◦ Tid (Juniperous Procera) or Coniferous trees: At an altitude of 2200 - 2800 m.a.m.s.l.
    ◦ Zigba (Podocarpus): At an altitude of 1800 - 2200 m.a.m.s.l.
    ◦ Woira (Oliia Africana) and Kosso (Hagenia Abbyssinia): At an altitude of 1500 - 1800 m.a.m.s.l.

ii. Lowland Forests

  • Altitude: Grow below 1500 m.a.m.s.l.
  • Description: Also known as gallery/riverine forests.
  • Locations: Grow along the banks of rivers like Awash, Wabe Shebelle, and Genale, where moisture is available in the soil.
  • Predominant Trees: Sholla and Warka.
  • Specific Type: Baphia forest predominates in areas where mean annual rainfall exceeds 500 mm.

C. Savanna Woodland

  • Altitude Range: 250-2300 m.a.m.s.l.
  • Annual Rainfall: 200-1400 mm.
  • Description: Grass mixed with scattered trees, shrubs, and bush lands.
  • Types:
    ◦ Juniperious woodlands - mountain grasslands with Tid trees.
    ◦ Accacia woodlands - grasslands with Ghirar trees.
    ◦ Mixed deciduous woodlands - grasslands with trees like Sholla and Warka.
  • Savanna Grasslands Characteristics: Experience marked seasons and are characterized by scattered acacia trees. Dominant at lower elevations and drier climates than forests.
  • Park-like Areas: Where mean annual rainfall is more than 1000 mm, these grasslands can form attractive park-like areas with acacia, wild fig, sycamore, and kosso trees.
  • Locations: Southern half of Ziway, Langano, Abiyatta, and Hawassa.

D. Semi-desert and Desert Vegetation

  • Dominant Vegetation: Xerophytic (drought-resisting plants).
  • Characteristics: Short acacia, thorn bushes, succulent plants, and a few rough grasses.
  • Locations in Ethiopia: Eastern (Afar lowlands), Northwestern, and Southeastern Lowlands (Ogaden lowlands).
  • Conditions: Areas where annual rainfall is below 600 mm and drought persists for long periods.

3.6.2 Main Kinds of Wildlife of Ethiopia

• Ethiopia's vast environmental diversity (relief, climate, natural vegetation) contributes to its rich wildlife.

  • Species Diversity:
    ◦ About 277 species of mammals.
    ◦ 862 species of birds.
    ◦ 201 reptile species.
    ◦ 63 amphibian species.
  • Endemic Species:
    ◦ 7 species of mammals are endemic to Ethiopia.
    ◦ 25 species of birds are endemic to Ethiopia.
  • Concentration: Wild animals are found in many parts of the country, largely concentrated in the south and western parts.

• The wild animals of Ethiopia are grouped into six broad categories:

  • Common Wild Animals:
    ◦ Found in many places across Ethiopia.
    ◦ Examples: Hyenas, jackals.
  • Game Animals:
    ◦ Mostly inhabit the grasslands found in the lowlands.
    ◦ Include both herbivores and carnivores.
    ◦ Herbivores: Browsers (e.g., giraffes), grazers (e.g., wild asses, zebras).
    ◦ Carnivores: Lions, leopards, cheetahs.
  • Arboreals (Tree Animals):
    ◦ Animals that climb trees.
    ◦ Mostly found in the rainforest regions of Ethiopia.
    ◦ Examples: Colobus monkey, apes, baboons.
  • Aquatic Animals:
    ◦ Animals that live in lakes and rivers.
    ◦ Examples: Hippopotamus, fish, crocodiles.
  • Birds:
    ◦ Ethiopia has different kinds of both endemic and migratory birds.
    ◦ Examples: Fish eagles, pelicans, geese, Abyssinian Ground Hornbill, flamingoes.
    ◦ Most are found in Rift Valley sanctuaries and forest lands.
  • Endemic Animals:
    ◦ Wild animals found only in Ethiopia.
    ◦ Exist in very small numbers and are at great risk of extinction.
    ◦ Inhabit highland and other areas.
    ◦ Examples:
      ▪ Walia Ibex (wild goat): Found in the Semein highlands.
      ▪ Mountain Nyala (Dega Agazon): Found in the Bale Mountains.
      ▪ 'Gelada' or 'Chilada' baboon: Found in the Semein highlands.
      ▪ Menilik's Bushbuk ('Dikula'): Found in the Shewan and Bale highlands.
      ▪ Swayne's Hartebeest ('Korkay'): Found in the Nechsar park and the Sankalle sanctuary.
      ▪ Semein Fox ('Key Kebero'): Found in the Bale and Semein Highlands.
      ▪ Wild Ass ('Yedur Ahiya'): Found in the Afar and Southeast Lowlands.

• These rare animals, especially the Walia Ibex and Semien Fox, are currently approaching extinction.
