UNIT FOUR: Population and Demographic Characteristics of Ethiopia

    This unit explores the fundamental concepts of human population, trends in population growth within Ethiopia, the composition of its population by age and sex, how population is distributed across the country, characteristic settlement patterns, the state of health and disease, the impacts of population growth on sustainable development, and the nation's rich linguistic and religious diversity.

4.1 Concept of Human Population
4.1.1 Concepts of Human Population

    Human population: The total number of human inhabitants of a specified area, such as a city, country, or continent, at a given time. This term is central to population studies.
    Population studies are a field of study that examines various aspects of populations, including their trends, characteristics, distribution, growth, and patterns. These studies are undertaken by various academic disciplines, such as:
        Geography
        Biology
        Sociology
        Medical Science
        History
    Population Geography: A specialized branch within geography that focuses on the spatial analysis of human population. It is particularly concerned with:
        Population-space relationships.
        The spatial aspects of human population.
        The interaction and interdependence between the human population and the physical environment.

Reasons for Studying Human Population

The study of human population is of great importance and is emphasized by many disciplines, including population geography, for several key reasons:

    It is dynamic, demonstrating significant quantitative and qualitative changes over time and space. Such changes have a tremendous impact on the socio-economic development of societies.
    Changes in the size, composition, structure, and location of the human population can have significant policy implications.
    Understanding the characteristics of the human population is crucial for adjusting situations to existing realities.
    Population studies provide knowledge essential for planning, especially by governments, in critical fields such as:
        Health
        Education
        Housing
        Social Security
        Employment
        Environment Preservation
    These studies also furnish the necessary information for formulating government population policies, which aim to modify demographic trends to achieve economic and social objectives.
    The human population is the major source of the labour force for both productive (e.g., agriculture, manufacturing) and non-productive (e.g., teaching, health services) economic sectors.
    The human population is also the main productive force and creator of material wealth, making its study vital for a country's overall socio-economic development.

4.2 Trends of Population Growth in Ethiopia
4.2.1 Trends of Population Growth in Ethiopia

    Ethiopia is a rapidly growing country in terms of population, ranking significantly in Africa and globally.

    Global and African Ranking: Ethiopia is the second most populous country in Africa and ranks 12th in the world.
    Population Size (2020): Estimated at 114.9 million (UN, 2021).
    Current Growth Rate: Approximately 2.6 percent, making Ethiopia's population among the fastest growing in the world.

Historical Population Growth in Ethiopia

Ethiopia's population has experienced significant growth over the past century:

    1900: 11.8 million persons.
    1920: ~13 million.
    1960: 23.5 million.
    2007 (Census): 73.8 million.
    2020 (Estimate): 114.9 million.

Population Doubling Time

    The time required for Ethiopia's population to double its size has been declining since 1960.
        From 1900 to 1960, it took 60 years for the population to double.
        Between 1960 and 1990, the population doubled in 30 years, indicating a rapid acceleration of growth.
        As of 2010-2020, the doubling time is estimated at 27 years.

Historical Growth Rates (Annual %)

    Before 1920: Less than 1.0 percent (reflected high mortality rate).
    After 1920: Slightly increased.
    By 1950: Rose to 2.0 percent.
    1950s and later: Rose further due to worldwide efforts to control diseases like malaria.
    1980: Reached 2.8 percent.
    Between 1980 and 1990: Around 3.0 percent.
    Between 2010 and 2020: Around 2.6 percent.

Although there has been a slight decline in the growth rate between censuses, Ethiopia continues to have one of the fastest-growing populations globally. For instance, the population increased from 42.6 million in 1984 to 53.5 million in 1994, and to 73.8 million in 2007.

4.3 Population Composition

    Population composition describes the characteristics of a population based on attributes like age, sex, marital status, and economic activities. Understanding these aspects is crucial for demographic analysis and national planning.

Population composition: Refers to those aspects of population that can be measured in terms of age, sex, marital status, family size, economic activities, nationality, language, and religion.
A. Age Structure

    Age structure: The classification of a given population into different age groups of various features. It is the pattern resulting from the distribution of population members into different age categories.
    Importance: The distribution of population by age is a fundamental demographic characteristic that plays an important role in the development of any society.
        The economic and cultural life of society critically depends on its age structure.
        Age structure data is extensively used in the planning process of any country for economic and cultural development, and for its regions.

Statistical Tools for Understanding Age Structure

Two important statistical tools are used to understand age structure:

    Age Groups:
        Widely used age groups: Typically five-year age groups (e.g., 0-4, 5-9, ..., 60-64, 65+).
        Broad age groups:
            0-14 years: Known as young age (the young dependent population).
            15-64 years: Known as working age (the economically active population).
            65+ years: Known as old age (the elderly dependent population).

    Population Pyramids:
        Population pyramid: A very useful graphic representation of the distribution of a population by age and sex. It aids in examining the age and sex structure of a population.
        Structure of a Population Pyramid:
            Vertical axis: Divided in years, usually in five-year age group intervals, from zero upwards.
            Horizontal axis: Shows the percentages of males and females within these age groups. Percentages can be calculated for each sex separately or for the total population.
        Shapes of Population Pyramids:
            Developing Countries (like Ethiopia): The population pyramid has the form of a triangle with a very broad base.
                This pattern indicates that many children are born (high birth rates), but fewer reach old age (high death rates, low life expectancy).
                The pyramid becomes increasingly narrower towards the top, showing that the percentage of the population decreases in upper age groups (65-69, 70-74, etc.).
                The dominance of the young-age population is a key feature.
            Developed Countries: The population pyramid is more rectangular, narrowing only near its top.
                This shape indicates lower birth rates and fewer people dying young.
                Both birth and death rates decline, resulting in a more equivalent number of people in each age group.

        Focus: The population pyramid of Ethiopia has a broad base that narrows towards the top as age increases. This shape is typical of a population with a high fertility rate and reflects high mortality rates in older age groups.

Age Dependency Ratio (ADR)

    Age Dependency Ratio (ADR): The relationship between the working or economically active population and the non-working (dependent) population.
    Concept of Dependency: People in the young (0-14) and old (65+) age groups are generally considered dependent on the working-age population (15-64).
    Purpose of ADR: It shows the magnitude of this dependency burden (represented by the non-working population) on the working-age population.
    Formula for Age Dependency Ratio (ADR):

Example (Country "Z"):

    Total Population: 65 million
    Children age (0-14): 40% of total population = 26 million
    Productive age group (15-64): 50% of total population = 32.5 million
    Old age group (65 and above): 10% of total population = 6.5 million
    Calculation:

        This result (100) indicates that there are 100 dependents for every 100 working-age people. In other words, each person of working age, on average, supports one dependent.
    Ethiopia's Age Dependency Ratio:
        In 2020, Ethiopia's ADR was 76.8/100.
        This means there are 76.8 dependents for every 100 working-age people.
        A high dependency ratio in Ethiopia signifies that the economically active population and the overall economy face a greater burden in supporting and providing social services for children and elderly persons who are often economically dependent.

B. Sex Structure

    Sex structure: Refers to the ratio of the male population to the female population at different age groups. It is one of the basic demographic characteristics of a population.
    Importance: Sex structure is very important for demographic analysis because it provides useful information about:
        Reproductive potential.
        Human resources.
        Potential impacts on marriage prospects, labour force participation, and other social and economic variables.
    Expression: Usually expressed as the number of males per 100 females in a population.
        A ratio greater than 100 indicates a greater number (an excess) of males than females.
    Formula for Calculating Sex Ratio:

    Ethiopia's Sex Ratio Trends:
        1984 Census: 99.4 males per 100 females.
        1994 Census: 101.3 males per 100 females.
        2007 Census: 101.9 males per 100 females.
        2020 Estimate: 100.12 males per 100 females.

4.4 Population Distribution

    Population distribution describes how people are spread across an area, while population density measures the concentration of people in that area. In Ethiopia, both are highly uneven due to a combination of physical and human factors.

    Population distribution: Refers to the way how population spreads out over a given area, of any size from a small area to the earth as a whole.
    Population density: The average number of people per square kilometre in a given area. It is an indicator of population distribution.

Uneven Population Distribution in Ethiopia

    Population in Ethiopia is unevenly distributed due to various physical and human-related reasons.
    Preferences for Settlement: People tend to concentrate in areas with agreeable life conditions, such as:
        Moderate climate
        Adequate supply of water
        Good vegetation cover
        Fertile soil
        Absence of disease-causing insects
    Highlands vs. Lowlands:
        Most people are concentrated in the highlands and plateaus, which offer favorable natural conditions for settlement and crop cultivation, leading to denser population. These areas generally lack vector-borne diseases.
        The lowlands have low population concentration.
        77.5 percent of Ethiopia's population lives in highlands at altitudes above 1800 meters.
        Only 22.5 percent lives at altitudes below 1800 meters.

Measures of Population Distribution

    Population distribution is a measurable concept, primarily through population density.
    Crude Density (CD): The most widely used method to measure population distribution. It represents the man-land ratio.
    Formula for Crude Density (CD):

    Ethiopia's Crude Density Trends:
        1990: 40.74 people/km²
        1998: 52 people/km²
        2020: 103.9 people/km²

Regional Variations in Population Density

    Ethiopia's population distribution is generally related to altitude, climate, and soil type.
    Highlands (endowed with moderate temperature, rich soil, adequate rainfall) show the highest population concentration.
    Lowlands (high temperatures and low rainfall) are very sparsely populated.
    There is considerable variation in population density among Ethiopia's administrative regions.

Factors Affecting Population Distribution in Ethiopia

The extreme unevenness of population distribution is a result of a combination of physical and human factors.
A. Physical Factors

The most significant physical factors influencing population distribution in Ethiopia are:

    Climate (mainly rainfall and temperature)

    Soil fertility

    Natural water supply

    Relief (slope and altitude)

    Vegetation cover

    Altitude as the Crucial Factor: Most physical factors in Ethiopia are influenced by altitude, making it the most crucial physical factor.
        Climatic Impact: Rainfall increases, and temperature decreases with an increase in altitude.
            Lowland areas: Characterized by very low rainfall and high temperatures.
            Highland areas: Characterized by high rainfall and moderately low temperatures.
        Soil and Vegetation: Soil formation and vegetation growth are closely associated with adequate rainfall and moderate temperatures, leading to better soils and vegetation cover in the highlands.
        Lowland Disadvantages: Besides scarce rainfall, high temperatures, and poor vegetation/soil, lowlands are also prone to tropical diseases such as malaria and yellow fever, which contribute to sparse population distribution.

    River Valleys: Most major river valleys in Ethiopia are characterized by sparse population concentration due to tropical diseases.
        Exception: The Awash River Valley, where irrigation agriculture is practiced, attracts more people. This contrasts with other parts of the world where major river valleys are zones of high population concentration.

B. Human Factors

The major human factors influencing population distribution in Ethiopia are:

    Types of economic activity
    Historical patterns of population movement

1. Types of Economic Activity

    The type of economic activity significantly influences the carrying capacity of land, which in turn affects the number of people an area can support.
    Pastoralism (arid and semi-arid lowlands):
        Requires large areas of grazing lands (e.g., 20+ hectares per head of cattle).
        Results in extremely low population densities.
    Crop-farming (highland areas):
        Generally has greater carrying capacity and higher population densities than pastoralist areas.
        Variations within crop-farming areas:
            Cereals (northern and north-central highlands): Have relatively low yields per unit area, leading to relatively lower carrying capacities and population densities.
            Enset and coffee-growing regions (southern regions): Have greater yields per unit area, contributing to very high population densities in some southern zones and woredas.
    Commercial Farms (Awash Valley):
        Development of commercial farms (small and large) has attracted thousands of settled and migratory people, leading to increased population concentration in formerly sparsely populated areas.
    Urban and Industrial Growth/Expansion:
        These factors cause population redistribution over time and are responsible for considerable spatial variation of population density currently.

2. Historical Patterns of Population Movement

    The historical movement of populations in Ethiopia has also played a role in shaping the current distribution.

4.5 Urban and Rural Settlement Patterns

    Settlements are organized groupings of people and their facilities. In Ethiopia, settlements are categorized as either rural or urban, distinguished primarily by their dominant economic activities and population density.

    Settlement: Refers to the characteristic groupings of population into occupancy units, together with the facilities in the form of houses and streets, which serve the inhabitants. It is also defined as a place where people live, carrying out activities like trade, agriculture, and manufacturing to make their livings.
    Origin: Settlements can be traced back to caves, where people gathered for protection against natural forces or rival tribes.
    Variety of Settlements: Hamlets, villages, towns, cities, metropolises, megalopolises, etc.
    Composition: An organized colony of human beings, including their residences, buildings (stores, factories, warehouses, etc.), paths, and streets.
    Influencing Factors: Settlements develop in response to physical and human factors, strategically located with respect to natural features (water, fuel, food, protection) and access to transportation and communications.

Ethiopian Settlement Categories

Ethiopian settlements are broadly categorized as rural and urban. The primary bases for this distinction are:

    Dominant economic activities:
        Urban settlements: Usually branded by non-agricultural economic activities.
        Rural settlements: Typically agricultural.
    Degree of population density:
        Urban settlements: Generally very high population densities.
        Rural settlements: Relatively lower population densities.

4.5.1 Rural Settlements

    Rural settlement: The name given to all villages and dispersed (scattered) settlements in areas far from urban centres. These settlements are widely dispersed and dominated by isolated homesteads.
    Inhabitants: Mainly engaged in agriculture.

Types of Rural Settlements in Ethiopia

Rural settlements in Ethiopia are broadly grouped into two: permanent and temporary settlements.
A. Permanent Settlements

    Permanent settlements: Considered permanent if their locations do not frequently change, i.e., if they remain in place for more than ten years.
    Location: Most rural settlements in the highland areas of Ethiopia are permanent, associated with crop-farming.
    Sub-types of Permanent Rural Settlements:
        Scattered (diffused or dispersed) settlements: Homesteads are separated by relatively long distances.
        Grouped (nucleated) settlements: Characterized by a large number of homesteads concentrated in one place.
    Villagization Programs:
        Derg Regime's compulsory villagization program: Resulted in the formation of grouped settlements in many parts of the country.
            Aim: To enhance communal land ownership and provision of social services.
            Outcome: People returned to original sites due to forced implementation and non-existent services.
        Current Government Programs: Recent villagization and resettlement programs are based on people's voluntary participation.

B. Temporary Settlements

    Temporary settlements: Mobile settlements inhabited by nomadic people of lowland Ethiopia.
    Location: Found in the Rift Valley region and lowlands of the west, east, and south, which are characterized by hot and dry climatic conditions.
    Lifestyle: These harsh conditions force people to seasonally move from one area to another with their herds.

4.5.2 Urban Settlements

    Urban settlements: Always larger and compact or nodal settlements.
    Economic Activities: Primarily involve processing primary goods (from farmers, miners, lumbermen), transportation, buying, selling, and financing.
    Functions:
        Centers of civilization, intellectual, and social capitals.
        Perform political, educational, and social character functions.
        Major transport centers, main assembly and break-of-bulk points, great markets, and major financial nodes.
    Urbanization: The process of the development of urban settlements.
        In Africa: Often a recent phenomenon, attributed to colonialism.
        In Ethiopia: An unplanned, natural phenomenon of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, not influenced by European colonialism.

Criteria for Urban Centers in Ethiopia

Not all grouped settlements are classified as urban centers. Ethiopia uses several criteria for assigning urban status to a settlement:

    The settlement has a minimum of 2000 people.
    Two-thirds of the population in the settlement are engaged in non-agricultural activities.
    The settlement has a chartered municipality.
    The presence of social services and amenities.

Growth of Urban Population in Ethiopia

    1984: About 322 settlements met urban criteria, comprising 10.23% of the total population (one of the least urban populations globally).
    1994: Number of settlements with >2,000 people increased to 539, representing 12.8% of the country's population.
    2007: Number further rose to 927.
    2020: The urban population constituted about 20 percent of the country's total population.

Spatial Variation of Urban Centers

    The distribution of urban centers in Ethiopia shows considerable spatial variation.
    This can be explained by the varying concentration of industries and services (such as schools, health institutions, water supplies, electricity, and means of transport).

4.6 Health and Disease in Ethiopia

    Ethiopia has made significant strides in improving the health conditions of its population, driven by government policies and health programs. However, the country still faces a substantial burden of preventable diseases, largely due to underlying socio-economic and infrastructural challenges.

Improvements in Health Condition

    Life Expectancy: Increased from 56.8 years in 2005 to 65.5 years in 2016.
    Mortality Rates: Three consecutive Ethiopian Demographic and Health Surveys (EDHS) (2005, 2011, 2016) showed declining trends in:
        Neonatal mortality
        Infant mortality
        Under-five mortality
        Maternal mortality
    Health Extension Program (HEP): This program has significantly contributed to increased access and coverage of high-impact public health interventions.
        Improved malaria control efforts.
        Increased access to safe drinking water.
        Improved toilet facilities.
        Expanded vaccination against childhood diseases.
    Government Investment: The Government of Ethiopia has heavily invested in health system strengthening through pro-poor policies and strategies, leading to significant gains.

Remaining Challenges and Disease Burden

Despite recognizable improvements, Ethiopia still faces:

    A heavy burden of diseases.
    A low rate of self-reported illness.
    Low health facility coverage and utilization.
    The majority of ill health is related to potentially preventable, communicable diseases and nutritional disorders.

Root Causes of Poor Health Status

    Lack of Access to Clean Water: Rivers and lakes, largely unsafe, remain the most important water sources, particularly for people in rural areas.
    Lack of Adequate Nutrition: Malnutrition is rampant and among the highest globally, with about half of children under five being malnourished, stunted, or wasted. This is due to food insecurity or poor knowledge about nutritional requirements and dietary habits.
    Disease Related to Beliefs, Behaviors, and Traditional Practices: Practices like Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) and early marriage have negative effects on health status.
    Lack of Health Services: The healthcare infrastructure has suffered from underfunding, leading to service coverage of less than 50% of the population. Services tend to be urban-biased.

Impacts of Poor Health and Nutrition

    Children: Likely to have life-long effects, making them physically unfit, unproductive, mentally inactive, and less dynamic.
    Adult Working Population: Protein and energy malnutrition affects this group, leading to high impacts on agricultural production and productivity.

Specific Disease Burdens

    Tropical Diseases: Lowlands are often infested with malaria and yellow fever, contributing to sparse population distribution in these areas.
    Major Killer Diseases: Account for about 75% of all deaths and include:
        Prenatal-maternal conditions
        Acute respiratory infection
        Malaria
        Nutritional deficiency (for children under 5 years)
        Diarrhea
        AIDS
        Tuberculosis

Health Workforce and Infrastructure

    Current Workforce (per 1000 population):
        Doctors: 0.04 (1 doctor for 26,943 people)
        Nurses: 0.43 (1 nurse for 2,311 people)
        Midwives: 0.05 (1 midwife for 21,810 people)
    Strategies to Overcome Shortages: The country has adopted a strategy to train health extension workers (HEP) and midwives, as well as scaling up family planning.
    Regional Variations: Regions like Amhara, Somali, and SNNP have population-hospital ratios of over 10,000, indicating poor access to doctors and hospital beds. Many people must travel significant distances to reach the nearest hospital.
    Future Outlook: Given Ethiopia's high population growth rate and the slow expansion of services, problems in health and other service areas are likely to continue.

4.7 Impacts of Population Growth on Sustainable Development in Ethiopia

    Ethiopia's rapid population growth rate significantly outpaces its economic growth and the carrying capacity of its natural resources. This leads to severe environmental degradation and socio-economic challenges, hindering sustainable development.

Environmental degradation: A process through which the natural environment is compromised in some way, reducing biological diversity and the general health of the environment.
Overall Impact of Rapid Population Growth

    Ethiopia's population growth rate (around 2.6 percent annually) is much higher and faster than its economic growth rate.
    It is growing beyond the carrying capacity of the country's natural resources (e.g., land, water, soil, forest).
    This has resulted in many negative environmental and socio-economic problems.

I. Population Growth and Environmental Degradation

    Occurrence: Massive environmental degradation has occurred in Ethiopia over the last few decades.
    Causes: Natural factors, unwise use of natural resources, unsound ecological practices, and population pressure.
    Major Accelerating Factor: Man's abusive actions, such as:
        Removal of natural vegetation cover through deforestation.
        Over-grazing.
        Inappropriate agricultural practices.
    Population Pressure: The rapidly growing population clears forests and vegetation at an alarming rate to meet increasing requirements for food, fiber, and energy.
    Land Degradation: Affects considerable parts of Ethiopia, especially the highlands, mainly due to water erosion.
        Soil erosion: The main feature of land resource depletion, leading to desertification.
        Consequences: Reduced agricultural production and shortage of food.
    Overall Effects: Population pressure and environmental degradation have driven the country into widespread food insecurity, drought, and famine for the last three decades.
    Main Activities Responsible for Environmental Degradation:
        Removal of vegetation cover.
        Over-cultivation (due to increasing population requirements for crop production and fuelwood).
        Overgrazing and high livestock density (leading to deterioration of rangeland resources).

II. Population Growth and Food Production

    Decline in Domestic Food Production: Ethiopia has experienced a decline in domestic food production over the last three decades.
    Ethiopian Agriculture:
        Dominated by smallholder peasant farming, contributing about 95% of annual food production.
        Heavily dependent on traditional and subsistent farming methods (limited modern technologies) and rainfall.
    Impact of Population Pressure:
        Rising population pressure and clearing of forests to satisfy basic demands (food, energy) make the soil susceptible to wind and water erosion.
        This directly affects both soil fertility and food production.
    Consequences: Sustained deterioration in per capita food production and hunger has resulted in widespread malnutrition, particularly among children, leading to physical and mental impairment.

III. Population Growth and Fuelwood Consumption

    Primary Energy Source: Fuelwood is the most important source of energy in Ethiopia.
    Supply Collapse: Its supply is steadily collapsing in most parts of the country because it is being collected faster than it can regrow.
    Causes of Deforestation and Shortage:
        Increased forest clearance to create farmland.
        Wood for construction purposes.
        Forage.
    Correlation: There is a direct correlation between population density and deforestation in Ethiopia - higher population means more trees cut down, without replacement.
    Direct Negative Consequences of Deforestation:
        Accelerates soil erosion.
        Destroys biodiversity.
        Affects rainfall by decreasing evapotranspiration.
        Affects the natural beauty of the affected areas.
    Increasing Population Needs Leading to Deforestation:
        Fuelwood
        Agricultural land
        Settlement land
        Grazing land

IV. Population Growth and Pollution

    Pollution: Refers to any undesirable change in natural conditions of water, air, and other components of the natural environment that has negative effects on the health and activities of human beings and other living creatures.
    Causes:
        Human activities: In households, industries, farmlands, means of transportation.
        Natural events: Volcanic eruptions, wildfires.
    Impact of Overcrowding: When an area is overcrowded by a rapidly growing number of people, the natural environment becomes polluted by a variety of unwanted and harmful wastes from human activities.
    Problem in Urban Centers: Pollution is a major problem in overpopulated urban centers like Addis Ababa, where many people live in unsafe environments with extremely polluted air and water, surrounded by garbage and pollutants.
    Increased Pollutants due to Rapid Population Growth:
        Sewage, solid wastes, and pollutant gases generated by households.
        Pollutant gases, liquids, and solid chemicals generated by expanded industries.
        Pollutant gases generated by the increasing number of automobiles.
        Agricultural pollutants, such as fertilizers, pesticides, and animal wastes.

V. Population Growth and Provision of Social Services

    Effect on Education:
        Rapid population growth has resulted in a growing demand for education.
        The total number of students has increased enormously, but many children still do not get the chance to go to school.
        Many urban centers face overcrowded classrooms.
    Effect on Health:
        Ethiopia has a poor health status and a high rate of population growth.
        The majority of the population has low access to modern health services, with the situation in urban areas being relatively better than rural areas.
        Contributing factors to poor health: Growing poverty, low level of education, inadequate access to clean water, shortage of sanitary facilities, and poor access to health facilities.

4.8 Language and Religious Diversity in Ethiopia

    Ethiopia is a country characterized by significant ethnic, linguistic, and religious diversity, with numerous languages belonging to two major super families and a rich tapestry of faiths.

4.8.1 Language

    Ethiopians are ethnically diverse, with linguistic categorization forming the basis for most important differences.
    About 80 languages are spoken in Ethiopia.
    Ethiopian languages belong to two major Super Families:
        Afro-Asiatic
        Nilo-Saharan
    Most Ethiopian languages belong to the Afro-Asiatic super family.

I. Afro-Asiatic Super Family

The Afro-Asiatic super family in Ethiopia is divided into three families:

    a) Cushitic:
        Predominant regions: Central, southern, eastern, and northeastern parts of Ethiopia, mainly in Afar, Oromia, Sidama, and Somali Regional States.
        Characteristics: Has the largest number of speakers and the widest spatial coverage.
        Examples of languages: Oromo (Afaan Oromoo), Somali (Af-Somali), Sidama (Sidaamu Afo), Afar (Qafaraf), Kembata (Kambatissa), Hadiya (Hadiyissa), Halaba (Halabissa), Gedeo (Gedeoffa), and others.
    b) Semitic:
        Predominant regions: Northern, central, and eastern parts of Ethiopia, particularly in the regional states of Tigray, Amhara, Harari, and Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples' Regional State (SNNPRS).
        Examples of languages: Amhara (Amarigna), Tigrai (Tigrigna), Gurage (Guragigna), Adere (Aderigna), and Argoba (Argobigna).
    c) Omotic:
        Predominant regions: South-central and south-western parts of Ethiopia, mainly between the Lakes of the southern Rift Valley and the Omo River.
        Characteristics: Numerous languages, though not as widely spoken as Cushitic and Semitic languages.
        Examples of languages: Wolaita (Wolaitatto), Dawuro (Dawurootsuwa), Kafa (Kafi noono), and Konta (Kontaatsuwa).

II. Nilo-Saharan Super Family

    Predominant regions: Spoken in the western lowlands of Ethiopia along the border with Sudan, in Gambella and Benishangul Gumuz Regional States.
    Examples of languages: Kunama, Beiji, Gumuz, Mao, Kewam, Nuer, Annuak, and others.

4.8.2 Religion

    Ethiopia is home to diversified religions.
    According to the Ethiopian Central Statistical Agency (2007) census data, the national religious composition includes:
        Orthodox: 43.5%
        Islam: 33.9%
        Protestant: 18.6%
        Catholic: 0.7%
        Others: 3.2%
