Unit One: Geological History and Topography of Ethiopia
- This unit explores the foundational aspects of Geography, including its definition, scope, and branches. It then delves into Ethiopia's specific location, size, and shape, analyzing their impact. Finally, it examines the geological history and the diverse landforms that have shaped Ethiopia.


1.1 Geography: Meaning, Scope, and Branches
- At the end of this section, you will be able to:

  ◦ Define geography as a subject.
  ◦ Describe the scope of geography.
  ◦ Identify the branches of geography.

1.1.1 Meaning of Geography
- Origin of the Term: The term "Geography" originated from ancient Greece, combining two words:

  ◦ Geo: Meaning "earth".
  ◦ Graphos: Meaning "writing".
- Early Definition: Eratosthenes (276-194 B.C.), a Greek philosopher, is credited with coining the term "Geography" and defined it as "the field of study that deals with the description of the earth."

- Evolution of Definitions: Over time, various scholars have provided definitions reflecting the evolving concerns and focus of geography:

  ◦ Alexander Von Humboldt (1769-1859): Described geography as "a synthesizing discipline to connect the general with the particular through measurement, mapping, and a regional emphasis."
  ◦ Concise Oxford Dictionary (1964): Defined it as "the science of the earth's surfaces."
  ◦ R. Hartshorne (1899-1992): Stated geography is "a branch of knowledge that is concerned with the provision of an accurate, orderly and rational description of distributions on the surface of the earth."
  ◦ M. Yeates (1968): Defined geography as "a science that is concerned with the rational development and testing of theories that explain and predict the spatial distribution and locations of things and phenomena on the surface of the earth."
- Contemporary Definition: While a single universally accepted definition is difficult due to the discipline's dynamic nature, a widely accepted definition is:

  ◦ Geography is the scientific study of the Earth that describes and analyses spatial and temporal variations of physical, biological and human phenomena, and their interrelationships and dynamism over the surface of the Earth.


1.1.2 The Scope of Geography
- Scope: Refers to the breadth and depth of a field of study. The scope of geography is very wide and dynamic, changing with new discoveries and ideas.

- Areas of Study: Geography examines a vast range of physical and human features, including their causes, effects, and interactions. This encompasses:

  ◦ Hydrosphere: All bodies of water (oceans, rivers, lakes).
  ◦ Biosphere: All living organisms on Earth's surface.
  ◦ Atmosphere: The layer of gases surrounding the planet.
  ◦ Lithosphere: The solid outer layer of rocks.
  ◦ Anthroposphere: The part of the environment modified by humans.
- Interdisciplinary Concerns: Geography also addresses economic, social, political, and ecological problems, and integrates modern technologies like GPS and GIS for analysis.

- Major Focus Areas:

  ◦ The Earth's position in the universe and its movements.
  ◦ Earth's physical features, the forces shaping them, and their spatial and temporal variations.
  ◦ The interrelationships and interdependence between humans and their natural environment.
  ◦ Atmospheric conditions, weather, and climate distribution.
  ◦ Earth's materials and diverse landforms.
  ◦ Major human economic activities and their environmental impacts.
- Holistic Discipline: Geography is considered a holistic discipline of knowledge and research, integrating phenomena from physical and biotic to human environments.


1.1.3 Branches of Geography
- The two main branches of geography are:

  ◦ Physical Geography
  ◦ Human Geography

A. Physical Geography
- Focus: Studies the distribution of natural features of the world, including climate, landforms, soil, vegetation, surface drainage systems, water resources, and animals. It also examines the causes, effects, and interactions of these features.

- Sub-branches:

  ◦ Geomorphology: Studies landforms and their distribution.
  ◦ Climatology: Examines patterns of climate.
  ◦ Biogeography: Studies the distribution of plants and animals.
    ▪ Zoogeography: Focuses on animal distribution.
    ▪ Phytogeography: Focuses on plant distribution.
  ◦ Soil Geography: Studies soil distribution.
  ◦ Oceanography: Studies oceans.

B. Human Geography
- Focus: Studies the distribution and influence of human aspects of the world, including cultures, population settlement, economic activities, and political systems.

- Sub-branches:

  ◦ Population Geography: Studies the distribution and characteristics of human populations.
  ◦ Cultural Geography: Examines the spatial distribution and diffusion of cultural traits.
  ◦ Economic Geography: Analyzes the location, distribution, and spatial organization of economic activities.
  ◦ Political Geography: Studies the spatial aspects of politics and political organization.
  ◦ Urban Geography: Focuses on the spatial structure and organization of cities.
  ◦ Historical Geography: Examines geographical phenomena in past times.


1.2 Location, Size, and Shape of Ethiopia
- At the end of this section, you will be able to:

  ◦ Describe the absolute and relative location of Ethiopia.
  ◦ Discuss the effects of the size and shape of Ethiopia on its sociocultural, political, and economic condition.

1.2.1 Location of Ethiopia
- Importance of Location: Location is crucial in geography as it describes the position of a place relative to its surroundings.

- Types of Location:

  ◦ Relative Location: The position of a place in relation to other geographic features.
    ▪ Vicinal Location: Describes a country's position in relation to its neighboring countries.
      • Ethiopia is a landlocked country bordered by six nations: Djibouti, Eritrea, Kenya, Somalia, Sudan, and South Sudan.
      • Total boundary length: 5,260 km.
      • Longest boundary shared with: Somalia (1,600 km).
      • Smallest boundary shared with: Djibouti (310 km).
    ▪ Strategic Location (Natural/Global Location): Describes a country's position in reference to major water bodies and landmasses.
      • Located in northeastern (Horn) Africa.
      • Southwest of the Asian continent.
      • Within the Nile Basin.
      • South of Europe.
      • Northwest of the Indian Ocean.
      • Southwest of the Red Sea.
      • South of the Mediterranean Sea.
  ◦ Absolute Location (Astronomical Location): Expressed in terms of latitudes and longitudes.
    ▪ Ethiopia is located between 3°N - 15°N latitudes and 33°E - 48°E longitudes.
    ▪ Extreme Points:
      • North: Northern tip of Tigray.
      • South: Moyalle (Borena).
      • West: Akobo (Gambella).
      • East: Tip of Ogaden.


1.2.2 Size of Ethiopia
- Areal Extent: Ethiopia has a total area of 1,106,000 square kilometers.

- Rank: It is the tenth largest country in Africa.

- Proportion:

  ◦ 0.7% of the world's land area.
  ◦ 3.6% of Africa's land mass.
- Size Comparison with Neighbors: Ethiopia is significantly larger than most of its neighbors (except Sudan).

- "Horn of Africa" Significance: Ethiopia is the largest country in the Horn of Africa, which, combined with its large population, confers advantages.

- Advantages of Large Size:

  ◦ Diverse agro-ecological zones, leading to a wide variety of fauna and flora.
  ◦ Large amount of arable land.
  ◦ Great variety of mineral resources.
  ◦ Home to diverse ethnic groups.
- Disadvantages of Large Size:

  ◦ Requires expensive administrative expenditure.
  ◦ Necessitates a large army for defense.
  ◦ Requires significant financial power for infrastructure development.
  ◦ Presents challenges for effective administration and socio-economic integration.


1.2.3 Shape of Ethiopia
- Shape Variations: Countries vary in shape (compact, elongated, truncated).

- Ethiopia's Shape: Ethiopia has a more or less compact (circular) shape. This is because its extreme north-south and east-west spans are comparable.

- Indicators of Compactness:

  ◦ Boundary-Circumference Ratio (B/C): Compares the country's boundary length to the circumference of a circle with the same area. A ratio closer to 1 indicates greater compactness.
    ▪ Example Calculation for Ethiopia: B/C ratio = 1.411, indicating a 41% deviation from a perfect circle.
  ◦ Area-Boundary Ratio (A/B): Compares the total area to the total boundary length. A larger area per unit boundary length signifies greater compactness.
    ▪ Implication for Ethiopia: If 1 km of border is defended, 210 km² of hinterland is protected.
  ◦ Actual Area - Area of Inscribing Circle (A/A'): Compares the country's area to the area of the smallest circle that can contain it. A value closer to 1 suggests more compactness.
- Advantages of Compact Shape: Primarily militaristic, offering better defense capabilities due to a shorter border relative to its area.


1.3 Geological History of Ethiopia
- At the end of this section, you will be able to:

  ◦ Explain the geological history of Ethiopia.
  ◦ Distinguish the geological processes that resulted in the current landforms of Ethiopia.
  ◦ Describe the major landforms of Ethiopia.

1.3.1 The Geological Processes in Ethiopia
- Geological Time Scale: Scientists use a geological time scale to measure Earth's history, divided into four major eras:

  ◦ Precambrian Era: 4.5 billion to 600 million years ago.
  ◦ Paleozoic Era: 600 million to 250 million years ago.
  ◦ Mesozoic Era: 250 million to 70 million years ago.
  ◦ Cenozoic Era: 70 million years ago to the present.
- Characteristics Distinguishing Eras:

  ◦ Relative positions of continents.
  ◦ Prevailing climate.
  ◦ Predominant life forms (plants and animals).

I. The Precambrian Era (4.5 Billion to 600 Million Years Ago)
- Significance: The oldest and longest geological era.

- Key Geological Events in the Horn:

  ◦ Frequent orogenic movements (mountain building, often through volcanic eruptions).
  ◦ Intensive volcanic activities.
  ◦ Denudation (wearing down of mountains) in later periods.
  ◦ Formation of folded mountains.
- Life Forms: First forms of life emerged (e.g., amoeba, jellyfish).

- Rock Formation: The old crystalline basement/basement complex rock formed. This forms the foundation beneath all other rocks.

  ◦ Outcrops of this rock are found in parts of Ethiopia (e.g., central/northern Tigray, Mettekel, Assossa, Abbay Gorge, southern Sidama, Omo, Bale, Borena).

II. The Paleozoic Era (600 Million to 250 Million Years Ago)
- Dominant Process: Denudation followed by peneplanation (reduction of land to a nearly flat surface).

- Structural Formation: No significant structural formations occurred.

- Rock Formation Gap: This era is noted for a gap in rock formation in Ethiopia.

- Inselbergs: Massive denudational activity led to the formation of inselbergs (residual hills).

- Life Forms: Predominance of invertebrates.


III. The Mesozoic Era (250 Million to 70 Million Years Ago)
- Dominant Processes: Alternate slow sinking (subsidence) and uplifting of the landmass (epeirogenesis).

- Rock Formation: This was an era of sedimentary rock formation.

- Periods:

  ◦ Triassic Period (250 - 180 million years ago):
    ▪ Landmass sank, followed by transgression (sea advancing inland).
    ▪ Formation of Adigrat sandstone (oldest sedimentary rock, younger towards the northwest).
  ◦ Jurassic Period (180 - 135 million years ago):
    ▪ Sea transgression continued northwestward.
    ▪ Formation of Hintalo limestone (younger in the northwest, older in the southeast).
  ◦ Cretaceous Period (135 - 70 million years ago):
    ▪ Landmass began to rise, and the sea regressed (retreated).
    ▪ Formation of Upper Sandstone (youngest sedimentary rock, overlying others).
- Sedimentary Rock Exposure: These sedimentary rocks were later buried by Cenozoic igneous rocks but are exposed in some areas (southeastern lowlands, central Tigray, Abbay and Wabishebelle gorges).

- Life Forms: Predominance of reptiles (e.g., dinosaurs). At the end of the era, dinosaurs disappeared, and mammals, birds, and flowering plants emerged.


IV. The Cenozoic Era (70 Million Years Ago to Present)
- Significance: The most recent geological era, marked by significant structural, climatic, and biological events that shaped modern landforms.

- Periods: Tertiary and Quaternary.

a. Geologic Events of the Tertiary Period (70 Million - 2 Million Years Ago)
- Uplifting: Continued and reached its maximum height, forming huge dome-like structures.

- Cracking and Lava Flows: Great cracks opened in the crust, leading to extensive basaltic lava flows (Trappean lava series).

- Formation of Highlands and Plateaus:

  ◦ Northwestern Highlands
  ◦ Southeastern Highlands
  ◦ Somali plateaus
- Formation of the Great East African Rift Valley System: Cracking and faulting during this period led to the formation of this major rift system, of which the Ethiopian Rift Valley System is a part.

b. Geologic Events of the Quaternary Period (2 Million Years Ago - Present)
- Recent Volcanic Activities: Took place after the formation of the Rift Valley.

- Structures Formed:

  ◦ Afar Horst (extending into Djibouti)
  ◦ Active volcano: Ertale (Afar)
  ◦ Dormant volcano: Fentalle (Eastern Oromia)
  ◦ Extensive lava fields (Metahara)
  ◦ Thermal springs and fumaroles in the rift valley
  ◦ Pluvial rainfall and resulting deposition in lowlands
- Human Evolution: This period is also considered the time of modern human evolution.


1.3.2 Landforms of Ethiopia
- Definition: Individual earth surface features.

- Formation: Largely a result of Cenozoic tectonic and volcanic activities.

- Characteristics: Great diversity including highlands, plateaus, ambas (flat-topped mountains), rugged mountains, deep river gorges, and lowlands.

- Altitude Variation: Ranges from 116 meters below mean sea level at the Dallol depression to 4620 meters above mean sea level at Ras Dashen.

- Highlands vs. Lowlands: 56% of Ethiopia's land is considered highland (defined by an altitude of 1000 meters).

- "Roof of Eastern Africa": Ethiopia's high proportion of elevated surface earns it this nickname.

- Influencing Forces: Landforms are shaped by two opposing forces:

  ◦ Endogenic Forces: Originating from inside the Earth (e.g., volcanic activity, tectonic forces).
  ◦ Exogenic Forces: Originating at the Earth's surface (e.g., denudation, peneplanation).

Major Physiographic Divisions of Ethiopia:
- The relief of Ethiopia can be divided into three main physiographic divisions:

I. The Western Highlands and Associated Lowlands
- Extent: Stretches from Tigray in the north to the highlands of Gamo and Goffa in the southwest.

- Separation: Separated from the southeastern highlands by the Rift Valley.

- Significance: The largest physiographic region, making up 44% of the country's area.

- River Sources: Source of major rivers like Abbay, Baro, and Tekezze, influencing geopolitics.

- A. The Western Highlands (Sub-divisions):
  ◦ a) The Plateau of Tigray:
    ▪ Location: Northernmost plateau, bounded by the Mereb River (north) and Tekezze River (south).
    ▪ Geology: Composed of eroded sandstones and limestones, with poor, thin soils. Exposed to severe erosion.
    ▪ Topography: Features very high mountains (over 3000 m).
    ▪ Prominent Peaks: Mount Tsibet (3988 m), Mount Ambalage (3291 m), Mount Assimba (3248 m).
  ◦ b) The North Central Massif:
    ▪ Location: Between the Abbay Gorge (south) and Tekezze Gorge (north). Includes highlands of Gondar, Gojjam, and Wollo.
    ▪ Topography: Most rugged and dissected plateaus in Ethiopia, capped by Tertiary basalts and surrounded by deep gorges. Features isolated arable lands called ambas.
    ▪ River Formation: Abbay and Tekezze rivers and their tributaries have formed deep gorges.
    ▪ Mountain Systems: Gondar/Gojjam mountains are separated from Wollo mountains by deep gorges, linked by the Yeju-Wadla Delanta land bridge.
    ▪ Key Massifs: South Gondar (Semein), Lasta and Wollo, Gojjam.
    ▪ Gojjam Massif: Most extensive tableland, formed on the Amedamit-Choke mountains, carved by the Abbay River.
    ▪ Agriculture: Known for cereals (teff), pulses, and oil seeds.
    ▪ Prominent Peaks: Mount Ras Dashen (4620 m - highest in Ethiopia), Mount Legeda (4532 m), Mount Analu (4480 m), Mount Tefaw Lezer (4456 m), Mount Kolo (4300 m), Mount Guna (4231 m), Mount Abuna Yoseph (4190 m), Mount Hey (4154 m).
  ◦ c) The Plateau of Shewa (Central Plateau):
    ▪ Characteristics: Smallest sub-division of western highlands; dome-shaped.
    ▪ Watershed: Serves as a watershed for Awash, Omo-Gibe, and Abbay River basins.
    ▪ Shape: Crescent shape, causing the Abbay River to swerve northwards.
    ▪ Boundaries: Separated by Abbay gorge (north), Awash River/Rift Valley (southeast), and Gibe River (south).
    ▪ Drainage: Tributaries of Abbay (west) and Awash (east) rivers.
    ▪ High Mountains: Mount Abbuye Meda (4000 m), Mount Guraghe (3721 m).
  ◦ d) The Southwestern Highlands:
    ▪ Location: South of the Abbay trough, heavily eroded by heavy rainfall.
    ▪ Climate: Wettest region in Ethiopia (average annual rainfall > 1500 mm).
    ▪ Included Areas: Wollega, Illubabor, Jimma, Kafa, Gamo, and Goffa highlands.
    ▪ Drainage:
      • Northwards: Dabus and Didesa (Abbay tributaries).
      • Westwards: Headstreams of Baro-Akobo River.
      • Southwards: Omo-Gibe River (to Lake Turkana).
      • Eastwards: Gojeb-Gibe River.
    ▪ Elevation: Generally lower than northern/eastern highlands (few areas above 2500 m).
    ▪ Highest Points: Gamo-Konso Highlands, Maji-Korma Highlands, Dawuro-Konta Highlands, Tullu Wallel, Benishangul mountain. Mount Gughe (4200 m) is the highest in this region.
    ▪ Agriculture: Known for coffee, spices, timber, rubber, cattle, honey, maize, and high forest cover.

- B. The Western Lowlands:
  ◦ Extent: From western Tigray to southern Gamo and Goffa, bordering Sudan and South Sudan.
  ◦ Elevation: 500-1000 m a.m.s.l.
  ◦ Climate: Arid and semi-arid.
  ◦ Wettest Lowland: Baro-Akobo lowland, due to consistent river flow (Baro river).
  ◦ Sub-divisions:
    ▪ Tekezze and Angereb Lowlands (northern).
    ▪ Abbay Dinder Lowlands.
    ▪ Baro-Akobo Lowlands.
    ▪ Omo-Gibe Lowlands.
  ◦ Livelihood: Primarily pastoralist and semi-pastoralist due to climate hardship.
  ◦ Notable Towns: Humera, Kurmuk, Omedla, Metema (border business centers).

II. The Southeastern Highlands and Associated Lowlands
- Location: Southeast of the Rift Valley.

- A. The Southeastern Highlands:
  ◦ Components:
    ▪ Hararghe plateaus.
    ▪ Arsi plateau.
    ▪ Bale Massif.
    ▪ Sidama highlands.
  ◦ Formation: Similar to North and Southwestern Highlands, capped by basaltic rock.
  ◦ River Sources: Main sources of the Wabe Shebelle and Genale rivers.
  ◦ Boundaries: Rift Valley fault line (west), Ogaden Lowlands (east), Elkerie and Borena Lowlands (south).
  ◦ a) The Hararghe Plateaus:
    ▪ Topography: Rises sharply from the Rift Valley fault line, gently extending east to Jigjiga, then descending to the Ogaden Lowlands.
    ▪ Drainage: Left-bank tributaries of the Wabe Shebelle River.
    ▪ Geology: Basaltic rocks eroded, exposing limestones and earlier sedimentary rocks.
    ▪ Agriculture: Significant producers of coffee, chat, sorghum, and millet.
    ▪ Prominent Peaks: Mount Gara Muletta (3381 m), Mount Jebel Tita (3122 m).
  ◦ b) The Arsi Plateau:
    ▪ Topography: Rolling plateau, suitable for farming; features Gugu and Chillalo Massif.
    ▪ Agriculture: Known for wheat production.
    ▪ Prominent Peaks: Mount Chillallo (4136 m), Mount Bada (4139 m), Mount Kaka (4180 m).
  ◦ c) The Bale Massif:
    ▪ Location: Adjacent to Arsi Plateau, separated by headstreams of the Wabe Shebelle River.
    ▪ Topography: Northern part is flat (basaltic plateaus); southern part features huge mountains.
    ▪ Agriculture: Known for barley production.
    ▪ Highest Points: Mount Tulu Dimtu (4377 m), Mount Batu (4307 m).
  ◦ d) The Sidama Highlands:
    ▪ Location: Southwest extension of southeastern highlands, separated from Bale Highlands by the Genale river valley.
    ▪ Topography: Plateau slopes gently south and southeast.
    ▪ Drainage: Genale River and its tributaries.

- B. The Southeastern Lowlands:
  ◦ Extent: Foothills of southeastern highlands along the Ethio-Somalian and Ethio-Kenyan borders.
  ◦ Elevation: 500-1000 m a.s.l.
  ◦ Components: Ogaden plains, Elkeri plains, Borena plains, Genale plains.
  ◦ Climate: Extreme conditions with low annual rainfall (often below 500 mm).
  ◦ Geology: Covered by sandstones and recent marine deposits.
  ◦ Livelihood: Pastoralist way of life due to harsh climate.

III. The Rift Valley
- Definition: A narrow strip of land formed by fractures in the Earth's crust.

- Ethiopian Rift Valley System: Part of the Great East African Rift Valley, extending from the Afar Depression (north) through the Rift Valley Lakes' region to Lake Turkana.

- Formation: Formed at the end of the Tertiary Period due to tectonic epeirogenic activity.

- Evidence of Instability: Presence of active volcanoes, minor faulting, hot springs, cinder cones, fumaroles, and geysers.

- Orientation: Runs diagonally from northeast to southwest, dividing the Ethiopian Highlands.

- Length and Area: Covers 1700 km in Ethiopia and Eritrea, comprising 18% of the country's total area.

- Subdivisions of the Ethiopian Rift Valley System:
  ◦ a) The Afar Triangle (Northern Part):
    ▪ Size: Largest and widest part (200-300 km).
    ▪ Altitude: Low, ranging from 116 m below sea level (Kobar Sink) to 900 m above sea level (Awash).
    ▪ Features:
      • Faulted depressions (Dallol Depression) and grabens (troughs).
      • Cinder cones and volcanic mountains.
      • Extensive salt plains and lakes (e.g., Lake Assale, Lake Afrera).
      • Bounded by parallel fault lines.
      • Floor composed of grabens, volcanic ash/lava deposits, lacustrine and fluvial deposits, and volcanic mountains (e.g., Mount Fentalle).
    ▪ Special Characteristics: Fossil-rich sediments and rich archaeological sites indicating it was home to ancient ancestors of primates and hominids.
  ◦ b) The Main Ethiopian Rift or the Lakes' Region (Central Part):
    ▪ Extent: From the lower Awash basin to Lake Chamo.
    ▪ Characteristics: Most elevated and narrowest part; also the wettest, most densely vegetated, and most densely populated.
    ▪ Livelihood: Sedentary farming is practiced.
    ▪ Features: Numerous lakes of economic and aesthetic value.
  ◦ c) The Chew-Bahir Rift (Southern Part):
    ▪ Also Known As: Omo-Gibe trough.
    ▪ Size: Smallest section of the Ethiopian Rift System.
    ▪ Separation: Separated from the Lakes' Region by the Konso Highlands.
    ▪ Features: Extensive shallow marshy area covered by tall grasses; Sagan and Woito streams end here.
    ▪ Splitting: Split into Ganjuli and Galena Valleys by the Amaro mountain range near Arba Minch.
    ▪ Livelihood: Primarily occupied by pastoralist populations.


Unit Summary
- Ethiopia is located in Eastern Africa within 3°N-15°N latitudes and 33°E-48°E longitudes. It has a generally compact shape.

- In terms of size, Ethiopia is the tenth largest country in Africa (1,106,000 sq km). Its large size offers advantages like diverse agro-ecological zones and resources but also disadvantages in administration and infrastructure.

- Ethiopia's geological structure is shaped by events across the four major geological eras:

  ◦ Precambrian: Formation of crystalline basement complex rocks and folded mountains.
  ◦ Paleozoic: Dominated by denudation and peneplanation.
  ◦ Mesozoic: Characterized by sedimentary rock formation (Adigrat sandstone, Hintalo limestone, Upper Sandstone) due to land sinking and uplifting.
  ◦ Cenozoic: Marked by tectonic and volcanic activities, leading to the formation of the Ethiopian Rift Valley System.
- Ethiopia's landforms are diverse, including rugged mountains, plateaus, river gorges, a rift valley, and lowlands. Altitudinal variation ranges from the lowest point at Dallol depression (-116 m) to the highest peak at Ras Dashen (4620 m).
