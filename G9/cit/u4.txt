Constitutional Experiences of Ethiopia[cite: 7]

This section explores the evolution of constitutional frameworks in Ethiopia, from traditional unwritten systems to modern written constitutions, highlighting their characteristics and impact on governance and citizens' rights[cite: 7].

1. Introduction to Ethiopian Constitutional History[cite: 7]

• Ethiopia possesses a rich and long history of constitutional experiences[cite: 7].
• Historically, Ethiopia primarily relied on traditional or unwritten constitutions until the early 1930s[cite: 7].
• The first written constitution in Ethiopian history was introduced by Emperor Haile Selassie I in 1931[cite: 7].

2. Traditional Constitutional Experiences of Ethiopia[cite: 7]
2.1. Basis of Rule[cite: 7]

• Until the early 1930s, the authority and rule of the Ethiopian Emperors were founded upon various traditional constitutions[cite: 7].
• Key traditional constitutional documents included:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Kibre Negest[cite: 7]
  ◦ Fetha Negest[cite: 7]
  ◦ Sirate Mengist[cite: 7]

2.2. Characteristics and Purpose[cite: 7]

• Dominance of Myths and Legends: The traditional constitutional experience was largely characterized by the prevalence of myths and legends[cite: 7].
• Legitimacy for Monarchs: The primary function of these traditional documents was to provide legitimacy to the monarchs' rule, rather than focusing on ensuring the basic rights and freedoms of citizens[cite: 7].
• Specific Examples and Their Justifications:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Kibre Negest (Glory of Kings): This document asserted that Ethiopian monarchs derived their power to rule directly from God[cite: 7].
  ◦ Fetha Negest (Law of Kings): This text justified the authority of kings and their officials. According to Fetha Negest, challenging the authority of the king was viewed as challenging or rebelling against one's Creator, God[cite: 7].
  ◦ Sirate Mengist: An important document that emerged in the 19th century, which served primarily as an administrative and protocol directive for the government[cite: 7].

3. Written Constitutional Experiences in Ethiopia[cite: 7]
3.1. The 1931 Written Constitution[cite: 7]

• Introduction: Ethiopia's first written constitution was granted by Emperor Haile Selassie I on July 16, 1931, just a few months after his coronation[cite: 7].
• Motivations for Introduction: The Emperor was motivated by two main reasons:[cite: 7]
  ◦ External Image: His high aspiration to showcase Ethiopia as a modern or civilized state to the outside world[cite: 7].
  ◦ Consolidation of Power: To formally consolidate the Emperor's absolute power over local nobilities through a structured constitutional framework[cite: 7].
• Structure: This constitution comprised 7 chapters and 55 articles[cite: 7].
• Principal Aim: The main goal of this constitution was to entrench and consolidate the power of the king, rather than to establish foundations for the fundamental rights and dignity of citizens[cite: 7].

3.2. The Revised Constitution of 1955[cite: 7]

• Revision Date: The imperial regime revised its constitution in 1955, twenty-four years after the adoption of the first written constitution[cite: 7].
• Reasons for Revision:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Internal Reason: The Italian invasion of Ethiopia in 1935 had interrupted the 1931 constitution, forcing the Emperor to flee[cite: 7].
  ◦ External Reason: The most basic external reason was the federation of Eritrea with Ethiopia in 1952[cite: 7].
• Structure: The 1955 revised constitution was more extensive, with 8 chapters and 131 articles[cite: 7].
• Limitations: Similar to the 1931 constitution, it did not limit the power of the Emperor and prominent officials, and the democratic and human rights of citizens were not guaranteed[cite: 7].

3.3. The 1987 Constitution of the People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia (PDRE)[cite: 7]

• Political Shift: In 1974, the feudal monarchy (Imperial Regime) was overthrown by the Derg military regime[cite: 7].
• Constitutional Vacuum: The period from 1974 up to 1987 is known as a constitutional vacuum or lacuna in Ethiopian political history, as the Derg regime ruled through decrees and proclamations without a formal constitutional status[cite: 7].
• Adoption: The Derg government adopted its own constitution in 1987[cite: 7].
• Structure: This constitution contained 7 chapters and 119 articles[cite: 7].
• Key Changes Introduced:[cite: 7]
  ◦ System of Government: It transformed the system of government from a monarchy to a republican form of government[cite: 7].
  ◦ Secularism: For the first time in Ethiopia, the 1987 PDRE constitution introduced the idea of secularism, or the separation of state and religion[cite: 7].
• Democratic Legacy: Similar to its predecessors, this constitution did not leave a legacy of democratic constitutional order[cite: 7].
• Overthrow: After 17 years in power, the Derg regime, led by Colonel Mengistu Hailemariam, was overthrown by the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) in May 1991[cite: 7].

3.4. The Transitional Charter of 1991[cite: 7]

• Introduction: The 1991 Transitional Charter was introduced by the EPRDF in May 1991, following the demise of the Derg regime[cite: 7].
• Purpose: It served as the law of the land for the transitional period from 1991 up to 1995[cite: 7].
• Structure: This was a very short document, addressing only some fundamental issues, and contained only 20 articles[cite: 7].
• Key Recognitions: The charter recognized:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Freedom[cite: 7]
  ◦ Equal rights[cite: 7]
  ◦ Self-determination of all nationalities[cite: 7]
  ◦ It also placed a high premium on human rights[cite: 7].

3.5. The 1995 Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia (FDRE) Constitution[cite: 7]

• Adoption: The period governed by the Transitional Charter concluded with the adoption of the FDRE Constitution in December 1995[cite: 7].
• Significant Changes and Introductions: The 1995 FDRE Constitution brought about several crucial changes compared to previous constitutional experiences:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Federal State Structure: It introduced a federal state structure for the first time in Ethiopia[cite: 7].
  ◦ Parliamentary Democracy: It established a system of parliamentary democracy[cite: 7].
  ◦ Bicameral Parliament: It led to the establishment of two houses of parliament: the House of Federation and the House of People Representatives[cite: 7].
  ◦ Rights and Duties: It explicitly guarantees the rights and freedoms of citizens and also describes the duties of the citizens[cite: 7].
• Structure: This constitution includes a preamble, 11 chapters, and 106 articles[cite: 7].

4. Fundamental Constitutional Principles of the 1995 FDRE Constitution[cite: 7]

The 1995 FDRE Constitution is built upon several core principles that define its nature and purpose. These principles ensure the sovereignty of the people, the supremacy of the constitution, and the protection of fundamental rights, guiding the governance and operation of the Ethiopian state[cite: 7].

The following are the fundamental constitutional principles enshrined in the 1995 FDRE Constitution:[cite: 7]

• A. Popular Sovereignty (Article 8):[cite: 7]
  ◦ Popular sovereignty: The ultimate or supreme power of the people[cite: 7].
  ◦ This principle establishes that all governmental authority and power originate from the people themselves[cite: 7].
• B. Supremacy of the Constitution (Article 9):[cite: 7]
  ◦ Constitution: A fundamental document that contains the basic laws of a country[cite: 7].
  ◦ This principle dictates that the 1995 FDRE Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and all other laws, acts, and governmental decisions must be in line with its provisions[cite: 7].
• C. Respecting Human and Democratic Rights (Article 10):[cite: 7]
  ◦ Respect for human rights: The human rights of individuals or groups must be respected by both government and ordinary citizens[cite: 7].
  ◦ This principle guarantees the protection and respect of fundamental human and democratic rights for all citizens, ensuring they are upheld by both state and individuals[cite: 7].
• D. Secularism or Separation of State and Religion (Article 11):[cite: 7]
  ◦ This principle ensures that the state maintains neutrality regarding religious matters, preventing the entanglement of governmental affairs with religious institutions and beliefs[cite: 7].
• E. Transparency and Accountability of Government (Article 12):[cite: 7]
  ◦ Transparency: The condition in which the conducts and actions of government officials shall be open or transparent to the public[cite: 7].
  ◦ Accountability: Refers to the condition in which government officials are held responsible for any failure in their official duties[cite: 7].
  ◦ These principles ensure that government operations are open to public scrutiny and that officials are held responsible for their actions and failures[cite: 7].

Understanding Indigenous Knowledge[cite: 7]

This unit delves into the concept of indigenous knowledge, its diverse forms in Ethiopia, and its crucial role in fostering responsible citizens by contributing to peace-building, socio-economic development, and environmental conservation within communities[cite: 7].

1. The Concept of Indigenous Knowledge[cite: 7]
1.1. Definition[cite: 7]

• Indigenous knowledge: Knowledge that has been developed by a local people as a result of long histories of their social life and interaction with their environment[cite: 7].
• It is deeply rooted in the culture, traditions, social interactions, rituals, values, and belief systems of a particular community or society[cite: 7].

1.2. Characteristics and Variability[cite: 7]

• Community-Specific: Indigenous knowledge systems are not uniform; they are specific to a community and thus vary significantly from one society to another[cite: 7].

1.3. Richness in Ethiopia[cite: 7]

• Ethiopia is recognized as a country with rich indigenous knowledge systems across a wide array of areas, including:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Architecture[cite: 7]
  ◦ Pottery[cite: 7]
  ◦ Medicine (e.g., folk medicine)[cite: 7]
  ◦ Agriculture[cite: 7]
  ◦ Cottage industry[cite: 7]
  ◦ Conflict resolution[cite: 7]
  ◦ Environmental conservations[cite: 7]
• Example of Indigenous Conflict Resolution: When a husband and wife experience conflict, they often do not immediately take their case to a formal court[cite: 7]. Instead, they typically approach local elders to resolve their disagreement through a win-win approach[cite: 7]. This is a popular indigenous conflict resolution mechanism found in most parts of Ethiopia[cite: 7].

2. The Role of Indigenous Knowledge in Producing Responsible Citizens[cite: 7]
2.1. Defining a Responsible Citizen[cite: 7]

• Responsible citizen: A person who has the knowledge and commitment regarding his/her contributions for the betterment of his/her livelihood, the community, and the country[cite: 7].
• Role in Society: Responsible citizens are vital as change agents in ensuring sustainable peace and driving socio-economic transformations within their community and their country[cite: 7].

2.2. Significant Contributions of Indigenous Knowledge[cite: 7]

• Indigenous knowledges make significant contributions to producing responsible citizens who can play a crucial role in:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Peace-building[cite: 7]
  ◦ Socio-economic development[cite: 7]
  ◦ Health[cite: 7]
  ◦ Agriculture[cite: 7]
  ◦ Arts[cite: 7]
  ◦ Environmental conservations[cite: 7]

2.3. Indigenous Conflict Resolution Mechanisms and Peace Building[cite: 7]
2.3.1. Role in Peace Building[cite: 7]

• Indigenous conflict resolution mechanisms are instrumental in ensuring sustainable peace within a given community[cite: 7].
• They achieve this by providing peaceful resolutions to conflicts at a very low cost[cite: 7].
• The culture of resolving conflicts through indigenous means is widely practiced in virtually every community in Ethiopia[cite: 7].

2.3.2. Examples of Mechanisms[cite: 7]

• Some prominent examples of indigenous conflict resolution mechanisms in Ethiopia include:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Jaarsummaa (of the Oromo people)[cite: 7]
  ◦ Shimglina and Erq (of the Amhara people)[cite: 7]
  ◦ Yejoka (of the Gurage people)[cite: 7]
  ◦ Mada'a and Mablo (of the Afar people)[cite: 7]
  ◦ Gedana (of the Sidama people)[cite: 7]
  ◦ Guurt system (of the Somali people)[cite: 7]

2.3.3. Significance and the Role of Elders[cite: 7]

• Paramount Importance: In Ethiopia, indigenous conflict resolution mechanisms hold paramount significance compared to modern conflict resolution institutions (such as courts) in ensuring sustainable peace[cite: 7].
• Elders' Crucial Role: Elders "take the lion's share" in discharging the moral responsibility of safeguarding the political, social, cultural, economic, and religious life of their people through these mechanisms[cite: 7].
• Intergenerational Transfer: This important role is not exclusively for elders; young people actively inherit this tradition from their elders, ensuring its continuity as they become tomorrow's elders[cite: 7].

2.4. Making Active Participation in Socio-Economic Activities[cite: 7]
2.4.1. Citizen's Responsibility[cite: 7]

• Making active participation in the socio-economic activities of a society is a fundamental responsibility expected of citizens[cite: 7].

2.4.2. Indigenous Technologies for Transformation[cite: 7]

• One significant way citizens can contribute to the socio-economic transformations of their societies is by introducing and utilizing indigenous technologies[cite: 7].
• Importance for Ethiopia: Indigenous technologies and practices are particularly important for Ethiopia, a country that relies heavily on outside supplies[cite: 7].
• Benefits of Indigenous Technologies:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Enable citizens to use local products[cite: 7].
  ◦ Offer products at reasonable costs and with better accessibility compared to often pricey and scarce imported goods[cite: 7].
  ◦ Provide valuable options for local people, often more suitable to their context than Western technologies[cite: 7].

2.4.3. Examples of Indigenous Technologies and Responsible Citizenship[cite: 7]

• Various indigenous technologies have been continuously used for long periods in different parts of Ethiopia[cite: 7]. These include:[cite: 7]
  ◦ Pottery[cite: 7]
  ◦ Iron smithing[cite: 7]
  ◦ Jewelry making[cite: 7]
  ◦ Weaving[cite: 7]
  ◦ Wood carving[cite: 7]
  ◦ Leather works[cite: 7]
• Attributes of Responsible Citizens: The individuals engaged in these works demonstrate great dedication in maintaining these indigenous knowledges, often resisting deep-rooted negative societal attitudes towards their crafts[cite: 7]. This commitment clearly indicates their real determination, hard work, and valuing of their own indigenous wisdoms, which are true attributes of responsible or good citizens[cite: 7].

2.5. Indigenous Environmental Conservation Mechanisms[cite: 7]
2.5.1. Definition and Significance[cite: 7]

• Environmental conservation: Refers to the wise use of resources available in the natural environment[cite: 7].
• Global Priority: Environmental conservation and preservation constitute one of the most pressing contemporary global issues, demanding everyone's attention because the life of both human beings and animals depends on a well-conserved and preserved environment[cite: 7].

2.5.2. Role of Indigenous Knowledge[cite: 7]

• Indigenous knowledge plays a significant role in promoting environmental conservation[cite: 7].
• In Ethiopia, despite often not receiving sufficient attention, indigenous knowledge offers valuable contributions to environmental conservations within different communities[cite: 7].
• Example: The Konso people's indigenous knowledge of water and soil conservation techniques is a notable illustration[cite: 7].
