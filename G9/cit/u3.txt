1. Effective Communication Through Digital Media (Lesson 2.5)[cite: 6]

This section explores the importance of communication, how digital media enhances it, and the various ways technology has improved our ability to connect and exchange information effectively[cite: 6].

1.1. Understanding Communication[cite: 6]

• Communication: One of the most important ways to transfer and exchange messages and information in our relation with others[cite: 6].
• Purpose: It is the art and technique of informing and influencing individuals and large audiences about important issues using scientific and ethical methods[cite: 6].

1.2. The Role of Digital Media in Effective Communication[cite: 6]

  ◦ Online networks and databases[cite: 6]
  ◦ Emails[cite: 6]
  ◦ These platforms facilitate communication with the public[cite: 6].
• Improvements by Digital Technologies: The fastest-growing information communication technology (ICT) has significantly improved both business and personal communication[cite: 6].
  ◦ Cellular technology and satellite communication:[cite: 6]
    - Increased communication speeds[cite: 6].
    - Made 'connection' easier[cite: 6].
  ◦ Internet:[cite: 6]
    - Enabled easy exchange of information over long distances[cite: 6].
    - Allowed accessing web page servers in far-off countries[cite: 6].
  ◦ Computers:[cite: 6]
    - Enabled better storage and presentation of information[cite: 6].

  ◦ Mobile phones:[cite: 6]

• Scenario: Megarsa Tullu, a farmer in a rural village called Dimtu, utilizes his mobile phone[cite: 6].
• Information Access: He easily obtains updated information about the market prices of agricultural products[cite: 6].

1.3. Key Definitions Related to Digital Media[cite: 6]

• Digital culture: The whole of the lifestyle and habits created by the innovations brought by the age in which human beings live, with technology taking more place in daily life. It describes how technology and the Internet significantly shape the way we interact, behave, think, and communicate as human beings in a societal setting[cite: 6].
• Digital media: Platform sites or spaces of multidirectional, instantaneous communication that operate with the use of any of various encoded machine-readable data formats. It is any form of media that uses electronic devices for distribution via electronic devices[cite: 6].
• Digital technology: A type of technology that is used to transfer information to move data quickly using digitalized internet-based social network and media. It enables immense amounts of information to be compressed on small storage devices that can be easily preserved and transported[cite: 6].
• Digital world: The existing, emerging, and probable human world dominance of the use of technology in the modern day, being driven and transformed by technology and by digitized ICTs. This involves the application of digital technology and media through social networks and media platforms, enabling speedy interaction[cite: 6].
• Social media: A computer-based technology that facilitates the sharing of ideas, thoughts, and information through building virtual networks and communities. Social networks are types of social media that help for exchanging thoughts, ideas, and contents with other users-often with users who share tastes and interests[cite: 6].

2. Challenges of Using Digital Media (Lesson 2.6)[cite: 6]

While digital technology and social media networks have become integral and beneficial parts of our daily lives, they also present significant challenges and can be used for negative purposes. This section outlines these major drawbacks[cite: 6].

2.1. General Drawbacks of Social Media[cite: 6]

Social media platforms, such as Facebook, can have several negative impacts:[cite: 6]

• Decreasing academic performance[cite: 6].
• Negatively affecting performance in the workplace[cite: 6].
• Causing problems in personal relationships[cite: 6].

2.2. Major Challenges of Internet-Based Platforms[cite: 6]

The main challenges involved in using internet-based digital technology and social media platforms include:[cite: 6]

2.2.1. 1. Disregard for Ethical Values[cite: 6]

• Ethics in digital media: Involves the moral problems associated with the adoption of new communication technologies in daily activities[cite: 6].
• Major Concern: The effect of information transmitted through these channels[cite: 6].
• Challenge: The accuracy of information is often uncontrolled, which can cause various negative impacts on society[cite: 6].
• Solution: To use social media effectively, digital technology must be used ethically and appropriately[cite: 6].

2.2.2. 2. Fabrication of Fake News[cite: 6]

• Online Phenomenon: Fake news has become a prevalent online issue that is difficult to control[cite: 6].
• Spread: Sharing of fabricated news to shape public opinion is common[cite: 6].
• Visibility: Fake news can achieve high levels of visibility quickly due to its ease of sharing via social media[cite: 6].
• Content: It includes reports, images, and videos shared to purposefully spread misinformation (information that is factually incorrect)[cite: 6].
• Purpose: Primarily used to manipulate public opinion for:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Political gain[cite: 6].
  ◦ Commercial gain (e.g., attracting clicks to linked websites to generate advertising income)[cite: 6].
• Conclusion: Fake news and misinformation are common problems across all social media platforms[cite: 6].

2.2.3. 3. Personal Privacy and Security Issues[cite: 6]

• User Concern: Privacy and security are among the most challenging aspects of using social media, causing significant worry among users[cite: 6].
• Privacy Difficulty: It is very difficult to maintain privacy on social media because they are public platforms where anyone can easily access others' personal information[cite: 6].
• Risk: Protecting individual privacy and keeping information confidential is at risk in the age of digital technologies[cite: 6].
• Example: When individuals post or upload details about their private life on platforms like Facebook and YouTube, someone else can share it publicly, making it visible to anyone[cite: 6].
• Broader Impact: In the present day, security issues posed by digital media can also become challenges to state national security[cite: 6].

2.3. Key Takeaways on Digital Media Challenges[cite: 6]

• Digital technologies are indispensable tools for quick and fast communication[cite: 6].
• However, their use is hindered by various challenges[cite: 6].
• Digital and social media technologies can be used not only for beneficial purposes but also for detrimental ones[cite: 6].
• Inappropriate use of social media may lead to ethical violations, with a disregard for ethical values being one of the primary challenges[cite: 6].

3. Understanding Constitution (Lesson 3.1)[cite: 6]

This section defines what a constitution is, explores its various classifications based on form, amendment procedures, and governmental structure, and outlines its fundamental principles that guide a state's governance[cite: 6].

3.1. Definition of Constitution[cite: 6]

• Constitution: A fundamental document that contains the basic laws of a country. It is the most fundamental or supreme law of a state that underlines the existence of the state[cite: 6].
• Aggregate of Laws: It is an aggregate of laws, customs, conventions, principles, rules, and regulations by which a particular government implements its administrative activities[cite: 6].
• Defining Document: A constitution is a fundamental document that defines:[cite: 6]
  ◦ The nature of government and its functions[cite: 6].
  ◦ The rights and duties of citizens[cite: 6].

3.2. Classifications of Constitution[cite: 6]

Constitutions can be classified based on different criteria:[cite: 6]

3.2.1. A. Written and Unwritten Constitution[cite: 6]

• Written Constitution:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: Constitutions that exist in a single document or in a codified form; a collection of documents in which the fundamental rules regarding the nature of the government and the rights and duties of citizens are clearly laid down[cite: 6].
  ◦ Characteristics: Precise, definite, and systematic[cite: 6].
  ◦ Examples: Ethiopia, South Africa, Nigeria, India, United States of America, France, Brazil[cite: 6].
  ◦ Advantages:[cite: 6]
    - Easily accessible to citizens (e.g., from libraries, online, bookshops)[cite: 6].
    - Enables citizens to easily learn about their rights and duties by referring to the guaranteed rights[cite: 6].
  ◦ Disadvantages:[cite: 6]
    - Rigidity: Compared to unwritten constitutions, it is not as easy to improve in changing circumstances[cite: 6].
• Unwritten Constitution:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: A constitution that is not found in a single written document. Instead, it exists in terms of customs and conventions[cite: 6].
  ◦ Characteristics: Unsystematic, indefinite, and imprecise[cite: 6].
  ◦ Examples: United Kingdom and Israel[cite: 6].
  ◦ Advantages:[cite: 6]
    - Simple or easy to make amendments or change some constitutional provisions[cite: 6].
    - Suitable for improvement or amendment because it is undocumented or uncodified[cite: 6].
  ◦ Disadvantages:[cite: 6]
    - Not easily accessible to citizens[cite: 6].
    - Citizens do not have the chance to easily learn about their rights and duties from a constitutional document, unlike with a written constitution[cite: 6].

3.2.2. B. Rigid and Flexible Constitutions[cite: 6]

This classification is based on the amendment procedure (modifications, adding, or removing issues from the constitution)[cite: 6].

• Rigid Constitution:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: Cannot be amended in the same manner in which ordinary laws are passed, amended, or repealed. It requires special or difficult procedures for the amendment process[cite: 6].
  ◦ Examples: The constitutions of Ethiopia and USA[cite: 6].
• Flexible Constitution:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: A constitution which is simple for amendment. It can easily be amended by perhaps an ordinary legislative process or by an ordinary resolution[cite: 6].
  ◦ Process: It does not require special procedures for amendment[cite: 6].
  ◦ Examples: The Constitutions of Britain and Israel[cite: 6].

3.2.3. C. Federal and Unitary Constitutions[cite: 6]

• Federal Constitution:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: Distributes power between the federal and regional governments[cite: 6].
  ◦ Example: The 1995 FDRE constitution (Ethiopia)[cite: 6].
• Unitary Constitution:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: A constitution that centralizes all state power at the central government[cite: 6].
  ◦ Example: The Constitution of Britain[cite: 6].

3.3. Fundamental Principles of Constitution[cite: 6]

A constitution consists of certain fundamental principles that enable its realization and guide its implementation[cite: 6].

• Popular Sovereignty:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: Refers to the ultimate or supreme power of the people[cite: 6].
  ◦ The people are the ultimate source of power[cite: 6].
  ◦ The government must be based on the consent of the people[cite: 6].
• Limited Government:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: The power of government must be limited by the constitution[cite: 6].
  ◦ The government should act according to the power and functions explicitly given to it by the constitution[cite: 6].
• Separation of Powers:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: The constitution assigns specific responsibilities and powers to each branch of the government (legislative, executive, and judiciary organs)[cite: 6].
  ◦ Purpose: To establish a system of checks and balances among these branches[cite: 6].
• Rule of Law:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: Refers to everyone acting according to the rule of law[cite: 6].
  ◦ The law applies equally to everyone, including those in power[cite: 6].
• Respect for Human Rights:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Definition: The human rights of individuals or groups must be respected by both the government and ordinary citizens[cite: 6].
• Transparency and Accountability:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Transparency: Refers to the condition of making the conducts and actions of government officials open or transparent to the public[cite: 6].
  ◦ Accountability: Refers to the condition in which government officials are held responsible for any failure in their official duties[cite: 6].

3.4. Key Takeaways on Constitution[cite: 6]

• The constitution is the highest and fundamental law of a state[cite: 6].
• It is crucial for ensuring the rule of law and keeping the government within its limits[cite: 6].
• A country's constitution can exist in either written or unwritten forms[cite: 6].
• Constitutions are classified into written/unwritten, rigid/flexible, and federal/unitary types[cite: 6].
• A constitution is realized and made effective through the fundamental principles stated within it[cite: 6].

4. Understanding Constitutionalism (Lesson 3.2)[cite: 6]

This section defines constitutionalism as a system of limited government and highlights its critical importance in safeguarding human rights, preventing abuse of power, and ensuring the prevalence of the rule of law[cite: 6].

4.1. Definition of Constitutionalism[cite: 6]

• Constitutionalism: Refers to a government system that is based on and acts according to the supreme law[cite: 6].
• Limited Government: It implies a limited system of government by law[cite: 6].
• Realization of Principles: In a system of constitutionalism, there is an actual or real practice of:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Fundamental rights and freedoms of citizens[cite: 6].
  ◦ Good governance[cite: 6].
  ◦ Rule of law[cite: 6].
  ◦ Separation of powers[cite: 6].
• Government Conduct: The behavior and actions of the government, as well as its agents, should be founded on the fundamental principles of the constitution[cite: 6].

4.2. Importance of Constitutionalism[cite: 6]

The real practice of constitutionalism significantly contributes to peace, stability, and human well-being. Its major contributions include:[cite: 6]

• Realization of Human Rights of Citizens:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Human Rights: Are inviolable and inalienable rights[cite: 6].
  ◦ Safeguard: Constitutionalism prevents tyranny and guarantees the liberty and rights of individuals or groups[cite: 6].
  ◦ Responsibility: Respecting human rights and fundamental freedoms for all is the responsibility of every citizen and, in particular, the government[cite: 6].
  ◦ Enabling Factor: This can only become real when there is a government that acts according to the supreme law[cite: 6].
• Controlling the Abuse and Misuse of Powers of Government:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Checks and Balances: Constitutionalism checks whether the acts of a government are legitimate[cite: 6].
  ◦ Official Conduct: It ensures that officials conduct their public duties in accordance with laws[cite: 6].
  ◦ Protection: It provides institutional safeguards against the abuse and misuse of powers by authorities[cite: 6].
• Prevalence of Rule of Law:[cite: 6]
  ◦ Foundation: Constitutionalism is a fundamental foundation for the rule of law[cite: 6].
  ◦ Principle: It proclaims the necessity of the rule of law in contrast to rule by arbitrary judgment[cite: 6].
  ◦ Citizen Obligation: Everyone must be a law-abiding citizen, accepting the limits on their freedom[cite: 6].
  ◦ Official Obligation: Officials must also observe the limits on their power[cite: 6].
