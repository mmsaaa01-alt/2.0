2.1.1 Ancient Egypt
Timeline and Geography

    Beginning: The Civilization of Ancient Egypt began around 3000 BC.
    Location: It flourished in the Nile Valley.
    Unification: Upper and Lower Egypt were unified by Menes around 3200 BC, according to tradition.

Economy

    Agriculture: Was the basis of the Egyptian economy.
        The silt from the Nile River replenished soil fertility, enabling large crop production.
        Crops: The Egyptian peasantry produced wheat and barley.
    Livestock: Formed an integral part of the economy.
    Trade: Was another important economic activity within the empire.

Government and Religion

    Political Structure: A large state ruled by an absolute king.
    Pharaoh: The title for the absolute king, who ruled Egypt.
    Administration: The king ruled through officials called Viziers.
        Viziers: Acted as mayors, tax collectors, and judges.
    Divine Rulership:
        Pharaohs were regarded as both kings and gods.
        Believed to be an incarnated god and would become a god after death, watching over Egypt's welfare.
    Religion: The Egyptians were polytheists.
        Polytheists: Believed in and worshipped many gods.
    Afterlife Beliefs: Ancient Egyptians believed in life after death.
    Mummification: The process of preserving the body for the afterlife.
        Mummification: The process of preserving a dead body to prevent decay, practiced by ancient Egyptians in belief of life after death.
        Developed into a skilled art, leading to advancements in medicine.

Society and Labor

    Social Hierarchy: Egyptian society was structured hierarchically.
    Peasantry: Undertook irrigational activities.
        They also constructed palaces, massive temples, and tombs for the Pharaohs.

Major Achievements

    Pyramids: Massive tombs constructed for the Pharaohs.
        Pyramid: A monumental structure, typically made of stone, with a square base and four sloping triangular sides meeting at a point, built by ancient Egyptians as tombs for pharaohs and noblemen.
    Writing System:
        Hieroglyphics: The art of writing developed around 3000 BC.
        Hieroglyphics: An ancient Egyptian writing system that combined pictograms (pictures representing objects), ideograms (pictures representing ideas), phonograms (pictures representing sounds), and determinatives (symbols clarifying meaning).
        It was a combination of pictograms, ideograms, phonograms, and determinatives.
    Papyrus: Introduced by Egyptians as a paper-like substance.
        Papyrus: A material similar to thick paper, made from the pith of the papyrus plant, used in ancient Egypt for writing or drawing.
    Medicine: Advanced as a result of the development of mummification techniques.

    Key Takeaway: Ancient Egypt, a powerful civilization rooted in the fertile Nile Valley, was characterized by its divine pharaohs, a polytheistic belief system centered on the afterlife, a hierarchical society, and significant achievements in monumental architecture (pyramids), writing (hieroglyphics), and early medical practices.

2.1.2 Nubia (Kingdom of Kush)
Timeline and Geography

    Location: A kingdom along the Nile River, in what is now northeastern Sudan.
    Existence: Existed as early as 2000 BC and lasted until about 350 AD.

Economy

    Agriculture: An important basis for social differentiation and stratification.
        Nubians cultivated cereals, vegetables, and dates fruit.
    Pastoralism: Formed another important base of the subsistence economy.
    Trade: Another important source of wealth and power for Nubian states.

Government and Industry

    Rulership: Absolute kings and queens ruled the Nubian states.
        Nubian kings and queens were considered divine rulers, similar to Egyptian pharaohs.
    Metal Technology: Nubia was renowned for its metal technology.
        Meroe: An important center for metal products such as gold, copper, silver, and iron.
        This indicates that Meroe had a flourishing iron industry.

Interactions and Decline

    Conquest by Egypt: At different times, Egypt conquered and ruled lower Nubia.
    Kushite Conquest of Egypt: The Kushites conquered Egypt around the eighth century BC.
    Destruction of Meroe: Around 350 AD, the Aksumite king, Ezana, invaded and destroyed Meroe.

    Key Takeaway: Nubia, also known as the Kingdom of Kush, was a long-lasting civilization along the Nile that shared political and cultural influences with Egypt. It developed a strong economy based on agriculture, pastoralism, and trade, and was particularly distinguished by its advanced metal technology and a flourishing iron industry centered in Meroe.

2.2.1 Mesopotamia
Geography and Origin

    Location: Included areas now eastern Syria, Southern Turkey, and most parts of Iraq.
    Sumerian Civilization: The earliest of the great river valley civilizations, found between the rivers Tigris and Euphrates.

Urbanization and Architecture

    First Cities: The Sumerians began to build the first cities in the world.
    Building Materials: Sumerians used mud bricks dried in the sun in their buildings.
    Ziggurats: Large temples made of mud bricks, known as stepped towers.
        Ziggurats: Massive stepped towers built in ancient Mesopotamia, typically topped with a temple, serving as religious and administrative centers.

Economy and Governance

    Fertile Soil: The soil of southern Mesopotamia was very fertile.
    Agriculture: With flood control and irrigation, large crops were possible.
    Divine Authority of Kings: Sumerian kings had divine authority. A king ruled as a representative of his city's major god.
    Religion: The Sumerians were Polytheists (worshipped many gods).

Innovations and Achievements

    Agricultural Tools: The plough first appeared in Mesopotamia.
    Potter's Wheel: The potter's wheel first appeared in Mesopotamia.
    Metallurgy: The Sumerians may have been the first to use bronze.
    Writing System:
        Developed around 3200 BC.
        Cuneiform: Their writing system.
        Cuneiform: An ancient Mesopotamian writing system, developed by the Sumerians, characterized by wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay tablets; it was a complex system of ideographs, not an alphabet.
        It was not an alphabet system, but a highly complex system of ideographs.
    Other Advancements: They also developed mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and metal works.

    Key Takeaway: Mesopotamia, specifically the Sumerian civilization between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, pioneered urban development, agriculture through irrigation, and crucial technological innovations such as the plough, potter's wheel, and bronze. It is most famous for developing the world's first writing system, cuneiform, and making advancements in mathematics, astronomy, and medicine.

2.2.2 Persia
Geography and Naming

    Location: A land that included parts of the area now called Iran and Afghanistan.
    Origin of Name: Persians called the region the "Land of Aryans," from which the name Iran is coined.
    Language: They called their language Aryan.

The Persian Empire

    Dynasty: Established a dynasty known as the Persian Empire.
    Size: Became the largest empire in the ancient world.
    Founder: Founded by Cyrus the Great around 550 BC.
    Extent: Stretched from Europe's Balkan Peninsula in the West to India's Indus Valley in the East, and included parts of North Africa.

Contributions and Religion

    Government: Made important contributions to government and law.
    Infrastructure: Built many new roads and developed the world's first postal service.
    Religion: Their religion was known as Zoroastrianism.
        Zoroastrianism: An ancient monotheistic religion founded by the prophet Zoroaster in Persia, emphasizing a dualistic cosmology of good and evil.
        Founded by a prophet called Zoroaster.

    Key Takeaway: The Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great, was an immense ancient power stretching across vast territories. It was notable for its administrative innovations, including an extensive road network and the first postal service, and for the establishment of Zoroastrianism as its principal religion.

Activities

    In small groups, discuss the contribution of the ancient Mesopotamian Civilization.

2.2.3 India
Geography and Timeline

    Origin: The first Indian Civilization began in the Indus Valley in Pakistan and Western India.
    Timeline: Began around 2500 BC and reached its apex around 1500 BC.

Systems and Economy

    Advanced Systems: The people had their own systems for counting, measuring, weighing, and writing.
    Agriculture: Dug ditches and canals around cities to irrigate their farms.
    Trade: Traded with one another and with other civilizations like Mesopotamia.

Craftsmanship and Society

    Artisans: Indus artisans used copper and bronze to make tools, mirrors, pots, and pans.
    Ornaments: Crafted gold ornaments.
    Social Hierarchy: Ancient Indian society was hierarchically divided based on work and birth, a system dating back some 3,000 years.

Religions

    Hinduism: Developed from the beliefs of the early Indo-Aryans.
    Buddhism: Emerged from Hinduism as a reform movement.
        Buddhism: A religion and dharma that originated in ancient India, founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha), which teaches a path to enlightenment through spiritual development and ethical conduct.
        Spread outside of India to Southeast Asia, China, and Japan.

    Key Takeaway: The ancient Indian Civilization, centered in the Indus Valley, was characterized by sophisticated systems for measurement and writing, advanced agricultural practices, and extensive trade. It also saw the development of deeply influential religious traditions, including Hinduism and Buddhism.

2.2.4 China
Geography and Timeline

    Origin: The Civilization of ancient China first developed in the Yellow River Region of Northern China in the third millennium BC.
    Expansion: Expanded over time to present-day China's central and southern parts.
    Height: Reached its height about 3000 BC in the Valley of Hwang Ho.

Dynasties and Achievements

    Shang Dynasty (c. 1700-1122 BC): The first dynasty in China.
        Achievements: Creation of bronze vessels, war chariots, and a writing system.
    Zhou Dynasty (c. 1122-256 BC): Overthrew the Shang dynasty and established its own rule.
        Achievements: Chinese literary classics developed, and Chinese philosophy emerged.

Philosophy and Social Order

    Confucius (551-479 BC): The most influential and respected philosopher in Chinese history.
    Confucianism: His teachings.
        Confucianism: A comprehensive philosophical system developed by Confucius in ancient China, emphasizing morality, ethics, social harmony, respect for authority (parents over children, men over women, educated over ordinary people), and the importance of a well-ordered society.
        Teaches the importance of a well-ordered society where parents rule their children, men lead women, and the educated judge ordinary people.

Major Constructions

    Great Wall of China: Constructed to keep out invaders from central Asia.
        Length: Extends about 7,400 Kilometers across southern China.

Writing System

    Logographic: The Chinese developed their system of writing.
        Logographic: A writing system where pictorial symbols represent entire words or morphemes of a language, rather than individual sounds.
        Meant the use of pictorial symbols to represent words of the Chinese language.

    Key Takeaway: Ancient China, originating in the Yellow River region, developed through powerful dynasties like the Shang and Zhou, which introduced bronze metallurgy, sophisticated warfare (chariots), and foundational philosophical thought like Confucianism. It is also renowned for its unique logographic writing system and the monumental construction of the Great Wall.

2.3.1 Maya
People and Timeline

    Identity: Native American people.
    Location: Developed a magnificent civilization in the western hemisphere.
    Peak Period: Civilization was at its peak from about 250-900 AD.

Achievements

    Arts: Produced remarkable architecture, painting, pottery, and sculpture.
    Science: Made outstanding achievements in astronomy and mathematics.
    Calendar: Developed an accurate yearly calendar.
    Writing: Developed an advanced form of writing.

Religion and Architecture

    Religion: Worshipped many gods and goddesses.
    Architecture: Built tall pyramids of limestone with small temples on top.

Decline and Conquest

    Spanish Conquest: The Spanish captured the southern part of the area in the 16th Century.
    Complete Domination: Spanish domination of the entire Maya region was completed in the late 17th Century.

    Key Takeaway: The Maya civilization in the Western Hemisphere, flourishing between 250-900 AD, was highly advanced, known for its exceptional achievements in architecture (limestone pyramids), arts, astronomy, mathematics, a precise calendar, and a sophisticated writing system. Their civilization eventually fell under Spanish colonial rule.

2.3.2 Inca
People and Empire

    Identity: South American native people.
    Empire Expansion: Began to expand around 1438.
    Territory: Ruled one of the largest and richest empires in the Americas, including present-day Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina.
    Capital: Cusco, located in southern Peru.

Skills and Crafts

    Engineering: Skilled in engineering, for example, they built a network of roads.
    Craftsmanship:
        Made fine artifacts from gold, silver, and other materials.
        Wove fine cotton and woollen cloth.

Conquest

    Spanish Conquest: Spanish forces conquered the Inca Empire after 1532.

    Key Takeaway: The Inca, a powerful South American native people, established one of the largest and wealthiest empires in the Americas. They were renowned for their advanced engineering skills, particularly their extensive network of roads, and their exquisite craftsmanship in metal and textiles, before their conquest by Spanish forces.

2.3.3 Aztecs
People and Empire

    Identity: Another Native American people.
    Location: Ruled a powerful empire in Mexico during the 15th and 16th centuries.
    Center: The center of the Aztec Civilization was the valley of Mexico.
    Capital: Tenochtitlan, the largest city, which stood on the site of present-day Mexico City.

Economy and Religion

    Economy: Agriculture formed the basis of the Aztec economy.
    Religion: Worshipped hundreds of gods and goddesses.

Writing and Conquest

    Writing: Used a form of writing called pictographic writing.
    Destruction: Their empire was destroyed by the Spaniards in 1521.

    Key Takeaway: The Aztec Empire in Mexico, flourishing in the 15th and 16th centuries, was a significant Native American civilization with its capital at Tenochtitlan. Its economy was based on agriculture, its religion involved the worship of many deities, and it utilized pictographic writing, until its destruction by the Spaniards in 1521.

2.4.1 Ancient Greek Civilization
Origin and Political Structure

    Emergence: Ancient Greece emerged about 2500 years ago.
    Significance: It was the birthplace of Western Civilization.
    City-States (Polis): Greek culture developed chiefly in small city-states called Polis.
        Polis: A Greek city-state, consisting of a city or town and its surrounding villages and farmland, which functioned as an independent political and cultural unit.
        Components: A city-state consists of a city or town and the surrounding villages and farmland.
        Key Examples: The best-known city-states were Athens and Sparta.
        Unity: The city-states never became united into a nation.
    Commonalities: However, they all had a common language, religion, and culture.
        Greeks called themselves Hellens and their land Hellas.

Outstanding Achievements (Classical Period)

    Architecture: Outstanding, with the temple of Athena in Athens (the Parthenon) as the finest example.
    Literature and Drama: The Greeks invented drama (theatre).
    Sculptures: Made statues of gods and heroes in marble and bronze.
    History Writing: The two great Greek historians were Herodotus and Thucydides.
        Herodotus: Wrote about countries and civilizations outside Greece and is a primary source for the Persian Wars.
        Thucydides: Wrote a good account of the Peloponnesian War, noted for its lack of bias despite his Athenian origin.
    Medicine: Hippocrates began the tradition of modern medicine in Europe.
        Identified symptoms, classified diseases, and suggested sensible remedies (e.g., fresh air and a good diet).
    Vocabulary: Words like "Democracy," "Psychology," "Olympic," and "Marathon" all have Greek origin.
        Democracy: A system of government in which power is vested in the people, who rule either directly or through freely elected representatives; a concept originating in ancient Greece.
    Philosophy: The two greatest philosophers of ancient Greece, who taught in Athens, were Plato and later Aristotle.
        Bertrand Russell stated: "Plato and Aristotle were the most influential of all philosophers, ancient, medieval or modern."

    Key Takeaway: Ancient Greek Civilization, the birthplace of Western thought, was characterized by independent city-states (polis) like Athens and Sparta, sharing a common language and culture. Its Classical Period yielded unparalleled contributions to architecture, drama, sculpture, history writing, medicine, and philosophy, profoundly influencing subsequent human civilization.

2.4.2 Ancient Roman Civilization
Origin and Influence

    Origin: Started in present-day Italy around 753 BC on the hills above the River Tiber.
    Influence: Had a significant influence on the development of western Civilization.

Major Contributions and Legacy

    Law: Roman law became the foundation for the legal systems of most countries in Western Europe and Latin America.
    Engineering: Roman roads, bridges, and aqueducts served as a model for engineering.
    Architecture: Ancient Romans built public baths and amphitheatres (outdoor arenas).
        Coliseum: The most famous amphitheater in the city of Rome.

Political Evolution

    Early Monarchy: A series of kings ruled ancient Rome at the beginning.
    Roman Republic (509 BC - 27 BC):
        Established after Roman nobles overthrew the king.
        Government: Headed by two elected officials called Consuls.
            Consuls: Two elected chief magistrates who headed the government of the Roman Republic, serving for only one year terms.
        Senate: The most powerful government body, whose senators served for life.
        Duration: Lasted nearly 500 years.
        Nature: Was a democratic society.
        State of War: Was in an almost constant state of war.
    Roman Empire (after 27 BC - 476 AD):
        Established after the Republic was destroyed by twenty years of civil war.
        Rulership: Run only by one man.
        Peace: The first 200 years were relatively peaceful.
        Fall: Lasted until Rome fell in 476 AD.

Expansion

    Aggressive Wars: Rome expanded beyond Italy through aggressive wars.
    Punic Wars (264-146 BC): The bloodiest wars, fought repetitively against Carthage.
    Empire Extent: Extended its empire step-by-step all around the Mediterranean Sea and beyond (Gaul, modern France, Britain, Belgium, and the Netherlands).

Key Differences: Republic vs. Empire

    Republic: Democratic society, almost constant state of war.
    Empire: Run by one man, first 200 years relatively peaceful.

Enduring Legacy

    Latin Language: Developed into the "Latin languages" of Europe and Latin America.
    Latin Literature: Authors like Cicero, Caesar (wrote history of his wars), and poets Vergil, Horace, and others.
    Roman Law: Was revived in the European Middle Ages.
    Julian Calendar: Introduced by Julius Caesar, it is the base for the current Gregorian calendar.

    Key Takeaway: Ancient Roman Civilization, originating in Italy, profoundly influenced Western society through its advancements in law, engineering, and architecture. It evolved from a Republic governed by Consuls and a powerful Senate to a vast Empire, leaving an enduring legacy in language (Latin), literature, legal systems, and the calendar.

2.5 The Rise and Spread of Christianity

    This section chronicles the origin and dissemination of Christianity, detailing its initial persecution under Roman rule, its eventual adoption as the official religion of the Roman Empire, and the subsequent divisions that shaped its diverse denominations.

Lesson Learning Outcomes

At the end of this lesson, learners will be able to:

    Describe the origin of Christianity.
    Explain the spread of Christianity into different parts of the world.

Origin and Early Persecution

    Origin: Christianity began in Palestine in the first century AD.
    Basis: It was a religion based on the teaching of Jesus Christ.
    Birthplace: Jesus was born in Bethlehem, in the Judea part of Palestine, under the Roman Empire.
    Roman View: The Roman government considered Christians as a threat against the unity of the Empire.
    Persecution: The Roman government began persecuting Christians during the reign of Nero (AD 54-68).

Shift to Toleration and Official Religion

    Emperor Constantine: Became a Christian in 312 AD and made Christianity the favored religion of the Roman Empire, which accelerated its spread.
    Edict of Milan (313 AD): Emperor Constantine ordered religious toleration.
        Christians gained freedom from persecution.
    Official Roman Religion (392 AD): Under Theodosius the Great, who ruled from 378 to 395, the Romans adopted Christianity as their official religion.

Impact and Church Divisions

    Unifying Force: Christianity replaced the Roman Empire as a unifying force in Western Europe during the Middle Ages.
    Early Competition: During the early Christian era, Rome and Constantinople competed for religious power.
        The Pope or Bishop of Constantinople asserted authority over Church affairs in the East.
        They had conflicting views on Church language and rituals.
    Great Schism (1054): These disagreements finally created a split in the Christian Church.
        The Church split into two distinct parts: the Roman Catholic Church at Rome and the Eastern Orthodox Church at Constantinople.
        Both retained separate identities today.
    Reformation (1517): A Reformation movement began.
        Reformation: A 16th-century movement in Western Europe that challenged the doctrines and practices of the Roman Catholic Church, leading to the establishment of Protestantism.
        Western Christianity was divided into the Roman Catholic Church and Protestantism.
        Monotheism: The belief in the existence of only one God. (Relevant to Christian belief, as opposed to polytheistic Roman beliefs)

    Key Takeaway: Christianity originated in Palestine in the 1st century AD, initially facing severe persecution from the Roman Empire. However, through Emperor Constantine's conversion and the Edict of Milan, it gained toleration and eventually became the official religion of the Roman Empire under Theodosius. This led to its widespread adoption and later significant divisions, resulting in the formation of the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, and Protestantism.

Activities

    Explain the term monotheism.
    Discuss the major reasons for the spread of Christianity.

Unit Summary

This unit showed that Egypt had a long civilization dating from the early 4th millennium BC. The Civilization was divided into Pre-dynastic and Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms, during which successive Egyptian pharaohs, supported by civilian officials, ruled the kingdom. The Egyptians were polytheists; the pharaohs were themselves considered gods. The Egyptians made important contributions in the areas of building technology and writing.

Nubia was also a vital kingdom ruled by powerful kings and queens. Like Egypt, its economy was dependent on agriculture, pastoralism, and trade. In addition, Egypt and Nubia had influenced each other politically and culturally. Besides its building technology, Nubia was famous for its iron industry.

Like Egypt, Sumerian Civilization developed in the fertile regions between the Euphrates and Tigris. Unlike Egypt, however, Sumerian Civilization was a Civilization of warring city-states rather than one unified kingdom. The Sumerians invented the plough, a writing system (cuneiform), and the wheel. They were also skilled builders of temples using mud bricks (Ziggurats).

The unit also added to your knowledge of ancient Greece by learning about the wars of Greece against Persia, the Peloponnesian War, and Greek culture in the classical and the Hellenistic Age. Many provincials, but not the Jews of Palestine, felt loyalty to Rome, and later, all free males became Roman citizens. Finally, the unit explained how Christianity rose, and its spread, especially after Constantine made it the Roman Empire's official religion.
