Unit 6: Africa and the Outside World 1500-1880s

    This unit explores the evolving relationship between Africa and the outside world from 1500 to the 1880s. It covers early European contacts, the profound impact of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade, the subsequent shift to "legitimate" trade in raw materials, the establishment of white settlements in South Africa, and the role of European explorers and missionaries on the continent.

6.1 Contact with the Outside World
Early Portuguese Engagement with West Africa

    Pioneering Role: Portugal was the first European country to establish significant early contacts with the coastal regions of West Africa.
    Distant Objective: Reaching India by sea.
    Immediate Goal: To bypass Muslim North Africa and gain direct access to the gold-producing region of West Africa.
        This direct access provided Portugal with a major source of national wealth.
        The accumulated wealth would then fund further exploration and discoveries.

Henry the Navigator and Portuguese Exploration

    Sponsor: Henry the Navigator, a Portuguese prince.
    Main Drivers (Motives):
        Spread of Christianity: A desire to evangelize and establish Africa as a bulwark against the Ottoman Empire.
        Commercial Links: To create direct trade relationships with African states.
        Financial Advantage: To use African gold to finance travel along new trade routes.
        Trade Route Security: Securing safe passage through Africa would open the entire Indian Ocean to direct Portuguese trade.

Key Portuguese Expansion and Settlements

    Ceuta (1415):
        First Step: The initial move in Portuguese expansion across the Atlantic coast.
        Expedition: Organized by King João I (John I) and his sons.
        Location: Across the Straits of Gibraltar, on the coast of North Africa (modern-day Morocco).
        Duration: The Portuguese occupied Ceuta from 1415 to 1769, a period marked by constant war due to local resistance.
    Further Exploration Southward:
        1432: Portuguese navigators reached Cape Bajador, southwest of Ceuta on the Atlantic coast of Morocco.
        1441: Explorer Nuno Tristao reached Cape Blanc, further south along the Atlantic coast.
    Senegambia:
        Arrival: Portuguese reached the shores of Sierra Leone in 1460.
        Decline: Portuguese influence as world exploration leaders began to wane in the 16th century.
        Trade Impact: Senegambia was one of the earliest West African regions affected by European trade.
        Slave Trade: The Senegambia state provided enslaved people for European purchase for approximately a century, supplying perhaps a third of all enslaved Africans exported during the 16th century.
    Gold Coast (Present-day Ghana):
        Encounter: During their exploration of the West African coast, the Portuguese encountered various African kingdoms.
        Resources: Some of these kingdoms controlled significant deposits of gold, salt, and enslaved people.
        Castle of Elmina (1482):
            First Permanent Trading Post: Built by the Portuguese on the western coast of present-day Ghana.
            Purpose: To protect Portuguese trade interests from European competitors and hostile African groups.
            Lasting Presence: The fortress still stands today.
        Impact of Contact:
            Intensive European contact led to the importation and spread of American crops, notably maize and cassava.
            The success of these crops is believed to have contributed to population growth in West and Central Africa during the 16th and 17th centuries.

European Competition and Portuguese Decline

    Emergence of Rivals: During the 17th and 18th centuries, other European powers like the Dutch, English, and French joined the competition for overseas trade.
    Principal Early Struggle: Between the Dutch and the Portuguese.
    Loss of Elmina (1642): The Portuguese permanently lost their key trading post, the Castle of Elmina, to the Dutch. This marked the end of Portuguese presence on the Gold Coast.

6.2 Slavery
Historical Context of Slavery

    Ancient Practice: Traffic in enslaved people was not new and had a long history in Africa, Europe, and Asia.
    Fifteenth Century: Slavery continued at a fairly steady level.
    Primary Market: Southwest Asia, where most enslaved people were used as domestic servants.
    Pre-Trans-Atlantic Examples:
        Ancient Pharaonic Times: Captives from Nubia were transported down the Nile to Egypt.
        Roman Times: Some were transported across the Sahara to North Africa.
        Northeast Africa: Others were sent from this region to work in the Persian Gulf and Indian states.
    Scale: The numbers involved in these earlier forms of slavery were relatively small compared to the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.

Nature of Slavery

    Manifestations: Slavery often appeared in the lives of individuals categorized as servants, serfs, apprentices, or slaves.
    Social Group: In ancient civilizations of Europe, Asia, and Africa, persons defined as slaves constituted distinct social groups whose lives were routinely controlled by others.
    Economic Role: The expansion of long-distance trade and the rise of early kingdoms (like Mali and Songhai) promoted the use of slave labor in agriculture.
        Example: In the West African state of Benin, large numbers of slaves were employed in agriculture.
        Symbol of Wealth: These slaves, considered private property, symbolized wealth and prosperity for their owners.
    Local Politics and Economics: The expansion of the slave market before the Trans-Atlantic trade was influenced by both local politics and economics.
    Trans-Saharan Slave Trade: Long before the Trans-Atlantic trade, enslaved Africans were sold across the Sahara and transported to the Mediterranean via land and sea routes to Asia Minor.

6.3 The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade
Origins and Causes

    Discovery of America (1492): This event set the stage for the Atlantic slave trade.
    Labor Demand in America: Europeans established large plantations and minefields in America, creating a significant demand for laborers.
    Failure of Native American Labor:
        Europeans initially enslaved Native American Indians (called "Red Indians").
        Most Native Americans died from diseases imported from Europe and harsh treatment.
    Failure of White European Slave Labor:
        The Portuguese and Spaniards attempted to transport white slaves from Europe.
        However, these white enslaved people were unable to endure the hardships of the tropical climate.
    Shift to African Labor: From the 15th century to the early 19th century AD, Europeans began transporting Black people from West Africa as slaves.

The "Triangular Trade"

    Definition: The Trans-Atlantic slave trade is also known as the triangular trade because it connected three continents: Africa, America (including the Caribbean), and Europe.
    Flow of Goods and People:
        Europe to Africa: Manufactured goods such as cotton, alcoholic drinks, firearms, and metal wares were taken from Western Europe to Africa.
        Africa to Americas: These European goods were exchanged for enslaved Africans. The enslaved Africans were then transported across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas.
        Americas to Europe: In the Americas, the enslaved people were sold to plantation owners. With their labor, raw materials like sugarcane, tobacco, and coffee were produced. These raw materials were then exported back to Europe and sold at high prices.

Phases of the Atlantic Slave Trade

    Piratic Slave Trade (15th century - 1580s):
        Nature: Conducted by individual merchants, adventurers, navigators, or common sea robbers.
    Monopolistic Slave Trade (Begins 1580s):
        Nature: Trade was controlled by monopolistic slave-trading companies.
        Support: These companies received official government support and were often backed by armed forces.
        Expansion: The "slave-hunting ground" was widened, the volume of trade increased significantly, and companies accumulated huge profits.
        Note: The entire system was called "Triangular Trade," but this phase describes the shift to corporate control.
    Free Trade (Begins 1690s):
        Nature: Several individual traders re-entered the slave trade.
        Competition: This led to fierce competition between individual traders and the established monopolistic companies.

Circumstances in West Africa Facilitating the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade

The Trans-Atlantic slave trade began in West Africa due to three important circumstances:

    Fragmentation of Political States: The breakdown of large, indigenous political states such as Ghana, Mali, and Songhai (particularly those with large armies).
    Growing Importance as Slave Source: The region's increasing significance as a source of enslaved people for caravan traffic across the Sahara into the Mediterranean and Levant.
    Coastal Conflicts: Endemic conflict between the small coastal states in the region and their willingness to cooperate with European traders, exchanging enslaved people for arms.

Impacts of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade

    On Africa:
        Economic:
            Undermined African craft technology and agriculture.
            Reallocated labor away from agriculture and industrial work.
            Exported Africa's productive workforce (youngsters) to America and the Caribbean.
        Political:
            Threw Black Africa into confusion and insecurity.
            Discouraged political development and encouraged violence.
            Constant wars destroyed African states.
        Social:
            Undermined the morality and dignity of Africans.
            Caused immense human suffering and horrors.
            Enslaved people were treated as property, not human beings.
            Depopulated Africa of its productive young men and women.
            Had tragic effects on the lives of individual victims and their families.
            Historical Records: Indicate that more than 12 million native Africans were enslaved and deported from the continent.
    On Western Europe:
        Helped Western European nations accumulate vast wealth.
        This accumulated wealth contributed significantly to the industrialization of Western Europe in the 19th century.

6.4 The "Legitimate" Trade
Transition from Slave Trade

    Dominance of Slave Trade: For two or three centuries, the slave trade controlled commerce along the West African coast, disrupting and distorting African developments.
    Disruption: Local agricultural production was often disrupted by violence and warfare associated with the slave trade.
    Abolition: As Europeans gradually outlawed the slave trade, they sought new forms of commerce in West Africa, leading to "legitimate commerce."

Definition and Nature

    Legitimate Trade: Refers to the trade in commodities between Africans and European merchants, where African raw materials, especially cash crops, were exchanged for European manufactured goods.
    Period: Conducted after the abolition of the slave trade in Africa.
    System: A "free trading system" emerged where Africans produced goods, which were then traded by foreign merchants.

Key Commodities and Demand

    West African Commodities (first half of 19th century): Supplanted the export of captives.
        Included: Palm produce, rubber, cotton, skin, cocoa, among others.
        Examples:
            Gum Arabic from Senegal.
            Groundnuts from Guinea.
            Palm oil from most coastal forest zones.
    Demand in Europe: These products were in high demand primarily due to the Industrial Revolution in Europe.

Consequences of "Legitimate" Trade for Africa

    Economic Weakness: The development of West Africa's export trade in raw materials did not provide African states with real opportunities to develop their economic strength and independence.
    Exploitation: It led to the intensive exploitation of African natural resources, such as minerals and crops.
    Colonialism: It stimulated the colonialism of the African continent.
    Unequal System: The spread of Christianity religion (as part of European influence) contributed to an unequal trading system.

6.5 The White Settlement in South Africa
The Boers Settlement

    Dutch Trading Company (1652): Established a small permanent settlement on the southern shores of South Africa.
        Purpose: To regulate trade with the Khoisan people and provide fresh fruit, vegetables, and a hospital for their ships and sick sailors. This paved the way for white settlement.
    Boers (Farmers):
        1657: Van Riebeeck, the company commander, released some soldiers from their contracts, allowing them to establish their own businesses as farmers.
        Labor Source: The Boers were provided with enslaved West Africans.
    Khoi-Dutch Wars:
        First War (1659): Directly resulted from the Boers settling on Khoisan grazing land. The Khoisan united and successfully pushed the Boers back to the coast.
        Second War (1673-77): The natives were unable to defeat the Dutch.
    Consequences for the Khoisan:
        Retreat: The Khoisan withdrew into the interior, beyond the boundaries of the Boer colony. Some groups maintained independence by moving to areas north and south of the Orange River.
        Loss of Independence: Many lost their economic and political independence to the Boers.
        Collaboration: They agreed to work for the Boers as herdsmen and hunters, retaining some access to land.
        Labor Source: Orphaned Khoisan children became an additional source of labor for the Boers.
        Cultural Assimilation: They lost material resources, political rights, language, and other aspects of their cultural identity. They began to adopt the Dutch language and culture, even serving as Boer commanders against their own people and the Xhosa in some cases.

Conflicts with the Xhosa

    Initial Contacts (early 18th century): Minor conflicts occurred between the Xhosa and the Trek Boers (settlers moving inland).
    Escalation (1770s): Actual violence began when settlers moved into the fertile grazing lands of Zuurveld.
    Xhosa as Formidable Opposition: The Xhosa had a larger population than the Khoisan and a closely-knit social organization, making them a significant challenge to the colonists.
    Frontier Wars (1779-early 19th century): An estimated nine frontier wars took place over a century.
        The Xhosa, with a strong warrior tradition, held onto most of Zuurveld until the early 19th century.

British Occupation of the Cape Colony and the Great Trek

    British Seizure (1795): The British government seized the Cape Colony from the Dutch settlers.
        Dutch Motive: Establishing a fresh station for their crew.
        British Motive: Strategic, to prevent it from falling into French hands.
    Impact on Boers: The British occupation led to a great movement of Dutch-speaking colonists and Boers northwards in the 1830s and 1840s.
    British Expansion and Policy:
        1818-19: The British cleared the Xhosa from the Zuurveld area, pushing them beyond the Keiskama River.
        1829: The strength of white settlers in the conquered Xhosa area grew with the settlement of 500 British immigrants.
        Sixth War (1834-35): The British pushed the Xhosa west of the Kei River.
        Controversial Policies: To the disgust of the Boers, the British:
            Handed back the territory between the Keiskama and Kei Rivers to the Xhosa.
            Abolished slavery in its Cape Colony.
        Boer Irritation: The Boers, who relied on slave labor, were angered by British policies that they perceived as favoring the natives.
    The Great Trek (1835):
        Movement: Several thousand Boer families and their servants trekked northwards from the Eastern Cape.
        Reasons:
            A reaction to the law that granted freedom to native Africans and "coloured" people.
            A sign of opposition to the new ideas of equality between black and white populations.

6.6 European Explorers and Missionaries
European Ignorance and Renewed Interest in Africa

    Persistent Ignorance: Despite centuries of coastal trading contacts, Europeans remained incredibly ignorant of Africa, its peoples, and its history.
    Stimulus for Revival: The movement for the abolition of the slave trade provided a new impetus for European interest in Africa.
    Period of Exploration (1780s-1870s): Many European explorers traveled into the interior of Africa to address this ignorance.

Motives of European Explorers

    Portrayal (before 1850s): Historians often depicted explorers as motivated by a spirit of pure, unbiased, and scientific inquiry. Many explorers may have viewed themselves this way.
    Personal Motives: Adventure, desire for fame, or both.
    Financiers' Motives: Those who funded these journeys, praised discoveries, and read the books were often driven by the chance of greater trading profit in the future.
    Reality: While much of Africa needed mapping, European explorers generally served as direct agents of colonial expansion.

Early Portuguese Catholic Missionaries

    Close Follow-Up: Roman Catholic Christian missionaries from Portugal closely followed the early Portuguese coastal penetration of tropical Africa.
    Mission (late 15th / early 16th centuries): Sent to Africa to convert several African rulers.
    Underlying Hope: It was hoped that these converted rulers would become valuable allies of the Portuguese.
    Reasons for Failure:
        African rulers recognized the strong political motivation behind the missionaries' presence.
        Missionaries and their few converts posed a direct challenge to the established political and religious order.
        Resulted in missionaries being expelled or even killed in many African states.
    African Rulers' Priorities: They were interested in contact with Europeans for new trading openings, technical assistance, and firearms, not for new ideas that threatened the traditional religious basis of their authority.
    Examples of Success and Failure:
        Benin: Portuguese missionaries made little headway; their attempt to convert the Oba (ruler of the Edo people) was abandoned in the early 16th century.
        Kongo: Achieved greater success, with a Christian convert becoming king in 1506.
        Ethiopia: Jesuit missionaries followed Portuguese military assistance in the 1540s but had little success converting Ethiopian Orthodox Christians to Roman Catholicism. They were expelled in the mid-17th century for political interference.
    Overall Impact (by 18th century): European Christianity, as a vehicle for religious and cultural change, had virtually no lasting impact on the peoples of Sub-Saharan Africa.

19th Century Protestant and Catholic Missions

    Renewed Missionary Effort: During the 19th century, many European Christian societies sent missionaries to Africa.
    Important British Societies:
        Anglican Church Missionary Society: Prominent in Sierra Leone.
        Wesleyan Methodist Missionary Society.
        London Missionary Society: Had bases and schools along the West African coastal region, and initially worked mainly in South Africa.
    Other Protestant Missions: Also came from France, Germany, Holland, and the United States.
    French Catholic Missions: Followed later in the century.

Unit Summary

    Portugal was the European country that established early contacts with the coastal regions of West Africa. Their immediate goal was to bypass Muslim North Africa and gain direct access to the gold-producing region of West Africa. The Trans-Atlantic slave trade began within three important circumstances in West Africa: the fragmentation of large, indigenous political states (Ghana, Mali, Songhai); the region's growing importance as a source of enslaved people for caravan traffic across the Sahara; and the conflict between small coastal states and their willingness to cooperate with European traders in exchanging slaves for arms. Although Europeans gradually outlawed the slave trade, they found West Africa a fertile field for "legitimate commerce." In 1652, the Dutch trading company established a tiny permanent settlement on the southern shores of South Africa to regulate trade with the Khoisan and provide provisions for ships, which later paved the way for white settlement. Roman Catholic Christian missionaries from Portugal closely followed early Portuguese coastal penetration, attempting to convert African rulers in the late 15th and early 16th centuries. In several cases, Christian missionaries played a significant role in promoting and shaping the advent of European colonialism.
