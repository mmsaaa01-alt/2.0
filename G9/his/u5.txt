Unit 5: Peoples and States of Africa to 1500

    This unit explores the rich history of African societies before 1500 AD, focusing on the diversity of languages and peoples, the emergence and development of various states and empires across North, West, East, and Southern Africa, the significant spread and impact of Islam, and Africa's intra- and inter-continental relations, particularly the Trans-Saharan trade.

5.1 Languages and Peoples of Africa

    Africa is a vast continent with immense linguistic diversity, boasting over 1000 interrelated languages. Linguists classify these languages into four major super-families, revealing deep connections among the continent's peoples.

Diversity of African Languages

    Africa is a vast continent inhabited by people who speak a wide variety of languages.
    Linguists have identified over 1000 diverse and interrelated languages spoken across the continent.
    Languages belonging to the same group often share certain vocabulary or words, indicating their relationship.
    Linguists classify the peoples of Africa into speakers of four super-families of languages based on these relationships.

Four Super-Families of African Languages

    Congo-Kordofanian (or Niger-Congo, which includes Congo-Kordofanian)
    Afro-Asiatic
    Nilo-Saharan
    Khoisan

Details on Each Super-Family
1. Afro-Asiatic

    Location: Speakers live in northern Africa, the Sahara, the Horn of Africa, and around Lake Chad.
    Sub-groups: Divided into six sub-groups:
        Berber
        Chadic
        Coptic (Ancient Egyptian)
        Cushitic
        Omotic
        Semitic
    Specific Sub-group Distribution:
        The Semitic language sub-group is spoken mainly in the Horn of Africa.
        The Omotic sub-family is spoken only in Ethiopia.
        The Cushitic branch extends from the Red Sea coast, through the Horn of Africa, and into central Kenya and Tanzania.

2. Niger-Congo (formerly Congo-Kordofanian in broader classification)

    Significance: Based on rough estimates, this super-family has the most significant number of speakers.
    Main Branches: Has two main branches:
        Niger-Congo:
            Covers much of West Africa and all of Bantu Africa.
            Bantu speakers live across much of sub-Saharan Africa.
            There are over 450 languages grouped in the Bantu family.
            Kiswahili (one of the most widely spoken African languages, a mixture of African and Arab cultures) belongs to the Bantu language group.
        Kordofanian:
            Languages are spoken in a small area in the Nuba hills, south of the Republic of Sudan.

3. Nilo-Saharan

    Significance: The third majority in terms of the number of speakers.
    Subgroups: Divided into six subgroups:
        Chari-Nile
        Songhay
        Saharan
        Meban
        Koman
        Fur

4. Khoisan

    Subgroups: Has two subgroups:
        Khoikhoi
        San
    Location: Speakers of these languages live in and around the Kalahari Desert of northern South Africa, Southwestern Botswana, and Namibia.

Non-Native Languages in Africa

    Not all languages spoken in Africa are native to the continent.
    Arabic language:
        Spoken by many people in northern Africa.
        Brought to Africa by the Muslim Arabs between the seventh and eleventh centuries.
    European languages:
        Brought to their respective colonies of Africa by the European colonial powers (e.g., English, French, Spanish, Portuguese).

Case Study: Living Along the Border

    The Horn of Africa is inhabited by peoples who speak various languages.
    Living along borders encourages interaction between people of diverse cultures and languages.
    Example (Neima in Moyale, Ethio-Kenyan border): Can communicate in Afan-Oromo, Amharic, Somali, Swahili, and English due to exposure, participating in local trade.

5.2 States in North Africa

    North Africa witnessed the rise and fall of powerful states, including Mamluk Egypt and the Funj Sultanate, which played significant roles in the region's political and economic landscape.

A) Mamluk Egypt

    Origin: The Fatimid rulers in Egypt used soldiers called Mamluks.
    Independence: Egyptian independence from Baghdad was declared in 969 AD.
    Mamluks:
        Were of Turkish origin.
        Gradually became military commanders and took power militarily.
        Founded their dynasty that ruled Egypt from 1250 to 1517 AD.
    Expansion: Under Mamluk rule, Egypt expanded into Palestine, Syria, Europe, and Asia.
    Economy: Egypt witnessed critical economic developments under the Mamluks.
    Ethio-Egyptian Relations: Marked by conflicts during Mamluk rule.
        Ethiopian ruler, Zera Yacob (r. 1434-1468), attempted to create smooth relations, but it was short-lived.
    Decline: Mamluk power began to decline due to:
        Corruption
        Decline in agricultural productivity
        Cattle plague
        Drought
    Fall: The Ottoman Turks, utilizing firearms, defeated the Mamluk force in Palestine and occupied Egypt in 1517 AD.

B) The Funj Sultanate

    Establishment: Established in 1504 AD by cattle-keeping nomads in northeast Africa (present-day Republic of Sudan).
    Capital: Sennar.
    Conflicts: Like the Mamluks, the Funj Sultanate fought against the Christian kingdom of Ethiopia due to rivalry over trade routes.
        The army of King Susenyos destroyed them in about 1620 AD.
    Duration: The Funj Sultanate continued to exist until Muhammed Ali of Egypt conquered it in the 1820s.

5.3 Spread of Islam and its Impact in West Africa

    The arrival and expansion of Islam profoundly transformed West Africa, promoting trade, fostering cultural exchange, and contributing to the rise of powerful empires by integrating political and religious authority.

Early Expansion of Islam

    Birth of Islam: Early 7th century.
    Conquest of Egypt: Muslim Arabs conquered Egypt in 642 AD.
    North African Expansion: Later in the 7th century, they expanded from Egypt to the rest of North Africa.
    Further Reach: By the end of the 7th century, the Arab empire had reached Morocco and conquered southern Spain.
    Revival and Immigration: Following politico-religious rivalries in the 11th century, there was a revival of Islam in North Africa, accompanied by a further wave of Arab immigration. This also coincided with the expansion of West African Empires.

Impacts of Islam on West Africa

    Promotion of Trade: Islam promoted trade between West Africa and the Mediterranean.
        It developed and widened the Trans-Saharan Caravan trade, enriching both West African and Muslim traders.
        Muslims from North Africa settled in commercial centers.
    Cultural Understanding and Cooperation: Islam linked diverse peoples through better cultural understanding and a spirit of cooperation and commonwealth.
    Rise of Empires and Wealth:
        The historical impact of Islam on trade significantly increased the wealth of African people.
        It helped the rise of great African empires by facilitating the emergence of political empires that encouraged trade and wealth.
    Political Authority: Islam was attractive to kings because of its concept of the caliph's combined political power with religious authority.

5.4 States in Western Africa

    West Africa was home to several flourishing states and empires, notably Ghana, Mali, and Songhai, whose power and prosperity were often intertwined with the control of Trans-Saharan trade routes and the influence of Islam.

A) Ghana Empire

    Location: Ancient Ghana was located in parts of present-day Mauritania, Mali, and Senegal.
    Founders: Founded by the Soninke people in the 4th century AD.
    Peak Power: Reached the height of its power around 1000 AD.
    Economy: Relied heavily on the Trans-Saharan trade, which enabled its rulers to build a vast empire.
    Decline and Fall:
        The rise of Islam was a main factor in both the greatness and decline of Ghana.
        Ancient Ghana came to an end in 1076 AD when the Islamic Empire of Almoravids invaded and captured its capital, Kumbi Saleh.

B) Mali Empire

    Emergence: Mali emerged after the fall of Ghana.
    Founder: Founded by Sundiata Keita of the Mandinka people.
    Cultural Influence: The Mali Empire had profound cultural influences on West Africa, spreading its language, laws, and customs along the Niger River.
    Greatest King: Mansa Musa (1312-1337 AD) was the greatest king.
        He made a pilgrimage to Mecca.
        During his reign, Islam expanded vastly in the Empire.
    Center of Learning: Timbuktu, the capital of Mali, became one of the most important centers for Islamic learning and culture.
    Decline:
        After Mansa Musa, there was no king as powerful.
        The kingdom weakened due to inefficient leadership and attacks from neighboring peoples, such as the Tuareg.
        After Mali fell, the Kingdom of Songhai took its place.

C) Songhai Empire

    Establishment: The Songhai people established the Empire, named after them, with its capital at Gao.
    History: The Songhai had been subject to Ghana and then to Mali at various times. Towards the end of the 14th century, they threw off the overlordship of Mali.
    Extension: The Empire extended from the central area of today's Nigeria to the Atlantic coast.
    Power Source: Songhai became powerful by controlling the Trans-Sahara trade.
        They had trading connections with the Berbers.
    Religion: Its kings accepted Islam early in the 11th century.
    Prosperity: Their prosperity grew as gold began to come from the forest region.
    End: The Empire ended in 1591 AD when a Moroccan army defeated Songhai in the Battle of Tondibi.

D) The Kingdoms Kanem-Bornu

    Location: One of many kingdoms in the grassland region of Africa.
    Ruling Family: A Muslim dynasty.
    Importance of Trade: Trade was crucial for Kanem's greatness.
        Kanem's army kept the trade routes safe and collected taxes from traders.
    Name: After Bornu became a province of Kanem, the Empire was often called Kanem-Bornu.
    Decline: Kanem-Bornu probably began to decline because trade centers shifted from inland routes to the Atlantic coast of West Africa.

E) The Hausa States

    Emergence: The Hausa city-states emerged in Nigeria between 1000 and 1200 AD.
    People: The people were both pastoralists and farmers.
    Major City-States: Kano, Katsina, Zazzau (Zaria), Gobir, Kebbi, Rano, and Zamfara.
    Economy: Based on agriculture and the Trans-Saharan trade.
    Noted For: Hausa cities were known for their leather goods, craft technology, and textiles.
        The most famous was the walled city of Kano.
    Spread of Islam: Islam spread to Hausa land from Kanem-Bornu.

5.5 Equatorial, Central and Eastern Africa

    Eastern Africa saw the rise of diverse states and societies, many heavily influenced by Islam and enriched by trade. The Bantu migrations also played a crucial role in shaping the region's demography and culture, leading to the development of kingdoms like Kongo and the Swahili city-states.

Introduction to East African States

    A variety of states and small societies emerged in eastern Africa.
    Many were strongly influenced by Islam.
    Some became extremely wealthy due to trade.
    Bantu Migrations: Beginning in the 1st millennium BC, new peoples, specifically farming peoples speaking dialects of the Bantu family, migrated into eastern Africa from the west.
        They moved slowly as small communities, not as invading hordes, into East Africa and the Congo River basin.

A) Kongo

    Location: The Kingdom of Kongo was located on the lower bank of the Zaire River.
    Founders: Founded by Bantu people in the late 14th century.
    People: Were hunters and warriors, and clever smiths.
    Economy: Apart from agriculture, trade played an important role in the kingdom's economy.
    European Contact:
        Explorers reached Kongo in 1482 and 1491.
        Missionaries, who converted people to Catholicism, followed.
        The Portuguese eventually colonized the kingdom.

B) Swahili City-States

    Establishment: About 2000 years ago, Bantu-speaking people established small village settlements along the coast of the Indian Ocean.
    Islamic Influence: After the rise of Islam, the region received significant impetus from Muslim Arabic-speaking merchants who married into local ruling families.
    Culture and Language: Subsequently, a distinct language and culture developed, which was African but with Islamic and Arabic influences.
    Swahili Language: These mixed Bantu-Arab populations called their language Swahili, meaning "coasters" in Arabic. Swahili is now a common language in East African countries.
    Cultural Diffusion: The Swahili Civilization is a prime example of cultural diffusion, formed from the interaction between Arabic, Persian, and Bantu traditions.
    Important Coastal Commercial Centers: Several important and influential coastal cities or ports:
        Mogadishu (in Somalia)
        Malindi and Mombasa (in Kenya)
        Zanzibar and Kilwa (in Tanzania)
        Brava Pate, Pemba, and Sofala
    Kilwa: The most famous and imposing city, situated on a small island. Kilwa became the main center of the East African gold and ivory trade.
    Governance: Wealthy Muslim elites ruled the Swahili cities.
    Trade Relations: Evidence suggests long-standing commercial relations with Arabia, the Persians, India, and China existed long before the Portuguese arrival.
    Portuguese Arrival:
        From the very beginning, the Portuguese aimed to destroy the growing power of Muslims in East Africa.
        Impressed by East Africa's gold and ivory trade, the Portuguese established a trading fort at Sofala in 1505.

5.6 Southern Africa

    The southern half of Africa saw the gradual formation of states from consolidated villages, with Great Zimbabwe standing out as a powerful trading empire. The indigenous Khoi-Khoi and San peoples, with distinct lifestyles, faced significant displacement following European arrival.

Formation of States

    States formed more slowly in Southern Africa than in the north.
    Independent villages gradually consolidated in some parts of Southern Africa, starting in the 11th century, leading to the formation of the first states.

A) Zimbabwe

    Founders: The founders of Great Zimbabwe were the Bantu-speaking people of Shona, who began their rule about 1000 AD.
    Great Zimbabwe:
        They built Great Zimbabwe.
        In the 13th or 14th century, stone houses and walls were built.
        It was a medieval African city known for its large circular wall and tower.
        Part of a wealthy African trading empire that controlled much of the East African coast from the 11th to the 15th centuries.
    Economy: Based on cattle husbandry, crop cultivation, and the trade of gold on the coast of the Indian Ocean.
    Trade Relations: Maintained trade relations with the Swahili states. Great Zimbabwe was known for its gold mines.
    Successor Empire: After 1450 AD, the Rozwi clan kings of the Shona people established the empire called Mwene Mutapa.
    Fall: The Portuguese conquered Mwene Mutapa in the late 16th century.
    Etymology: "Zimbabwe" is a Shona (Bantu) word meaning "stone houses."

B) The Khoi-Khoi

    Initial Lifestyle: Initially, the Khoi-Khoi people were hunters.
    Change in Lifestyle: Since 1000 AD, they transitioned from hunting to sheep and cattle breeding.
    Expansion: Beginning from 1300 AD, the Khoi-Khoi expanded from present-day Botswana and were well-established in Southern Africa by the 15th century.
    European Contact and Displacement: After the Dutch settlers landed in South Africa in the mid-17th century, the Khoi-Khoi people were driven out of their land to the Kalahari Desert.
    European Name: Europeans called them "Hottentots."

C) The San

    Original Location: Before being driven out by European settlers, the San lived in parts of present-day Angola and Namibia.
    European Name: Europeans called them "Bushmen."
    Lifestyle: The San lived by hunting and gathering. Men hunted animals with bows and poison-tipped arrows.
    European Contact and Displacement: Dutch farmers pushed the San to the Kalahari Desert, where they still lived as hunters.
    Physical Characteristics: They have yellowish-brown skin and tightly coiled black hair.
    Language: They speak Khoisan languages, which are characterized by clicking sounds.

5.7 Africa's Intra and Inter-continental Relations

    Africa maintained a complex web of relations both within the continent and with outside powers. The Trans-Saharan trade routes were vital for intra-continental exchange and the spread of Islam, while later European contacts profoundly reshaped Africa's interactions with the rest of the world, leading to the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.

Definitions

    Intercontinental relation: Occurs between two or more continents.
    Intracontinental relation: Occurs within a continent.

Early Intra-continental Contact

    People living north and south of the Sahara Desert had a long history of irregular contact.
    Trade between these peoples became better organized after the introduction of the camel.

Trans-Saharan Caravan Trade

    Began regularly in the 4th century, expanding from pre-existing intra- and inter-regional trade within Africa.
    Contribution: Contributed to developing strong economic and political systems on both sides of the Sahara.
    Impact of Islam: The spread of Islam in tropical Africa further strengthened the relationship with the peoples of North Africa.

Arab Trade and Exchange of Ideas

    The Arabs traded with societies in North and East Africa.
    Trade contacts facilitated the exchange of ideas.

European Contact

    The exploration and subsequent contact with European powers contributed to the ever-changing Africa.
    Outcome: The outcome of Afro-European contact during the exploration era was the opening of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.

5.8 Trans-Saharan Trade

    The Trans-Saharan trade was a pivotal network connecting diverse African regions and linking Africa to the wider world. It facilitated the movement of goods, people, and ideas, enriching empires and spreading Islam across West and West-Central Africa.

Routes and Connections

    The Trans-Saharan trade linked various African peoples despite their diversity.
    Main Trade Routes:
        Connected west and west-central Africa with north-central and north-west Africa, linking major ancient and medieval states of West Africa.
        Crossed the central Sahara and connected North Africa with the kingdom of Kanem.
        A sub-branch linked West Africa and Egypt.
    The trade routes across the desert also connected Africa with the outside world.

Key Role and Traded Items

    Key Role: The Trans-Saharan trade network played a key role in the movement of people, materials, and ideas.
    Important Traded Items:
        Gold from the regions of West Africa.
        Salt mined in northwest Africa.
        Enslaved people became important trade goods after the emergence of the Arabs in the 7th century AD.

Impacts of Trans-Saharan Trade

    Economic and Political Development: Contributed to developing robust economic and political systems on both sides of the Sahara.
        For example, the great West African empires of Ghana, Mali, and Songhai became powerful because they could control the Trans-Saharan trade route.
    Spread of Islam: Facilitated the spread of Islam and Islamization in West and West-Central Africa.

5.9 Early Contacts with the Outside World

    Africa's East African coast was a hub for early contacts with various outside civilizations, leading to trade and cultural exchange. However, later European exploration and dominance, driven by economic ambitions, fundamentally altered these relations, culminating in the initiation of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.

East African Coast Accessibility

    The accessibility of the East African coast led to widespread interaction between it and people from the outside world.
    Early Contacts: Mainly with Arabs, Greeks, Persians, Romans, Chinese, Indians, Spain, and Portuguese.

European Perception of Africa

    Europeans created an image of Africa that was the "perverse opposite of Europe's."
    This image was used to establish Europe's general superiority as "self-evident."
    Europe's idea of itself was thus predicated on its image of Africa.

Global Events and European Dominance (Mid-15th Century Onward)

    Since the mid-15th century, global events brought Africa closer to the rest of the world.
    Portugal and Spain: These European countries were determined to dominate international trade and sponsored navigators for exploration.
    Impact of European Contact: The exploration and subsequent contact with European powers enormously contributed to the ever-changing African economy, culture, language, politics, and environment.
    Outcome: The most significant outcome of Afro-European contact during the exploration era was the opening of the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade.

Unit Summary

    Africa is a vast continent with diverse peoples speaking languages from four major super-families: Niger-Congo, Afro-Asiatic, Nilo-Saharan, and Khoisan. Not all languages are native, with Arabic and European languages introduced. Before 1500 AD, significant historical developments occurred, establishing large Islamic empires in North Africa, such as Mamluk Egypt (1250-1517 AD) and the Funj Sultanate (until 1820s), both often in conflict with Christian Ethiopia. The 11th century saw an Islamic revival in North Africa, preceding the expansion of West African Empires like Ancient Ghana, Mali, and Songhay, which thrived by controlling the Trans-Saharan trade. Other states like Hausa States and Kanem-Bornu also flourished. In Southern Africa, Great Zimbabwe exemplified high civilization, with the Mwene Mutapa empire succeeding it after 1450 AD, before Portuguese conquest in the late 16th century. The Trans-Saharan trade fostered robust economic and political systems and strengthened relations with North Africa through the spread of Islam. Africa's long-standing relations with the outside world, primarily through trade, and the introduction of Christianity and Islam, shaped its global interactions. European powers, particularly Portugal and Spain, began to dominate Africa, leading to the decline of many kingdoms by the 16th century and the commencement of the Atlantic slave trade.
