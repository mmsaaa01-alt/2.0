Political, Social and Economic Processes in Ethiopia: Mid-16th to Mid-19th Century

    This section reviews the political, social, and economic developments in Ethiopia from the mid-16th to the mid-19th century, focusing on various peoples and states in different regions. It covers the emergence of independent polities, their structures, and their relationships with each other, as well as the Gondarine period, the Zemene-Mesafint, and the Yejju Dynasty.

8.1 Peoples and States of the Eastern, Central, Southern, and Western Regions

    This section reviews some polities that flourished in the southern part of Ethiopia in the period and their major political, economic, and social features.

From the middle of the 16th century to the middle of the 19th century, many people of Ethiopia were politically autonomous, and independent polities flourished. They maintained independence from the Christian Kingdom and other powers, yet maintained relationships through trade, marriage, and conflicts over resources. These interactions led to territorial expansion or loss.
8.1.1 Southern States

    This section focuses on the Omotic States under the Tato and Kawo/Kati systems, as well as Cushitic States under the Sera-Woma system.

    Key Terms:
        Tato: Title of the king in Kafa and Yem kingdoms.
        Kawo: Title of the king in Wolaita, Gamo, and Gofa.
        Kati: Title of the king in Dawuro and Konta.
        Sera: A set of local cultural norms regulating the communal social structure among Cushitic people.
        Woma: King or height of authority in the traditional administrative system of Cushitic states.
        Gondar: A city in Ethiopia and the name for its castle.
        Zemene-Mesafint: The Era of Warlords.
        Yejju Dynasty: A ruling family founded by Ali Gwangul.

Omotic States and Peoples under Tato system
Kafa

    Located south of Enareya, south of the Gojeb River, and north of the Omo River. Known since at least the end of the fourteenth century, according to local traditions.
    The area receives heavy rainfall, and has dense forests and fertile soil, inhabited by people related to the Bench.
    Political Structure:
        Ruled by a king whose title was Tato.
            The insignia of kingship included twelve golden objects. The crown was a helmet made of gold and silver with a triple phallus of gold in front.
        Kingship was hereditary.
        An advisory council of nobles called Mikkerecho assisted the king.
            The Mikkerecho chose the king's successor (not necessarily the eldest son).
            They were also responsible for regional administration and maintaining law and order.
        Regional Administration:
            Districts were governed by kinglets or chiefs, who wore golden armlets as insignia.
            Sub-districts (woraafo) were in charge of junior chiefs (woraafe rasho), who reported to the king.
        Center of the kingdom: Bonga
        Second seat of the kingdom: Anderecha, southeast of Bonga.
    Historical Context:
        An independent kingdom from the Christian Highland state.
        Influenced by the Christian kingdom, but independent by the beginning of the 16th century.
        Survived Oromo pressure and disconnected its relation with the Christian Kingdom.
        Remained independent until the late 19th century.
    Economy:
        Based on agriculture and trade.
        Coffee (said to have originated in Kafa) was the main crop.
        Enset was the major food crop.
        Main trade items: ivory, musk, honey, coffee, enslaved people, and gold.
    External Relations:
        Had considerable trade relations with neighboring Oromo and Shewan states in the nineteenth century.
    Defense:
        Used deep trenches called Kuripo as a defensive barrier.
    Cultural Influences:
        The Kafa rulers adopted the title Tato from the neighboring kingdom of Ennarya, whose ruling elite moved south due to Oromo pressure.
        Christianity was introduced from Enaria but remained limited to the royal families. The local people largely continued to practice their indigenous religion.

Yem

    Established a state before the late 16th century.
    Power was transferred from the Gama dynasty to the Mowa dynasty (traced their origin from Gonder before the 15th century).
    The Mowa dynasty ruled until the late 19th century from its capital at Angari.
    Political Structure:
        Hierarchically organized, similar to Kafa and Ennarya.
        King was called Tato or Amno.
            He was a political and religious leader.
        Twelve councillors (astessors), led by Waso, assisted the king.
            The council nominated the king's successor, made decisions on wars, foreign relations, and administration.
    Religious Influences:
        Influenced by Orthodox Christianity.
        Paid tribute to the Christian kingdom in horses (according to a few sources).
        Relationship with the Christian kingdom seems to have discontinued after the arrival of the Oromo to the Gibe region.
    External Relations:
        Repulsed the Oromo in the Gibe region.
        Pressure intensified after the formation of the kingdom of Jimma Abba Jiffar.
        Raids and counter-raids with Jimma continued until the last quarter of the 19th century.

    Key Takeaways: The kingdoms of Kafa and Yem were independent states with hierarchical political structures, influenced by both indigenous traditions and Christianity. Their economies were based on agriculture and trade, and they maintained complex relationships with neighboring states.

Omotic States and Peoples under Kawo/Kati system
Overview

    The Omotic states of Wolaita, Gamo, Gofa, Dawuro, and Konta, located on both sides of the Omo River, practiced nearly similar political structures.
    Wolaita is located between the Bilate River in the east and the Omo River in the west. The Omo River separates them from the Dawuro people. The Gamo people live south of Wolaita, in the Gamo highlands.
    The first written records mentioning Wolaita and Gamo date back to the fifteenth century.

Political Structure

    The people of Wolaita, Gamo, and Gofa were led by a hereditary king called Kawo, whereas the Dawuro and Konta people used to call him Kati.

Wolaita

    The state of Wolaita might have been part of the kingdom of Damot.
    The ruling dynasty was known as Wolaita Malla. They believe that Motalami was its first king.
    Wolaita was mentioned as paying tribute to the Christian king Yeshaq (r. 1413 - 1430).
    Kings (kawos) belonged to the Wolaita Malla and Tigre-Mala dynasties.
        The Wolaita Malla began with the kingdom of Damot in the 13th century.
        King Motolami was traditionally said to have been the founder of the Wolaita-Mala ruling family until the Tigre-Mala replaced it in the late fifteenth century. The Tigre-Mala lasted until the late 19th century.

Dawuro

    The Dawuro state was established in the 16th century by an immigrant clan called Kawuka.
    By about 1700, the Kawuka dynasty had created a big state.
    Notable Rulers:
        Kati Erashu (r. 1719-1775)
        Kati Halala (r. 1775-1835)
    Administrative hierarchy (ascending order): Huduga, Abaliya, Danna, Guuda, Erasha, Woraba, Bushasha, and Kate.

Konta

    The khati of Konta controlled and supervised subordinate officials with the titles of Gana, Torancha, Woraba, Erasha, Guda, and Dana.

Gamo

    The Gamo organized between 40 to 55 politically autonomous units called dere.
        Each dere administered itself independently and had a hereditary ruler, kawo.
        The dere assembly called duletha was the major administrative body.
        The assembly was made up of elected officials called halaka, who were the real administrators of the deres. They decided on all major decisions.

Other Omotic Groups (Oyda, Zayse, Basketo, Maleh, Kara, and Kore)

    The states of Wolaita, Gamo, and Gofa had tributary relations with the Christian kingdom until the movement of the Oromo in the 16th century cut it off. They resisted Oromo pressure.
    The administration was hierarchical among the Oyda people, with officials bearing titles of Khati, Chamicha, Bitane, and Erasha.
        The Chamicha preserved peace and security.
        The eldest son succeeds the khati.
    The Zayse king belonged to the Zulessa clan.
        Subordinate to khati were seven public administrators with the title of maga, whose position was hereditary.
    The realm of Gamo was divided into seven kingdoms, each ruled by a king (kawo).
        The king delivered verdicts and appointed officials: Woraba, Erasha, Guda, Gana, and Mochena (Mura).
    The highest authority over the socio-political structures of the Basketo, Maleh, Kara, Zayise, Oyda, and Kore was a hereditary king called khati.
        Administrative hierarchy in Basketo: Dana, Guda, Bitena, Gudena, and Chemach.
        Officials in Maleh: Goda, Kagotoka, and Katoydka (goda were the most powerful).
        The Maleh's Khati possessed spiritual powers.
        Traditional Kara social structure: Arti and Ghana.
    The Omotic language family speaking people of Kore possessed an elective administration with officials: Awajite, Daynena, Ushete, Tora Maga, Fizena, and Mura.

Economy and Society

    Economically, the Omotic states and polities are agriculturalists.
        Enset was their main crop.
        They cultivated crops like maize, taro, and sorghum.
        They also raised livestock.
    Complex relationships between states:
        They fought wars over territory and tribute. Wolaita expanded its influence during their Tigre-Mala dynasty.
        They traded and had marriage relationships.
    Land ownership:
        Ordered according to kinship, polity, and social status.
        Communal lands were allocated for grazing and social gatherings for all members of the society except artisans.
    Defense:
        The Omotic states defended their boundaries by using fortifications called Kella.
            Wolaita's most famous fortification was Amado Kella, constructed during Kawo Amado (r.1799-1834).
            In Dawuro, Kati Halala built stone fortifications, the Kati Halala Kella.

    Key Takeaways: The Omotic states were characterized by hereditary kings (Kawo/Kati), hierarchical administrations, and agricultural economies centered on enset cultivation. They engaged in both conflict and cooperation with each other and neighboring groups.

Cushitic States and Peoples under Sera-Woma system
Overview

    Since the mid-16th century, several states emerged among the "Highland East Cushitic" language speakers: Hadiya, Kambata, Sidama, Tambaro, Kabena, Halaba, Gedeo, Konso and Burji.
    Due to relative freedom from the Christian Kingdom, the Sultanate of Adal, and the Oromo population movement, they developed similar traditional political structures.

Sera Tradition

    A web of relations traditionally ruled by the sera.
        Definition: A set of local cultural norms regulating the communal social structure; an unwritten law.
        Not a codified body of rules, but several sets of norms.
        Implementation is consultative and compromise-oriented, influenced by circumstances as elders see fit.

Woma

    In their traditional administrative system, woma (king) is the height of authority.

Hadiya

    The administrative system was called Sera.
    Hadiya was an important Muslim state during the 13th-16th centuries.
    The wars of Imam Ahmed and the Oromo population movement transformed its territorial, social, and political landscape in the 16th century.
    During the wars of Imam Ahmed, the rulers of Hadiya joined the Imam.
    In the 17th century, Hadiya was confronted with the Christian kingdom.
    The traditional administrative system leadership positions were all elected officers.
    Equivalent to a king is named adila (garada), a term derived from the Arabic word adil.
        The kings led the council of elders.
        Power transfer was hereditary through ritual ceremonies.
    Each clan (Giicho) administered itself independently without recognizing any higher authority.
        The clan leaders continued to use the title of garad.
        The garad was not an absolute ruler.
        He could also be an elected war leader with the title of gatanna or abegaz.
        The position was not hereditary.

Kambata

    Maintained the tradition of monarchy from king Anno to king Dilbato, all from the Oyata clan.
    Mount Hambaricho: an important symbolic site for the Kambata.
    Christian churches on Mount Hambaricho were not destroyed during the wars of Ahmed ibn Ibrahim. Religious objects were concealed in caves.
    The earliest clans were believed to be seven in number and settled on Mount Hambaricho.
    Christianity was introduced sometime in the 14th or 15th centuries.
    Before introducing local administration, Kambata was governed by a Christian governor called Hamelmal.

Halaba and Tambaro

    The woma of Halaba came from the Sidae clan.
    The offices of kings of Tambaro were from Molla clans.

Sidama

    Power transfer took place in luwa ceremonies once every eight years.
    The Sidama people comprise several sub-tribes ruled independently by their king, the woma (supreme judge).
    The woma of the different sub-tribes make up the woma-songo, the assembly of sub-tribal kings or judges (the highest traditional authority).

Qabena

    Ruled by a group of elected leaders, the woma.
    An administrative council comprises 12 judges governing based on traditional and Islamic law.
    The woma of Halaba chaired the council of clans called Ogate and presided over ritual ceremonies.

Gedeo and Konso

    Political process varies from contemporary Cushitic states.
    Gadaa system was introduced in Gedeo after the period of Akumanoye. It was an era when women succeeded one another in dynastic rule and was reported to have been dictatorial.
    The Gedeo Gadaa system comprised nine members council called yiea.
    The Gadaa system announced power distribution and introduced egalitarian, democratic values.
    The Gadaa system transferred power once every eight years.
    Among Konso's nine clan leaders, poqala tuma possessed administrative and judicial authority.

Economy and Society

    One unifying feature of these groups was their economies.
    They were farmers belonging to the "enset-culture complex" and cattle herders.
    Enset was a crucial staple for almost all of them except the Konso (due to their environment). They, therefore, focused on producing cereals crops.
    They have grains, the most important of which are sorghum and millet.
    The region receives low and erratic rainfall, making these crops ideal.
    Konso developed an intensive agriculture system through time, terracing their hillsides with stones.

Enset Cultivation

    Enset (Ensete ventricosum) is mainly cultivated by different ethnic groups west of the Rift Valley, except the Sidama, the Gedeo and the Amarro areas east of the Rift Valley.
    The enset plant provides a large amount of carbohydrates.
    Enset cultivation made the high density of population possible.
    Enset can be stored for months, allowing planned storage.
    The Enset cultivation and the cattle breeding complemented each other.
    Enset food products have been used as a staple and/or co-staple food by the people who inhabit the country's southern, southwestern, and western parts. These regions are among the most densely populated areas in Ethiopia.
    The markets became places for communication across ethnic borders.

Gurage

    Although not a Cushitic language speaking population, their culture has been strongly influenced by the Cushitic speaking population.
    They are also part of what has been called the "enset-culture complex".
    The Gurage chiefdoms were concentrated in the area west of Lake Dambal and the highlands of Dawaro.
    The Gurage were mentioned in written sources for the first time in the 14th century.
    Origins: Oral tradition suggests that they were descendants of Christian settlers from Gura, Eritrea, led by a man called Azmach Sebehat.
    Cultural Influences: influenced culturally by the medieval Islamic and Christian Semitic culture.
    Economy: practice agriculture. Enset is their main staple crop.
    Its local chiefs used the titles of Azmach, Abegaz and Nigus.
    Socio-political organization:
        The basic unit of socio-political organisation was the clan assemblies.
        Local power is vested in lineages; these descent groups display corporate rights, obligations, and influence.
        The religious or ritual system is highly centralised; ritual officials sanction the authority of the political elders.
        Elected officials exercised political, social and legal power.
        They distributed land administered justice and organised social events like funerals.
        This was called the Yajoka Sera among the Sebat Bet, while among the Kistane, it was known as Ye Gordana Sera.

Siltie

    The Siltie people live predominantly in Siltie zone, which is located in central Ethiopia.
    The ethno-genesis of the Siltie has exhibited two versions:
        One version: the people existed in what is known eastern part of Ethiopia in ancient Muslim Sultanate of Harla in the ninth century, and they are one of the ancient people who had established the medieval Muslim Sultanate of Hadiya which was incorporated into the Christian highland Kingdom during Amde-Tsion (r. 1314-1344).
        Other sources: the Siltie people moved from this part of the country to the present area following Imam Ahmed ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi during the first half of the 16th century.
    The traditional administrative system termed as Siltie Sera politically united the Siltie people in a centralized administrative mechanism since at least 9th century.
    The Siltie's centralized political system is widely referred among the Siltie as YeSiltie Sera.
    The term YeSiltie Sera describes the various norms and values that regulate the interactions of members, and signifies the norms and values that every member of the Siltie society is expected to respect and abide by.
    The supreme leader of the Siltie Sera is known as Mule Gerad.
    In the system, the patrilineal descent (Abotgae) is a key factor to the Siltie social organization.
        The Siltie trace also their descendants from mother lineage (Ummegae).
    The general assembly of the Siltie Sera is called YeMule Siltie Melcho, while its judiciary apparatus is called the Raga system.
    Economy:
        Largely depends on mixed agriculture.
        In the low land and semi-highland areas Maize, Wheat and species are widely produced.
        In the highland Siltie, however, enset is widely produced and used as a staple food.
        Additionally, the Siltie have a long history as trans-regional traders, and they were actively involved in caravan trade activities in medieval Ethiopia.

    Key Takeaways: The Cushitic states under the Sera-Woma system exhibited diverse political structures, ranging from elected leaders to hereditary kings. They were primarily agricultural societies, with enset cultivation playing a vital role in their economies.

8.1.2 Western States

    This section focuses on the Oromo monarchies (Gibe and Leqa states) and the Sheikdoms and Peoples of Beni-Shangul Gumuz, as well as local chiefs of Gambella peoples.

Oromo Monarchies: Gibe States
Overview

    The Oromo had an egalitarian and republican socio-political organisation system known as the Gadaa, which is based on age groups.
    However, following their expansion in the Gibe and Wallaga regions, the system changed in the early nineteenth century.
    The Oromo gradually abandoned the Gadaa system and began to form monarchial states in these regions.

Factors for the Transformation of the Gadaa System

    Changing of Oromos from a pastoral to agrarian mode of life and the class differentiation that this brought about.
    The continuous wars that the Oromo waged during their movement and expansion.
        The war tended to strengthen the powers of the abba dula (the war-leader in the Gadaa system) at the expense of the abba boku (head of the Oromo society).
        They controlled land, markets and trade routes, and used much of the war booties to built up their private armies.
        Finally, they were able to gain control over their respective clans and regions and began to assume the title of moti, meaning king.

Emergence of Monarchies

    Manifested in two regions:
        Gibe River area: Gibe monarchies
        Wallaga: Leqa Naqamte and Leqa Qellem

Gibe States

    Five small Oromo states emerged in the Gibe region in the early nineteenth century: Limmu-Ennarya, Jimma, Gumma, Gomma and Gera.

Limmu Ennarya

    The earliest Gibe state.
    Center: Saqqa
    Established on the territories of the older state of Ennarya.
    Strategic location enabled it to control long-distance trade routes linking the region with the north.
    Became the strongest state in the region during the first half of the nineteenth century.
    Strongest ruler: Abba Bagibo, or Ibsa (r. 1825-1861)

Jimma

    Stronger than Limmu-Ennarya and other Gibe states since the middle of the nineteenth century.
    Became popular under Sanna or Abba Jifar I (r. 1830-1855).
    Named after its founder Abba Jifar I.
    Emerged in southwestern Ethiopia in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
    Depended on the ideological support of Islam and the commercial prosperity from coffee and slave trade.
    Jimma's success resulted in its supremacy in the second half of the 19th century.

Gumma

    Emerged in the first decade of the nineteenth century.
    Became an important state in 1820.
    Political importance grew up under its strong ruler, Oncho Jilcha.
    He involved Gomma in successive wars against the neighbouring states.
    Gomma was the earliest Gibe state to accept Islam.

Gera

    Became well known around 1835 during its strongest ruler, Tullu Gunji, a popular warrior king and a good administrator.
    It was in great conflict with the state of Gumma.

Conversion to Islam

    A distinctive feature of the Gibe states was their conversion from indigenous religions to Islam in the first half of the nineteenth century.

Oromo Monarchies: Leqa States

    Two main centres of monarchical power in Wallaga in the early nineteenth century: the states of Leqa Naqamte and Leqa Qellem.

Leqa Naqamte

    Established by Bakare Godana.
    Grew even more powerful under his successors Moroda and Kumsa Moroda.
    After his conversion to Christianity, his successors, Moroda and Kumsa (later Dejazmach Gabra-Egziabher), followed in his footsteps and consolidated Leqa Naqamte.

Leqa Qellem

    Founded by one of the Qellem chiefs, Tullu.
    His son and successor, Jote, consolidated it in the second half of the nineteenth century.
    Besides agriculture, the economy of Leqa states was based on the Ethio-Sudanese frontier trade.
        Some foreign merchants from Funj Sultanate of Sudan used to visit these two Leqa states.
    Jote Tullu's kingdom was in constant interaction, both hostile and peaceful, with the sheikhdoms of Asosa.

Sheikdoms and Peoples of Beni-Shangul Gumuz
Overview

    Inhabited the narrow lowland strip along the Ethio-Sudanese border in the west.
    These peoples are mainly speakers of the Nilo-Saharan language family.
    This area was a gold-producing region since Punt and commonly identified as the Silent Commerce, probably due to language barriers between merchants.

Gumuz

    Society practiced shifting cultivation as chief subsistence strategies on a small scale in the pre-19th century.
        The Gumuz used slash-and-burn agriculture as a system of farming and as a natural resource management system.
        The Gumuz farmers identified the fertility of the soil by observing the color of soil and its drainage capacity.
    The Gumuz had a positive attitude towards the forests in their surroundings.
        The Gumuz natural resource management was tied to their belief system; they believed that natural resources are the best gifts of Yama (supreme deity).
        They had indigenous knowledge on how to manage their natural resources.
    The Gumuz used fire as an essential tool for hunting, providing new vegetation growth for their herds and diminishing the ravages of the tsetse fly.
    Crop Rotation:
        Sow sorghum on the first virgin plot.
        Sow millet and sorghum again for the second round on the same plot.
        Sow cotton based on a peasant's need for the third round.
    Gumuz do not cultivate a single plot more than three times. After the third round of cultivation, this plot is left to become fallow for the regeneration of the forest.

Shinasha

    One of the groups of Gonga people who established a powerful kingdom in southwestern Ethiopia before the 16th century.
    Demographic and political pressures since the 16th century pushed them further west.
    The 16th century Oromo expansion weakened and divided the Gonga kingdom into different groups.
        The southern group was represented by the Kafa people, while the Shinasha people represented the northern group.
    The Gonga people lived along the sides of the Blue Nile, extending up to the Ethiopian escarpment long before the reign of Susenyos (1607-1632).
    Shifting cultivation was one of the subsistence strategies of Shinasha, Gumuz and Benishangul.
        The Shinasha shifting cultivation was based on cooperative labour work coordinated and led by a community leader known as Dawa Nasho.
        Shinasha followed a similar shifting plot and crop rotation pattern with the Gumuz. After third round cultivation on a single plot, they left fallow.
    The other subsistence strategy of the Shinasha people was cattle herding.
        Their knowledge of traditional medicine enabled them to restrict widespread cattle diseases in their locality.
        Shinasha's social and cultural institutions gave special value to the preservation of forests, and cutting trees from reserved forests was forbidden.

Benishangul

    Live in Benishangul Gumuz along the border of Ethiopia and Sudan.
    Mixed farmers involved in livestock raising, trade, beekeeping, and coffee cultivation.
    Their staple food is sorghum.
    Had organised their local administrative system led by their own chiefs and local elderly councils, based on kinship lines.
    Indigenous religious followers in the early period.
        Islam was introduced as a result of frontier trade and cultural relations with Sudanese.
    The people of Benishangul Gumuz used a unique musical instrument known as Zumbara.
    Were the main agents of frontier trade and cultural relations between the Ethiopian interior and its neighbours due to continuous interactions between the local Benishangul-Gumuz and the Arabic-speaking merchants.
    The Muslim states of Asosa, Benishangul and Khomosha were formed Benishangul Gumuz.
    Because of the immense gold deposits of the region, Sheikhdoms became targets for Egyptians in the 1820s and Sudanese-Mahdist penetration in 1880s.
    Nevertheless, the Sheikhdoms defended their independence and the territory of Ethiopia as well.

Local Chiefs of Gambella peoples
Overview

    The people in Gambella include Anywaa, Nuer, Majang, Komo and Opuo.
    The history of Gambella peoples was influenced by broad regional developments.
    The socio-political organisation of the different populations of Gambella was characterized by the absence of a strong centralized state structure.

Anywaa

    The political organisation was centered in their villages.
    Villages were administered by either noble called nyieye or headmen known as kuaari.
    The primary responsibility of both officials was to maintain peace and redistribute wealth.

Majang

    Traditionally had a very egalitarian society.
    The traditional leaders of the Majang were called tapad, who come from the Melanir clan.
    Their position, however, was more religious than political. They provided ritual services.
    Lived along the banks of rivers by cultivating the floodplains.
    Grew maize, tobacco, sorghum, among others and engaged in some hunting.
    Fishing and gathering were also important sources of livelihood.
    Trade connected the peoples of Gambella with their neighbours.
    Before the 1850s, different kinds of manufactured goods from Northern Sudan passed through Benishangul land and thence to the Oromo territories, and it seems that some of these goods were taken into South Sudan.

    Key Takeaways: The Western States included the Gibe and Leqa Oromo monarchies, which emerged from the Gadaa system, and the Sheikhdoms and Peoples of Beni-Shangul Gumuz, who practiced shifting cultivation and maintained trade relations with neighboring regions. The Gambella peoples had decentralized political structures with local chiefs.

8.1.3 Eastern States: Harar, Afar, Somali

    This section focuses on the states of Harar, Afar (Aussa, Bidu, Gobaad, Tajura and Rahayito) and Somali.

Harar
Historical Context

    In 1332, Ifat was defeated by the Christian kingdom and lost its independence.
    Some members of the ruling family of Ifat retreated farther to Harar and established the Sultanate of Adal around 1380.
    Harar served as a political center of the Sultanate of Adal up to the sixteenth century.
    The Sultanate of Adal was reduced to the walled town of Harar because of the Oromo expansion.
    After the death of Imam Ahmed, the Muslims of Adal were reorganised under Emir Nur Mujahid.
        In 1559 Nur Mujahid defeated and killed Gelawdewos in the Awash valley.
        He built a wall around the town of Harar, which is known as the Jegol wall.
    Following the Oromo movement and expansion, the Walasma rulers abandoned it and moved to Aussa.
    The emirate of Harar was established under a local Harari dynasty in the middle of the seventeenth century.
        The founder of this dynasty was Emir Ali Ibn Dawd (r. 1647-1662).
        Harar functioned as the capital of the Harari emirate from 1520 to 1568 became an independent emirate in the 17th century.

Significance

    The Emirate of Harar continued to serve as a strong center of Islamic culture and political power.
    Controlled the rich trade routes from the Gulf of Aden and the coastal areas of the Indian Ocean.
    From the late 16th century to the 19th century, Harar was an important trade center between the coast and the interior highlands and a location for Islamic learning.

Expansion

    Harar consolidated its power over the neighbouring communities, mainly through Islam and marriage alliances.
    Starting from 1875, the Egyptians occupied Harar for about a decade.
    The local Harari, Emir Abdullahi, revived the Harari dynasty in the second half of the 19th century.
    Harar was finally incorporated into Meneliks Empire in 1887 after the battle Chelanqo.

Afar (Aussa, Bidu, Gobaad, Tajura and Rahayito)
Geographical Context

    Since ancient times the Afar people have resided principally in the Horn of Africa in the region referred to as the "Afar Triangle", which includes Ethiopia, Eritrea and Djibouti.
    The land inhabited by the Afar in the Horn region is known as the cradle of human beings, and it has abundant natural resources.
    The Afar land is one of the sites of archaeological studies in human history.
    Afar people are predominantly Muslims.

History

    The ancestors of the Afar settled in farmland in the Ethiopian highlands and primarily raised livestock.
    Later, they began a gradual transition to a more pastoral lifestyle and moved to the area they currently occupy.
    The Afar people used the ports of Adulis, Zula, Obok and Tajura for trade.
    The commercial relations between the Afar and the interior part of Ethiopia date back to the early Christian era.
    Since time immemorial, the Afar land provided Ethiopia with a bulk of salt supplies.
        The rock salt (called amole) was used for various purposes. It also served as currency for a long period of time.

Political System

    Historically, the territory occupied by the Afar was organised into sultanates and semi-independent regions ruled by Sultans.
    Traditionally each sultanate was made up of several villages.
    In the early 14th century, the kings of the Christian kingdom associated the Afar with the medieval Adal Sultanate.
    The "Afar Triangle" maintained a confederation of sultanates, including:
        Sultanate of Aussa
        Sultanate of Bidu
        Sultanate of Tadjoura
        Sultanate of Rahaito
        Sultanate of Goobad

Aussa

    Ruled by the local Afar chiefs who had the title of Sultan.
    Later, the Walasma rulers of Adal ruled Aussa after they had left Harar in 1577.
        In 1577, the Adal leader Imam Muhammed Ga'as (r. 1573-1583), moved his capital from Harar to Aussa in Afar.
