Unit 4: The Middle Ages and Early Modern World, C. 500 to 1750s

    This unit explores the significant historical periods and developments from the decline of the Roman Empire through the early modern era, covering major civilizations in Europe and Asia, the rise of new economic and political systems, and transformative intellectual and technological changes.

4.1 The Middle Ages in Europe

    This section covers the "Dark Age," the rise of feudal society, and the enduring Byzantine Empire in Europe.

4.1.1 The Dark Age

    Time Period: The "Dark Age" refers to a period in West European history from 500 AD to 1000 AD.
    Reason for Name: It was called the Dark Ages because Civilization declined in Western Europe during this time.
    Causes of Decline:
        Internal Problems: The Western Roman Empire suffered from division and decay within.
        External Problems: It was invaded in the 5th Century AD by various tribes:
            Germanic Tribes: Anglo Saxons and Franks.
            Asiatic Tribes: Huns and Magyars.
    Changes in Western Europe Due to Invasions:
        The Greco-Roman Civilization declined.
        Societies became rural, and cities declined.
        Feudalism emerged as the socio-economic and political order.
        Churches, castles, and palaces were built.
        Bishops and monks were responsible for keeping records.
        Knights practiced chivalry.
        There was widespread war among rulers and lords, and common people faced murders and blood feuds.
    Return to Stability: By c. 1000 AD, more stability began to return, and feudal society emerged from the Dark Ages.

4.1.2 Feudal Society

    Emergence of Feudalism: As Western Europe fell apart (500 to 1500 AD), the number of separate powerful lords and vassals increased, leading to the emergence of feudalism, a new socio-political system.
    Ruling Class Components:
        Kings
        Nobles
        Knights (soldiers)
        Higher clergy
    The Vassalage System:
        This system defined the relationship between a higher lord and a lower lord in feudalism.
        The higher lord would grant a fief to the lower lord.
        In exchange, the lower lord became a vassal of the higher one, providing service.
        Fief or Feud: A piece of land granted in exchange for service.
        Feudalism: A system of holding land and providing service in exchange for that land.
    Basic Units of European Feudalism:
        Manor (villages): These were the fundamental organizational units.
    Manorial Lord's Power:
        The Manorial lord exercised political, judicial, and military power over the peasants of his Manor.
    Mass of Feudal Society:
        Peasants
        Craftsmen
        Traders
        Note: These groups had no political and economic power.
    Nature of West European Feudal Society: It was exploitative.
    Role of the Church:
        The Roman Catholic Church held significant authority over both the ruling class and ordinary people.
        It owned land.
        It was responsible not only for religion but also for education and culture.

4.1.3 Byzantine Empire

    Identity: The Byzantine Empire was the East Roman Empire, which continued after the fall of the West Roman Empire.
    Capital: Its capital was Constantinople (known today as Istanbul).
    Official Language: Greek became the official language of the Empire.
    The Orthodox Church:
        Officially separated from the Roman Catholic Church in 1054.
        It became the national church of the Empire.
        It supported the emperor.
    Economic Base:
        Agriculture
        Trade
        Crafts
    Architecture: Byzantine architecture was notable for its use of domes and beautifully colored glass.
    Social Hierarchy: The society was divided into:
        The emperor
        The higher clergy
        The nobility (who owned land)
        The peasants (who cultivated the land)
    Height of Power: The Byzantine Empire reached its greatest territorial extent and power under Emperor Justinian (reigned 527-565 AD).
    Challenges and Decline:
        Seljuk Turks: Began military occupation in Asia Minor in 1040 AD, challenging the Empire.
        Crusades:
            Started in the 1090s.
            Waged by Christian Europe against the Seljuk Turks to liberate Christian holy lands.
            There were eight major Crusades; the first began in 1096.
            They failed to crush Muslim power in the holy lands but did protect Western Europe from eastern invasions.
        Battle of Manzikert (1071): The Byzantine Empire suffered a disastrous defeat, losing most of Asia Minor first to Seljuk Turkish rulers and later to Ottoman Turks.

4.2 The Middle Ages in Asia

    This section focuses on the monumental rise and expansion of Islam, the establishment of the Ottoman Empire, and the unique political concept of the Dynastic Cycle in China.

4.2.1 The Rise and Expansion of Islam

    Significance: The rise of Islam in the early seventh century AD was a great event in world history.
    Origins:
        Began in the Arabian Peninsula.
        Associated with the life and teachings of Prophet Mohammed in Mecca around 610 AD.
    Definition of Islam: The term Islam means "submission to the will of Allah (God)."
        Islam involves worship in Allah.
        Those who worship Allah are called Muslims.
    Early Opposition and Hejira:
        Islam was initially opposed by the Quraysh Arabs, who persecuted Prophet Mohammed and his followers.
        As a result, in 622 AD, Prophet Mohammed and his followers moved from Mecca to Medina.
        This travel is known as Hejira (or Hegira), and it marked the beginning year of the Islamic Calendar.
    Impact of Muslim Arabs: They significantly changed the political, religious, and social structures of the Near East and Middle East countries.
    Expansion After Prophet's Death:
        Muslims came under the Arab Caliphates of the Umayyad and Abbasid dynasties.
        Great Arab conquests established a Muslim Arab empire stretching from North Africa and Spain in the west to Central Asia in the east.
    Contribution of Turkic Groups: The military occupation by Seljuk and Ottoman Turks in Palestine, Asia Minor, and Arabia further contributed to Islam's expansion throughout their domains and other parts of the world.

4.2.2 The Expansion of the Ottoman Empire

    Predecessors: The Seljuk Turks initiated military occupation in Palestine, Asia Minor, and Arabia in 1040.
    Emergence of Ottomans: Ottoman Turks replaced the Seljuk Turks in the region by the end of the 13th Century.
        They were named after their chief, Osman (or Othman).
    Center of the Empire: The Ottoman Empire was centered in what is now Turkey, hence its alternative name, the Ottoman Turkish Empire.
    Capital Cities:
        They seized Anatolia in 1336, making it their capital.
        They conquered Constantinople in 1453, renamed it Istanbul, and made it their new capital.
    Territorial Expansion (Height of Power):
        Captured Adrianople (1356) and Salonika (1387).
        Defeated the Serbs (1389) and Bulgarians (1393).
        By the 15th century, they predominantly conquered the Balkan Peninsula, including Greece.
        Conquered Syria in 1516 and Egypt in 1517.
    European Resistance: European forces successfully defended Vienna (capital of Austria) from a Turkish attack in 1529.
    Military Organization: The Ottomans formed a group of highly trained soldiers called janissaries.
    Religion: They spread Islam throughout their empire.
    Economy and Trade:
        Gained wealth from trade.
        Controlled the trade route from Europe to Asia.
        Banned European merchants from traveling to the Far East.
    Ottoman Turkish Society: Divided into four social groups:
        Sultan
        Grand Viziers (bureaucrats)
        Janissaries (soldiers)
        Occupational groups
    Culture: The culture of the Ottoman Empire was that of the Islamic world.
    End of European Expansion: Turkish expansion in Europe ended in 1571 when European fleets defeated the Turkish navy at the Battle of Lepanto, near Greece.

4.2.3 Dynastic Cycle in China

    Influence of Chinese Civilization: The Chinese created a great civilization that significantly impacted other countries in East Asia, particularly Korea and Japan.
    Political Concept: The Dynastic Cycle:
        The Chinese accepted the idea of a "dynastic cycle" in politics.
        New Dynasty: When a new dynasty took power, it was typically strong, ruled well, and had the support of the people.
        Decline: Over time, if a dynasty became corrupt and weak, it would lose the support of the people.
        Loss of Mandate of Heaven: This meant it would lose the "Mandate of Heaven," which was its authority and legitimacy to rule China.
        Replacement: The dynasty would then be replaced by another new dynasty, often founded by a rebel.
        Cycle Repetition: This cycle would repeat over time.
    Imperial Dynasties: There was a long succession of imperial dynasties ruling China, from the Qin to the Qing dynasty, which finally ended with a revolution in 1911/1912.

4.3 Development of Early Capitalism

    This section examines how early capitalism distinguished itself from prior economic systems and the factors that encouraged its development, particularly the revival of trade.

    Distinction of Capitalism: The key development that set capitalism apart from previous systems was the use of accumulated capital to enlarge productive capacity rather than investing in economically unproductive enterprises (like pyramids and cathedrals).
    Factors Encouraging Development: This characteristic was fostered by several historical events.
    Revival of Trade and Urban Centers:
        Most ancient cities and towns in Europe had been ruined after the collapse of the Roman Empire.
        However, trade slowly revived, and old Roman cities were restored.
        Towns became the centers of production and exchange.
    Merchant Practices: Merchants learned how to:
        Buy raw materials.
        Have workers perform manufacturing.
        Sell finished products at the best prices.
    Long-Distance International Trade:
        After the "discoveries" (Age of Explorations), new long-distance international trade became very important.
        Significant profits could be made from this trade.
    Challenges in Trade: Despite the profits, trade faced several problems:
        Transport difficulties.
        Insecurity caused by war and robbery.
        Heavy tolls (taxes) often charged on the transit of commodities.

4.4 The Age of Explorations and Discoveries

    This section details the European expeditions of the 15th and 16th centuries, their motivations, key figures, and the profound impact of these voyages on global geography and politics.

    Time Period: The age of European explorations and discoveries occurred during the 15th and 16th centuries.
    Primary Motivation: Explorations were partly initiated by the need to bypass the Ottoman Turkish blockade of the old long-distance trade route from Europe to the Far East.
    Factors Encouraging Explorations:
        European interest in long-distance trade.
        New inventions like the compass.
        Development of better maps and ships.
        Growing interest in geographical knowledge.
    Key Patrons and Leading Nations:
        Prince Henry the Navigator (1394-1460) of Portugal.
        Queen Isabella (1451-1504) of Spain.
        These monarchs sponsored voyages for economic gain, religious motives, and national glory.
        Spain and Portugal were the leaders in exploration and discoveries during the 15th and 16th centuries.
        They were closely followed by Holland, Britain, France, and Russia.
    Major Explorers and Discoveries:
        Vasco da Gama (Portuguese): Discovered a new sea route to India and the Far East in 1498.
        Christopher Columbus (European, in service of Spain): Was the first European explorer to reach America in 1492.
            He died without knowing he had found new lands.
            The land found became known as America, named after the Italian mapmaker Amerigo Vespucci.
            These newly "discovered" areas were also called the "New World."
        Ferdinand Magellan (Portuguese, in service of Spain): Circumnavigated the world between 1519 and 1522.
    Consequences of Discoveries: The newly discovered lands were later conquered and became colonies of European powers.

4.5 The Renaissance

    This section describes the Renaissance as an intellectual and artistic "rebirth" in Europe, highlighting its key characteristics, influential figures, and lasting impact on modern thought and culture.

    Definition: Renaissance was an intellectual movement, and the word literally means "rebirth" of learning.
    Time Period: It began in the 14th century and concluded in the 17th century.
    Origin and Spread: The Renaissance started in Italy and then spread to the rest of Europe, primarily to England, France, Germany, Spain, and Holland.
    Core Characteristics:
        Urban Society: Renaissance Italy was essentially an urban society.
        Age of Recovery: It was an age of recovery from the disasters of the fourteenth century, affecting both politics and art.
        New View of Human Beings: A new perspective emerged emphasizing individual ability.
        Classical Study: Focus on the study of ancient Rome and Greek languages, literature, arts, and the use of reason.
        Freedom of Thought: Support for freedom of thought and a questioning mind.
        Vernacular Language: The use of the vernacular (national language, such as Italian and English) in writing, rather than Latin.
    Economic Impacts (Linked to Age of Exploration):
        Europeans gained huge profits from local trade networks, enhancing the globalizing process.
        With subsequent colonial conquest, the voyages promoted the faster development of capitalism.
    Outstanding Persons and Works:
        Literature:
            Dante Alighieri (Italian): Wrote Divine Comedy.
            Thomas More (Englishman): Wrote Utopia.
            Cervantes (Spaniard): Wrote Don Quixote.
            Martin Luther: Translated the Holy Bible into German.
            William Shakespeare (Englishman): Produced various great plays.
        Arts:
            Leonardo da Vinci (Italian): Painted Last Supper and Mona Lisa.
            Michael Angelo (Italian): Painted The Creation of Adam and created statues of David, Moses, and the Virgin & the dead Christ.
        Science:
            Vesalius (Belgian): Studied human anatomy.
            Nicholas Copernicus (Polish): Declared that the Earth revolved around the Sun, challenging the previous geocentric view.
    Influence on Modern Society and Culture:
        Brought about a new focus on humanism.
        Resulted in a turning away from the dominant ideas put forth by the Church.
        The dawning recognition of human potential and scientific inquiry profoundly shaped world history.
        Still influences contemporary societies' culture today.

4.6 The Reformation

    This section explores the religious movement that led to the formation of Protestantism, its key figures, the Catholic Church's response, and the conflicts that ensued.

    Definition: The Reformation was a religious movement that resulted in the emergence of Protestantism from the Catholic Church.
    Time Period: Began at the beginning of the 16th century.
    Origin and Leadership: It started in Germany, led by Martin Luther in 1517.
    Causes: The Reformation had diverse causes, including:
        Social factors
        Political factors
        Religious factors
        Economic factors
    Principles of Protestant Reformers: They supported the principles of capitalism, individualism, and nation-states.
    Establishment of Protestant Churches:
        Jean Calvin (French Protestant, lived in Switzerland): Established a Calvinist Protestant church in Switzerland.
            Calvinist followers in France were known as Huguenots.
        John Knox (Scottish): Founded his type of Calvinist Protestantism, called Presbyterian, in 1560.
        King Henry VIII and Queen Elizabeth I (England): Separated the English Protestant Church from the Roman Catholic Church in England, establishing a national church called the Anglican Church.
    The Counter-Reformation:
        Protestantism compelled the Catholic Church to initiate the Counter-Reformation (also known as the Catholic Counter-Reformation).
        The Catholic Church undertook reforms in response to the Protestant challenge.
        Society of Jesus (Jesuits): A missionary organization formed in Spain by Ignatius Loyola in 1534.
            Its purpose was to reform Catholics in America, Asia, and Africa.
            Members, called Jesuits, were very active in the Americas.
    Conflicts and Wars:
        Anti-Catholics were attacked and some were even killed.
        The Peasants' Wars of Germany against Catholics occurred between 1524 and 1525.
        The Reformation played an essential role in the Thirty Years' War (1618-1648).
        The Peace of Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War in Germany in 1648.

4.7 Industrial Revolution

    This section defines the Industrial Revolution as a shift from manual labor to machine-based manufacturing, tracing its origins, technological advancements, and spread across the globe.

    Definition: The term Industrial Revolution signifies the invention and use of machines in place of hard labor.
        Machines now performed work previously done by hand with simple tools and equipment.
    Economic Shift: Europe experienced a fundamental shift from an economy based on farming and handicrafts to one based on manufacturing by machine in factories.
    Origin and Spread:
        Began in Great Britain in the 1780s.
        Took several decades to spread to other Western nations.
    New Power Source: Steam Power:
        The machines of the Industrial Revolution were operated by a new form of power known as steam power, replacing earlier wind and water power.
        Key Developers of Steam Engines:
            Savry (1698)
            Newcomer (c.1705-1711)
            James Watt (1782-84): Significantly improved steam engines and adapted them for operating all kinds of machinery, including factory machines.
    Applications of Steam Power:
        Operating various machinery in factories.
        Transport:
            First steamboat on rivers.
            Later, steamships at sea.
            Land transport: railways (the first railway locomotive was built in 1804).
    Great Britain's Industrial Dominance:
        By the mid-nineteenth century, Great Britain had become the world's first and richest industrial nation.
        Its cotton industry alone in 1850 was equal in size to the industries of all other European countries combined.
    International Spread of Industrial Revolution:
        Also occurred in the United States in 1800.
        While Britain led in the early and mid-nineteenth century, countries like the United States eventually surpassed Britain in industrial production.
        The Industrial Revolution spread to the rest of Europe at different times and speeds.
        The first countries to industrialize in continental Europe were Belgium, France, and Germany.

Unit Summary

    This summary consolidates the main themes and developments discussed across the Middle Ages and Early Modern World.

    The Dark Ages in Europe were followed by feudal society, where the Catholic Church and feudal lords controlled land and people, thus holding power. The economy was primarily agricultural with increasing productivity.
    Simultaneously, the Byzantine Empire, an Orthodox imperial autocracy, continued in parts of Europe and Western Asia until its fall to the Ottoman Turks in 1453.
    Islam arose in the Arabian Peninsula with the monotheist revelations of Prophet Mohammed. After his death, Arabs expanded to create a vast and powerful empire and civilization under the Umayyad and Abbasid Caliphs.
    The Ottoman Sultanate continued this Islamic Civilization, becoming a superpower of its time.
    In China, a distinct Civilization developed, an imperial autocracy governed by highly educated Confucian scholars. Key to Chinese historical understanding were the concepts of the dynastic cycle and the "Mandate of Heaven."
    In the early modern period, Europe saw the mass population living in poverty, but towns revived as centers of production and exchange. The Crusades contributed to the revival of trade in the Middle Ages.
    Renaissance Italy's city-states were centers of political, economic, and social life, emphasizing individual ability and classical learning.
    Martin Luther's religious reforms led to the emergence of Protestantism from the Catholic Church.
    The Age of Discoveries enhanced the pace of globalization among the world community.
