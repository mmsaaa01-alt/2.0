UNIT SEVEN: STATES, PRINCIPALITIES, POPULATION MOVEMENTS AND INTERACTIONS IN ETHIOPIA

7.1 THE CHRISTIAN KINGDOM UNDER THE RESTORED "SOLOMONIC" DYNASTY
Restoration of the "Solomonic" Dynasty:
- Fall of Zagwe: The Zagwe Dynasty fell primarily due to opposition from its tributary region of Amhara (present-day southern Wollo and northern Shewa), with local ruler Yikuno Amlak (r. 1270-85) seizing power around 1270 and initiating the Ethiopian Medieval Period (1270-1855).
- Legitimacy & Kibre Negest: To justify their rule, Yikuno Amlak claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba through the last Aksumite king, Dil Naod, framing Zagwe rulers as "illegitimate." These claims were documented in the 14th-century book Kibre Negest.
- Roving Capitals: The state's center shifted to Shewa, characterized by "moving" or "roving" capitals that relocated frequently to obtain fresh provisions and suppress rebellions, until the founding of Gondar in 1636.

Basic Features, Administration, and Economy:
- Territorial Expansion: Initiated vigorously by King Amde Tsiyon (r. 1314-44), expanding the kingdom through the conquest of Damot (1316/17), Gojjam (1323/24), and parts of the northern territories.
- The Gult System: A feudal administration system where administrative and military officials (Bale Gult) were granted the right to levy tribute (in kind) and utilize corvee labor from Rist landholders, acting as local governors and judges.
- Succession Solution: Around 1300, Amba Gishen (Debre Negest) was established as a mountain fortress where all male royal descendants (except the reigning king's sons) were confined to prevent bloody power struggles.

Wars Against Muslim Sultanates:
- Ifat Conflict: Triggered by economic competition over the Zeyla trade routes and the arrest of Amde Tsiyon's commercial agent, leading to the defeat of Sultan Sabr ad-Din I in 1332 and Ifat's reduction to a tributary state.
- Rise of Adal: The Walasma rulers later moved east to found Adal around 1380. Following prolonged struggles, Sultan Ahmed Badlay revived Adal before his defeat in 1445, marking the peak of Christian highland predominance.

7.2 THE EXPANSION OF ISLAM AND THE EMERGENCE OF MUSLIM SULTANATES
Introduction and Trade:
- Spread of Islam: Driven by trade and Islamic expansion via the Red Sea coast and, more importantly, the Port of Zeyla (Gulf of Aden), where Muslim merchants peacefully introduced Islam to interior pastoral communities.
- Zeyla Trade: The port of Zeyla served as the primary hub for internal and external trade, connecting diverse states and generating substantial royal revenue for the Christian highland kingdom.
- Major Sultanates: Included Shewa (the earliest), Ifat, Fatagar, Dawaro, Darha, Bali, Hadya, Arababni, Sharkha, and Adal, whose rulers held the title of sultan and exercised both religious and political powers.

Socio-Economic and Political Conditions of Key Sultanates:
- Ifat: Emerged in the mid-13th century in the lowlands east of the Shewan plateau under the Walasma dynasty, monopolizing the Zeyla trade routes before becoming tributary to Amde Tsiyon.
- Hadya: Occupied the westernmost territory around the Gibe River and Lake Hawasa, ruled by a Garad, known for rich natural resources, mixed farming, and frequent rebellions against imperial rule.
- Bali, Sharkha, Dawaro, Fatagar, Darha, and Arababni: Flourished along southeastern trade routes as agricultural and commercial centers before being incorporated into the Christian highland kingdom's tributary network.

7.3 POLITICAL AND SOCIO-ECONOMIC CONDITIONS OF THE WESTERN, SOUTHERN AND CENTRAL STATES
- Kingdom of Gojjam: Inhabited by the Agew south of Lake Tana, reduced to tributary status by Amde Tsiyon, and gradually Christianized through monastic networks.
- Bizamo: Located on the southern bend of the Abay River, inhabited by Omotic speakers (Shinasha), later disrupted by the Macha Oromo migration.
- Wajj and Ganz: Polities situated around Lake Ziway and the Rift Valley that paid tribute to the Christian kingdom and were later occupied by the Oromo.
- Ennarya and the Gonga Peoples: Ennarya, situated north of the Gojeb River, was a major source of pure gold trade before being defeated by the Limmu Oromo around 1710. The Gonga peoples (including Kafa, Shekka, and Shinasha) built elaborate state systems and defensive trench/stone fortifications.

7.4 RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN THE CHRISTIAN HIGHLAND KINGDOM AND THE SULTANATE OF ADAL (1520S - 1559)
Conflict and Rise of Imam Ahmad:
- Imam Ahmad ibn-Ibrahim al-Ghazi (Gragn): Rose to leadership in Adal in the early 1520s, unifying the warring Afar, Somali, and Harari communities to launch a campaign aimed at regaining Adal's independence and controlling trade routes.
- Battle of Shimbra Kure (March 1529): Imam Ahmad decisively defeated King Libne Dingil's army, leading to the annexation of Dawaro, Bali, and southern states between 1531 and 1533, followed by the conquest of northern provinces up to Aksum by 1335.

Foreign Intervention and Final Battles:
- Portuguese Assistance: King Libne Dingil's successor, Gelawdewos (r. 1540-59), received 400 Portuguese soldiers led by Christovao da Gama in 1541.
- Ottoman Support: Adal received 900 musketeers and cannon troops from the Ottoman Turks, resulting in the defeat and death of Christovao da Gama at the Battle of Wefla.
- Battle of Woyna Dega (February 1543): Gelawdewos led a combined Ethiopian and Portuguese force that decisively defeated Imam Ahmad, who was shot and killed by a Portuguese musketeer.
- Consequences: The two-decade conflict caused immense material destruction, weakened the Christian highland kingdom's centralized army, disrupted the Zeyla trade, and facilitated population mixing.

7.5 POPULATION MOVEMENTS IN THE ETHIOPIAN REGION
Somali, Afar, and Argoba Movements:
- Somali: Moved into the interior of the Horn from coastal areas (Zeyla, Berbera, Mogadishu) and formed a significant part of Adal's military forces.
- Afar: Inhabited northeastern Ethiopia, moving towards the middle Awash due to drought, famine, and conflict avoidance, while fighting alongside Imam Ahmad.
- Argoba: Early Muslim converts and trade agents in the region whose distribution was profoundly shaped by medieval trade routes and conflicts.

The Oromo Population Movement:
- Causes: Initiated in the 16th century from the Bale highlands, driven by environmental and economic pressures (population and cattle growth causing pasture shortages) and weakened Christian/Adal frontiers.
- Directions: The Borana confederacy moved toward central and southwestern Ethiopia, while the Barantu confederacy moved northeasterly toward the Awash Valley, Harar, and Wollo.
- Consequences: Destroyed or heavily influenced pre-existing medieval kingdoms, triggered socio-cultural exchanges, and integrated conquered communities through the Moggassa and Guddifacha institutions.

The Gadaa System:
- Structure: An indigenous democratic socio-political system (recognized by UNESCO in 2016 as World Cultural Heritage) regulating Oromo political, economic, social, and religious life through age-based Gadaa grades spanning an 8-year cycle.
- Government: Governed by elected officials (including the Abbaa Bokku/Abbaa Gadaa, Abbaa Sera, and Abbaa Duulaa) operating within the Chaffee assembly, adhering to principles of term limits and the removal of corrupt leaders (Buqqisu).
- Siinqee: A parallel women's institution promoting gender equality and participating in peace-making processes.
