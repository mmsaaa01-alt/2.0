UNIT NINE: THE AGE OF REVOLUTIONS, 1789 TO 1815

9.1. THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION
Defining the Revolution and Precursors:
- Definition: A profound shift from hand production to machine manufacturing that began in England in the mid-18th century, transforming industries like textiles, iron, and transportation[cite: 9].
- Agricultural Revolution: Paved the way through enclosures (wealthy landowners buying and fencing larger fields) and scientific farming methods (the seed drill invented around 1701, crop rotation, and improved livestock breeding)[cite: 9].

Why England Led:
- Factors: Possessed an abundant workforce, extensive natural resources (water power, coal, iron ore, rivers, and harbors), an expanding economy with a developed banking system, political stability, and all essential factors of production (land, labor, and capital)[cite: 9].

Inventions and Industrialization:
- Key Textile Inventions: John Kay's Flying Shuttle (1733), James Hargreaves' Spinning Jenny (c. 1764), Richard Arkwright's Water Frame (1769), Samuel Crompton's Spinning Mule (1779), and Edmund Cartwright's Power Loom (1787)[cite: 9].
- Factory System & Cotton Gin: Bulky machines led to the factory system near rivers; Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) drastically sped up cotton seed removal and increased plantation demand[cite: 9].

Improvements in Transportation:
- Steam Engine & Railways: James Watt improved the steam engine (1765); Robert Fulton built the steamboat *Clermont* (1807); George Stephenson launched the first railroad line (1822), driving industrial growth, urbanization, and job creation[cite: 9].

Social and Economic Impacts:
- Challenges & Urbanization: Initial phases brought unhealthy working conditions, long hours, pollution, and child labor, accompanied by rapid urbanization and class tensions (including the Luddite machine-breaking movement)[cite: 9].
- Long-Term Benefits: Increased production, national wealth, higher standards of living, and expanded educational opportunities[cite: 9].

Philosophies of Industrialization:
- Laissez-faire Economics: Advocated free markets and no government interference, defended by Adam Smith in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) through the laws of self-interest, competition, and supply and demand[cite: 9].
- Socialism & Marxism: Socialism argued for public ownership of production and government planning; Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published *The Communist Manifesto* (1848), analyzing the class struggle between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat[cite: 9].

9.2. THE AMERICAN WAR OF INDEPENDENCE
Genesis of the Conflict:
- Tensions: Following victory in the Seven Years' War (1754–1763), Britain imposed direct taxes like the Stamp Act (1765) to pay war debts, sparking the cry of "Taxation Without Representation" and protests like the Boston Tea Party (1773)[cite: 9].

Road to Independence:
- Revolution Starts: Armed conflict began at Lexington and Concord on April 19, 1775, with George Washington commanding the colonial army[cite: 9].
- Declaration of Independence: Drafted by Thomas Jefferson and issued on July 4, 1776, by the Second Continental Congress, firmly grounding colonial separation in John Locke's Enlightenment ideas of natural rights and the social contract[cite: 9].

Course, Outcome, and Effects:
- Victory: Aided by strong motivation, British tactical errors, and crucial French military and financial alliances (from 1778), American and French forces trapped Lord Cornwallis at Yorktown (1781)[cite: 9].
- Global Impact: Created the United States, liberated the colonies from British rule, and deeply influenced later revolutions in France and Latin America, though it failed to abolish slavery[cite: 9].

9.3. THE FRENCH REVOLUTION
The Old Regime and Causes:
- Social Structure: Divided into the First Estate (Catholic clergy), Second Estate (nobility), and Third Estate (commoners, peasants, and the bourgeoisie who bore heavy taxation)[cite: 9].
- Core Causes: Absolute monarchy under Louis XVI, severe state bankruptcy caused by deficit spending, poor harvests, and the spread of Enlightenment philosophe ideas[cite: 9].

Phases of the Revolution:
- National Assembly (1789–1791): Formed by the Third Estate after the Tennis Court Oath; marked by the storming of the Bastille on July 14, 1789, the Women's March on Versailles, and the adoption of the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* ("liberty, equality, fraternity")[cite: 9].
- Legislative Assembly (1791–1792): Established under the 1791 Constitution as a limited monarchy, facing opposition from émigrés, the Church, and invading foreign armies[cite: 9].
- The Convention (1792–1795): Established the First French Republic, executed Louis XVI (January 21, 1793) and Marie Antoinette, and endured the Reign of Terror (1793–1794) led by Maximilien Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety until Robespierre's execution on July 28, 1794[cite: 9].
- The Directory (1795–1799): Established by the 1795 Constitution with a five-member executive branch, plagued by corruption, economic struggles, and the rise of French nationalism[cite: 9].

9.4. THE PERIOD OF NAPOLEON BONAPARTE
Rise to Power and Reforms:
- Rise: Seized supreme power on December 25, 1799, beginning the Consulate period and ruling as an enlightened despot[cite: 9].
- Major Reforms: Introduced the Napoleonic Code (providing legal unity, property protection, and permanent revolutionary achievements while restricting women's rights), signed the Concordat of 1801 with the Church, established the Bank of France (1800), and reformed public education[cite: 9].

Military Campaigns and Fall:
- Expansion & Continental System: Crowned himself emperor in 1804, built the largest European empire since Roman times, and instituted the Continental System to cripple Britain's economy after the naval defeat at the Battle of Trafalgar (1805)[cite: 9].
- Decline & Congress of Vienna: Following the disastrous invasion of Russia (1812) and defeat at the Battle of Leipzig (1813), Napoleon was abdicated, escaped during the Hundred Days, and suffered final defeat at the Battle of Waterloo (June 1815) before being exiled to St. Helena[cite: 9].
- Congress of Vienna (1814–1815): Led by figures like Klemens von Metternich, European powers redrew the map of Europe based on the principles of legitimacy, compensation, and the balance of power[cite: 9].
