UNIT EIGHT: POLITICAL, SOCIAL AND ECONOMIC PROCESSES IN ETHIOPIA, MID 16TH TO MID-19TH CENTURY

8.1. PEOPLES AND STATES OF SOUTHERN, WESTERN AND EASTERN ETHIOPIA
Omotic Peoples and States:
- Under the Tato System: The Kingdom of Yem (ruled by the Amno), the Kingdom of Kafa (ruled by the Tato from the Minjo clan, defended by the 'Hiriyoo' trench and known for resisting Oromo expansion), and the Kingdom of Shekka (ruled by the Tato from the Bushasho dynasty).
- Under the Kawo/Kati System: The Kingdom of Wolaita (ruled by the Kawo with the 'Goqa' warrior class and 'Ijajo Kalla' walls), the Kingdom of Dawuro (ruled by the Kati, featuring the 'Kati Halala Kalla' defensive walls), the Kingdom of Gamo (independent village units called 'Darie' ruled by a Kawo), and the Kingdom of Gofa (dual administration with landed chiefs and rain kings).

Cushitic States and Peoples:
- Sera-Woma System: Eastern highland Cushitic groups (Sidama, Hadya, Kambata, Tambaro, Halaba, Qabena) used the Sera-Woma system for governance. The Sidama used the 'Luwa' age-grade rotation system on Fiche Chamballalla, while Kambata used the 'Hambarcho' judiciary council.
- Other Peoples: The Gedeo utilized the 'Ballee' council of elders; the Konso used the 'Hayyota' council; the Gurage relied on the 'Yajoka Qicha' and 'Gordanna Sera' assemblies; and the Silt'e used the 'Malga Sera'.

Western States:
- Gibe and Leqa Oromo States: Transformed from the Gadaa system into monarchies around 1800 due to trade, Islam, and the rise of powerful Abbaa Duulaa. Five Gibe monarchies emerged (Limmu-Enarya, Gumma, Gomma, Jimma-Kakka, and Gera). The Leeqaa Oromo formed Leeqaa Naqamtee and Leeqaa Qellam.
- Benishangul-Gumuz & Gambella: The Benishangul region formed Muslim Sheikhdoms (Assossa, Benishangul, Khomosha) driven by Sudanese trade and Islam. Gambella's ethnic groups (Anywaa, Nuer, Majang, Opuo, Komo) maintained traditional headmanships and spiritual leadership roles.

Eastern States:
- Harar: Capital of Adal remnants; the Jugol wall was built by Emir Nur in 1552-53, and an independent Harari dynasty under Emir Ali Dawud emerged in 1647, making Harar a prominent trade and Islamic center until Egyptian occupation (1875-1885).
- Afar & Somali: The Afar established four sultanates (Tajura, Rahayito, Aussa, Goba'ad), while the Somali maintained the 'Shir' council of elders for governance and resource allocation.

8.2. INSTABILITY VERSUS CONSOLIDATION IN THE CHRISTIAN HIGHLAND KINGDOM, 1559-1855
Attempts at Consolidation & Catholic Controversy:
- Political Shifts: The kingdom shifted its center to the Lake Tana environs under King Minas, facing continuous challenges from Oromo expansion, Ottoman Turkish occupation of Massawa (1557), and warring warlords.
- Imposition of Catholicism: Jesuit missionaries pushed for Catholic conversion. Emperor Susinyos officially converted in 1622, sparking violent peasant rebellions and civil wars. He abdicated in 1632 in favor of his son, Fasiledes, who restored Orthodox Christianity, expelled the Jesuits, and introduced a "closed-door policy" backed by alliances with Muslim Red Sea states.

The Gondarine Period (1636-1769):
- Golden Age: Centered at Gondar (founded by Fasiledes around 1636), it was an era of magnificent castle architecture, vibrant trade, and flourishing church education, art, and literature under rulers like Fasiledes, Yohannis I, and Iyasu I.
- Decline & Oromo Influence: Following Iyasu I's murder, power struggles escalated. Etege Mintiwab and her Quara kinsmen faced growing rivalry from the Yejju Oromo faction tied to Bersabeh and Iyasu II, paving the way for regional fragmentation.

The Zemene Mesafint (Era of the Warlords, 1769-1855):
- Characteristics: A period marked by the collapse of central imperial authority, continuous civil wars among regional lords for the 'Rasbitweded' title, and severe economic decline affecting peasantry, trade, and agriculture.
- End of the Era: Kasa Hailu launched military campaigns, defeating regional lords at Gur Amba, Gorgora Bichen, Ayshal (1853), and Deresge, and was crowned Emperor Tewodros II in 1855.

The Kingdom of Shewa & European Contacts:
- Shewa's Independence: Founded by Negasi Kirstos, Shewa remained stable and independent from northern civil wars, reaching its climax under Nigus Sahle Selassie (r. 1813-1847), who welcomed European envoys and signed trade treaties.
- European Explorers: Early 19th-century travelers (Henry Salt, Abbadie brothers, Walter Plowden, W.C. Harris) visited Ethiopia for trade, science, and missionary work, paving the way for eventual colonial intervention.
