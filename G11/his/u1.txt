UNIT ONE: HISTORY, HISTORIOGRAPHY, AND HUMAN EVOLUTION

1.1. HISTORY AND HISTORIOGRAPHY
- History Definition: A branch of knowledge dealing with all past thoughts, sayings, and deeds of people; an organized study based on evidence interpretation.
- Scope & Etymology: Covers pre-history (before writing began ~4000 B.C.). The word comes from Greek "istoria", meaning inquiry or knowledge through investigation.
- Importance: Learn from past achievements, understand society to forecast the future, establish identity, and build problem-solving skills.
- Abuse of History: Misused to justify the present or ruined by judging the past using present values.
- History vs. Sciences: Unlike natural sciences, history lacks controlled experiments and involves value judgments, but both use systematic methods and evidence.

Historiography:
- Definition: The history of historical writing and research techniques.
- Greek Figures: Herodotus ("father of history", Greco-Persian wars) and Thucydides (Peloponnesian War).
- Modern Academic History: Developed in the 1800s. Leopold von Ranke is the "father of modern historiography".
- Key Elements: Sources are decisive, proper interpretation avoids misrepresentation, and objective writing presents unbiased truth.
- Ethiopian Historiography: Indigenous tradition from the 14th to early 20th centuries via chroniclers and hagiographers. Job Ludolf founded European Ethiopian studies. Past flaws included northern bias, political focus, and lack of objectivity.

Sources of History:
- Primary Sources: Direct relation to the event (monuments, coins, diaries, chronicles, eyewitness accounts).
- Secondary Sources: No direct relation; historical reconstructions like history books.
- Oral Sources: Primary (eyewitness) or secondary (hearsay). Critical examination ensures authenticity.

Periodization:
- Definition: Dividing the past into chronologically arranged periods.
- Approaches: Human Behaviour (Savagery, Barbarism, Civilization), Tool Making (Stone, Bronze, Iron Age), Economic Life (Hunting/gathering, Farming, Industry).
- Divisions: Pre-history vs History split by writing (~4000 B.C.). Ethiopian history spans Ancient (~1000 B.C.–1270 A.D.), Medieval (1270–1855 A.D.), and Modern (1855 A.D.–present).

1.2. ORIGIN OF HUMAN BEINGS
Theories of Origin:
- Creationism: Universe, Earth, and life were created by divine supernatural forces.
- Scientific View (Evolution): Simple life forms evolved into complex creatures over millions of years. Charles Darwin published "Origin of Species by Natural Selection" in 1859.

Stages of Human Evolution:
- Hominids: Family of humans and extinct human-like creatures with enlarged brains and bipedalism (shared common ancestor with apes 8–5 million years ago).
- Australopithecines ("Southern Ape"): Evolved in Africa ~6 million years ago. Small canine teeth, bipedalism, small brains (390–550 cc). Species include A. afarensis (Lucy, Ethiopia).
- Genus Homo: Originated in Africa ~2.5 million years ago with larger brains and smaller jaws:
  - Homo habilis: Lived ~2 million years ago; made first stone tools (Oldowan technology).
  - Homo erectus: First to migrate out of Africa; used Acheulean hand axes and mastered fire ~1.5 million years ago. Ethiopian sites include Omo Basin, Melka-Kunture.
  - Homo sapiens: Archaic (300,000–600,000 years ago, Bodo) and Modern (at least 100,000 years old, Kibish and Porc Epic).

Early Cultural Developments:
- The Stone Age: First stage of civilization (2.5 million to 5,000 years ago).
  - Lower Paleolithic: Oldowan (Homo habilis) and Acheulean (Homo erectus) technologies.
  - Middle Paleolithic: Smaller flake tools, spear points (~200,000 years ago). Sites: Gademotta.
  - Upper Paleolithic / Later Stone Age: Standardized blade technology and microliths for composite tools (bows and arrows).
- Neolithic Revolution: Shift from hunting/gathering to food production via plant/animal domestication after 11,000 years ago. Brought sedentary life, pottery, millstones, division of labor, and early conflicts. In Ethiopia, indigenous crops include teff, enset, noog, and coffee (evidence at Gobedra, Lalibela Cave, Lake Beseka).

1.3. THE EMERGENCE OF STATES
Theories of State Emergence:
- Divine Right Theory: Priests combining religious and political power evolved into kings.
- War Leadership: Successful war leaders gained followers and became kings.
- Control of Trade Routes: Chiefs collecting tolls/protection taxes grew wealthy and built royal power.
- Oriental Despotism Theory: Strong despotic states emerged in river valleys (Egypt on Nile, Mesopotamia on Tigris/Euphrates) to manage large-scale irrigation.

Main Features of Early States:
- Agricultural Foundation: Food surplus supported farmers, artisans, officials, and soldiers.
- Social Classes & Civilization: Populations divided into specialized classes. Civilized states featured cities, literacy, technology, monumental architecture, and organized religion.
- Divine Authority: Emperors viewed as divine representatives with fixed social hierarchies by birth.
