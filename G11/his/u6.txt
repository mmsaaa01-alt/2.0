UNIT SIX: AFRICA AND THE OUTSIDE WORLD: 1500-1880S

6.1. MEDIEVAL AFRICAN STATES
North East and Northern Africa:
- The Funj Sultanate: Following Arab-Islam expansion in Nubia, cattle-keeping pastoral Funj peoples overthrew Arab supremacy and established the Funj Sultanate with its center at Sennar in 1504 under Amara Dunqas. It expanded into Nilotic Sudan, experienced cultural Africanization, and fought major battles with the Christian kingdom of Ethiopia (such as King Susenyos's raids and the defeat of King Iyasu II's army) before falling to Mohammed Ali's Egypt in 1821.
- The Moroccan Sultanate: Unified under the Sa'dids, Morocco reached its peak under Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur (r. 1578–1603) after driving out the Portuguese. In 1591, al-Mansur sent a firearms-armed army across the Sahara that captured Gao and Timbuktu, destroying the Songhay Empire, though internal struggles led to fragmentation until the Alawid Dynasty restored unity in 1669.

Forest Region States of West Africa:
- Benin Empire: Built by the Edo people in southern Nigeria, reaching its peak in the late 15th century through ocean trade with the Portuguese and Dutch (exchanging pepper and dyed cotton for manufactures) and renowned for bronze sculptures.
- Oyo Empire: Formed in the 17th century by united Yoruba chieftaincies, it flourished through agriculture and crafts before disintegrating in the 19th century due to Fulani invasions and jihads led by Usman dan Fodio.
- Dahomey: Established as independent in 1625, it became a leading participant in the Trans-Atlantic slave trade and was famous for training elite female military regiments known as the Abomey Amazons.
- Ashante: Unified at the end of the 17th century by the Ashantehene from the Akan people, building prosperity through gold, kola nuts, and slave trade with the Portuguese at the Gold Coast.

Eastern, West, and South-Central African Kingdoms:
- Buganda & Rwanda: Buganda flourished under the kabaka northwest of Lake Victoria, while Rwanda formed through the integration of Tutsi cattle keepers and Hutu farmers.
- The Kingdom of Kongo: Emerged around 1400 south of the Congo River under the Manikongo. It participated heavily in the Trans-Atlantic slave trade under kings like Afonso I, but collapsed by the late 17th century due to internal unrest from slave raiding and Jaga invasions.
- Torwa, Mutapa, and Rozwi: Following the abandonment of Great Zimbabwe, successor states emerged including the Torwa state, the Mwene Mutapa Empire (founded by Mutota, dominating gold and ivory trade in the Zambezi valley), and the Rozwi Empire established by Changamire in the 1690s after expelling the Portuguese.

Southern Africa:
- The Zulu Kingdom: Developed in the late 18th and 19th centuries amidst intertribal upheavals and Boer/British encroachment, attaining dominance under Shaka Zulu (r. 1818–1828) before falling to British conquest in the 1860s.

The Ottoman Conquest of North Africa:
- Conquests: Driven by the Ottoman-Spanish conflict, the Ottomans conquered Egypt in 1517 and extended control over Tripolitania, Tunisia, and Algiers by the end of the 16th century, introducing the millet system and provincial administration via pashas and deys before local military rulers gained practical independence in the 18th century.

6.2. CONTACTS OF AFRICA WITH THE OUTSIDE WORLD
Early Contacts along Coasts:
- Portuguese Initiative: Sponsored by Prince Henry the Navigator in the 15th century to gain direct access to West African gold and bypass Arab/Turkish trade blockades, establishing footholds in Ceuta, Madeira, Cape Verde, and along the West and East African coasts.
- West-Central & East Africa: Diogo Cao reached the Congo River in 1482; Portuguese allied with the Kongo and Ndongo kingdoms (encountering fierce resistance from Queen Nzinga Mbande). In East Africa, Vasco da Gama's 1497-1498 voyage led to Portuguese destruction of Swahili city-states' trade, though they were later expelled north of Mozambique by Omani Arabs and the Rozwi.

---

6.3. SLAVERY AND SLAVE TRADE IN AFRICA
Slavery Inside Africa:
- Nature: Indigenous slavery existed from ancient times, acquiring slaves via warfare, kidnapping, and tribute. Slaves served as domestic servants, agricultural labor, miners, soldiers, and administrators, and were often integrated into households with certain legal rights and protections.

External Slave Trades:
- Trans-Saharan Slave Trade: Existed from the 8th to late 16th centuries, moving West African slaves and gold to North Africa in exchange for salt.
- Red Sea & Indian Ocean Slave Trades: Ancient routes exporting slaves from inland regions (such as Ethiopia, Nubia, and East Africa) to the Middle East, India, and French plantation islands like Mauritius and Reunion.
- Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade: Triggered by Columbus's discovery of America in 1492, lasting from the 16th to mid-19th centuries to supply "free labor" for American plantations. It operated through three phases (Piratic, Monopolistic/Triangular Trade linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas, and Free Trade).

Abolition and Effects:
- Abolition: Driven by African resistance, European humanitarian movements, and the economic shift of the Industrial Revolution making slave labor unprofitable, leading Britain to ban it in 1807 and enforce abolition via naval patrols.
- Effects on Africa: Nearly 18 million slaves were shipped across the four trades (12 million via the Atlantic). It caused severe demographic loss, political instability, and agricultural disruption, though it temporarily enriched certain states like Dahomey and Ashante through trade participation.

---

6.4. THE LEGITIMATE TRADE
- Shift: Following the abolition of the slave trade, European powers shifted to "Legitimate Trade" in West Africa, importing raw materials crucial for industrializing economies—most notably palm oil (used as machine lubricant), gum arabic, and groundnuts.
- Consequences: While controlled largely by African rulers using internal slave labor, it primarily benefited a small elite, imported European manufactured goods that undermined local economies, and intensified trade competition that catalyzed the "Scramble for Africa."

---

6.5. THE WHITE SETTLEMENT IN SOUTH AFRICA
- Initial Settlement: Established in 1652 by the Dutch East India Company at the Cape of Good Hope as a refreshment and provisioning station for ships traveling to the Far East.
- Expansion & Conflict: The introduction of permanent Boer (Afrikaner) farmers in 1657 led to the Khoi-Dutch Wars and the displacement of indigenous Khoisan and Bantu populations. Following British takeover of the Cape Colony in 1795, dissatisfied Boers undertook the "Great Trek" in the 1830s-1840s to establish interior territories, ultimately leading to intense conflicts with the Zulus and final British control after 19th-century mineral discoveries.

---

6.6. EUROPEAN EXPLORERS AND MISSIONARIES: 1770-1870
European Explorers:
- Motives: The transition to "legitimate trade" spurred a demand for geographic and economic intelligence regarding Africa's interior, river systems, and raw materials.
- Key Expeditions: Financed by bodies like the African Association (founded 1788), explorers like Burton, Speke, and Grant traced the source of the Nile, David Livingston explored the Zambezi, and Henry Morton Stanley charted the Congo River, paving the way for colonial conquest.

European Missionaries:
- Roles: 19th-century Protestant and Catholic missionaries arrived in West and Southern Africa. Due to slow initial conversion rates, they frequently appealed to their home governments for military and political protection (such as in Namibia and Buganda), directly smoothing the path for the formal declaration of European protectorates and colonial rule.
