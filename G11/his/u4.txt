UNIT FOUR: THE MIDDLE AGES AND EARLY MODERN WORLD, C. 500 AD-1789

4.1. THE EUROPEAN MIDDLE AGES
Fall of the Western Roman Empire:
- Decline Factors: Worsening internal problems, division into the Eastern Empire in 395 AD, and successive Germanic tribe invasions.
- Major Invasions: Visigoths into Spain (416 AD), Angles/Jutes/Saxons into Britain (450 AD), and Franks into Gaul (480s AD). Rome officially fell in 476 AD.
- Consequences: Disappearance of strong central government, destruction of trade networks, ruralization, and a decline in classical culture and learning.
- The Church: The Roman Christian church was the sole surviving major institution, providing leadership, collecting taxes, and maintaining hospitals and inns.

Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire:
- Foundation: Constantine moved the capital to Constantinople (330 AD); the East survived for a thousand years after the West's fall due to wealth and better geographical defense.
- Justinian I (r. 527–565): Expanded territory, commissioned the Justinian Code, and ordered the construction of the Hagia Sophia (532–537 AD).
- Culture & Religion: Preserved classical Greek and Roman traditions. The Great Schism of 1054 split the church into the Western Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church. The Ottoman Turks conquered Constantinople in 1453.

Western Europe:
- The Frankish Kingdom: Charles Martel founded the Carolingian dynasty and stopped the Muslim advance at the Battle of Tours (732 AD).
- Charlemagne (r. 768–814): Reunited much of the Western Roman Empire, was crowned Emperor of the Romans in 800 AD, and revived learning. After his death, the empire fragmented and faced invasions from Muslims, Normans (Vikings), and Magyars.

---

4.2. MAIN FEATURES OF THE MIDDLE AGES
Feudalism and the Manorial System:
- Feudalism: A system where lords granted land (fiefs) to lesser lords (vassals) in exchange for loyalty and military service.
- Manorial System: Manors were self-sufficient economic centers. Serfs were bound to the land and required to work the lord's demesne in exchange for protection and a plot to farm.

Feudal Society:
- Social Hierarchy: Divided rigidly into the privileged upper class (kings, nobles, clergy, knights) and unprivileged lower class (peasants and serfs).
- Knights & Chivalry: Mounted warriors bound by a code of conduct (chivalry) requiring bravery, loyalty, and protection of the weak.

Power of the Church:
- Christendom: The Church was the largest landowner and held supreme political influence, establishing the College of Cardinals in 1059 to elect popes.

---

4.3. REVIVAL OF TRADE, TOWNS, AND CULTURE
Towns and Middle Class:
- Economic Revival: Growth of trade in the 11th century spurred town expansion and gave rise to the middle class (merchants, traders, artisans).
- Guilds: Associations of artisans and merchants that protected economic interests, regulated hours, and set quality standards and prices.

Culture and Education:
- Architecture: Transitioned from massive Romanesque structures to tall, pointed Gothic cathedrals featuring flying buttresses.
- Universities: Evolved from cathedral/monastery schools in the 1100s, with the University of Paris serving as a prominent model.

---

4.4. THE CRUSADES (1096-1270)
- Causes: Pope Urban II called for the First Crusade at the Council of Clermont (1095) after Seljuk Turks restricted Christian access to the Holy Land.
- Key Events: The First Crusade (1096–1099) captured Jerusalem; Salah al-Din (Saladin) recaptured it in 1187. The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) resulted in the looting of Constantinople by Venetian merchants.
- Impacts: Failed to retain permanent control of the Holy Land but increased trade, introduced a money economy, strengthened monarch power, and expanded the European worldview.

---

4.5. THE MIDDLE AGES IN ASIA
Rise and Expansion of Islam:
- Origins: Founded by Prophet Muhammed (born c. 570 AD), who preached monotheism. The Hijira (622 AD) marked the migration to Medina and Year 1 of the Islamic calendar.
- Sunni-Shi'ite Split: Disagreement over succession led to the split between Shi'ites (supporting Ali and hereditary Imams) and Sunnis (electing qualified leaders from Muhammed's tribe).
- Sharia: Islamic law derived from the Quran and teachings of the Prophet.

The Islamic Empire:
- Dynasties: Expanded rapidly under the first four caliphs, reaching its peak under the Umayyad Dynasty (capital at Damascus) and entering a golden age under the Abbasid Dynasty (capital at Baghdad).
- Legacy: Advanced algebra, trigonometry, medicine (Ibn Sina/Avicenna), history (Ibn Khaldun), and trade networks.

Ottoman Turkish Empire:
- Expansion: Founded by Osman I, utilizing gunpowder and cannons. Sultan Mehmet II conquered Constantinople in 1453, renaming it Istanbul.
- Governance: Reached its peak under Suleiman I ("the Magnificent"). Non-Muslims were organized into autonomous religious communities called millets.

The Dynastic Cycle in China:
- Mandate of Heaven: The traditional belief governing the rise and fall of dynasties.
- Major Dynasties: Sui (reunified China, built Grand Canal), Tang (golden age of prosperity and merit-based civil service), Song (technological innovations like gunpowder and movable type), Yuan (Mongol rule under Kublai Khan), Ming (restored native rule and naval expeditions), and Qing (Manchu rule until 1912).

4.6. DEVELOPMENT OF EARLY CAPITALISM, EXPLORATION, AND RENAISSANCE (1500-1789)
Age of Exploration and Globalization:
- Motivations: Desire for direct sea routes to Asian spices to bypass Ottoman control, curiosity, and technological advancements (compass, astrolabe).
- Key Voyages: Vasco da Gama reached India via Africa (1497–1498); Christopher Columbus reached the Americas (1492); Ferdinand Magellan's crew circumnavigated the globe (1519–1522).
- Impacts: The Columbian Exchange transferred plants, animals, culture, and devastating diseases between the New and Old Worlds, accelerating global trade.

The Renaissance:
- Core Ideas: "Rebirth" of classical Greek and Roman culture starting in Italian city-states, emphasizing humanism (focus on human potential) and secularism.
- Achievements: Realism in art by Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo; scientific inquiries by Copernicus and Galileo; and the invention of Johann Gutenberg's printing press around 1450.

The Reformation:
- Causes: Questioning of Church authority, corruption, and the sale of indulgences.
- Key Figures: Martin Luther posted his 95 Theses in 1517, arguing for salvation by faith alone. John Calvin introduced predestination, and King Henry VIII established the Anglican Church via the Act of Supremacy (1534).
- Counter-Reformation: The Catholic response included the Council of Trent, the Inquisition, and the founding of the Jesuit order.

Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment:
- Scientific Revolution (c. 1500–1700): Emphasized observation and experimentation, featuring breakthroughs by Copernicus, Galileo, and Isaac Newton.
- Enlightenment (mid-1700s): Applied reason and scientific principles to society. John Locke championed natural rights, Montesquieu advocated for the separation of powers, Voltaire supported freedom of expression, and Rousseau proposed the "general will".
