UNIT FIVE: PEOPLES AND STATES OF AFRICA TO 1500

5.1. ANCIENT AND MEDIEVAL AFRICAN STATES
African Languages: Classification and Distribution:
- Linguistic Diversity: Africa hosts between 1,000 and 2,000 languages, accounting for roughly one-third of the world's languages.
- Four Major Superfamilies: Classified into Niger-Congo, Afro-Asiatic, Nilo-Saharan, and Khoisan languages.
- Niger-Congo: The largest family, distributed across western, central, and southeastern Africa; includes Bantu tongues, Swahili, Yoruba, Kirundi, Lingala, Sesotho, and Shona.
- Afro-Asiatic: The second-largest family with 200 to 300 languages found in North and Northeast Africa (including Arabic, Somali, Amharic, Afan Oromo, Berber, and Hausa).
- Nilo-Saharan: Comprises around 80 languages spread across central, eastern, and northeastern Africa (such as Lugbara, Zarma, and Dholuo).
- Khoisan: Includes 40 to 70 languages predominantly found in Southern Africa, as well as Hadza in Tanzania and Naro in Botswana.

5.2. NORTH AFRICA
Mamluk Egypt:
- Origin & Rise: The Mamluks were a military aristocracy of Turkish slaves who ruled Egypt from approximately 1250 to 1517 AD, stepping into power after Ayyubid power struggles and successfully defeating the Crusaders (capturing King Louis IX) and the Mongols.
- Economy & Culture: Experienced significant economic growth through the Trans-Saharan Trade and Spice Trade, alongside cultural flourishing in architecture, craftsmanship, and scholarship.
- Decline: Challenged by the Portuguese and Ottoman rivalry over Red Sea trade, leading to the Ottoman conquest in 1516-1517 AD, after which the Mamluks ruled under Ottoman suzerainty until 1805.

---

5.3. THE SPREAD OF ISLAM TO NORTH AFRICA
The Maghreb Region:
- Almoravid Movement: Began in the 11th century among the nomadic Lamtuna Berber tribe under leaders like Ibn Yasin, Emir Yahiya, and Abu Bakar, establishing Marrakesh as capital in 1062 and enforcing strict Islamic principles across Morocco and western Algeria.
- Almohad Movement: Emerged in the mid-12th century under Ibn Tumart and Abdul Mumin to replace the Almoravids, eventually controlling the entire Maghreb by 1160 before falling to internal civil wars and Christian European attacks in Spain by 1269.

---

5.4. STATES IN WEST AFRICA
Empire of Ghana:
- Origin: The oldest known kingdom in Western Sudan, founded around the 4th century by the Soninke people and reaching its peak around 1000 A.D. with centers at Kumbi Saleh and Awadaghust.
- Economy & Fall: Built wealth acting as middlemen on Trans-Saharan Trade routes trading gold and salt, but collapsed after Almoravid attacks captured Awadaghust (1054) and Kumbi Saleh (1076).

Empire of Mali:
- Origin: Founded by Sundiata (r. 1230–1255) after defeating his enemies and securing control over gold fields like Wangara and Bambak.
- Peak & Islam: Reached its zenith under Mansa Musa (r. 1312–1337), who embraced Islam and undertook a famous, lavish pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324. Declined due to weak successors and external attacks from Berbers, Songhai, Mossi, and Tuareg.

Empire of Songhai:
- Rise & Expansion: Emerged in the 1400s with Gao as its capital; Sunni Ali (1464–1492) built the largest state in West Africa's history.
- Golden Age & Fall: Askia Muhammad established a Muslim dynasty, built a structured bureaucracy, and transformed Timbuktu into a major center of intellectual life. Conquered in 1591 by Moroccan invaders armed with gunpowder.

Empire of Kanem-Bornu:
- Dynasty & Territory: Ruled by the Saifuwa dynasty from the 9th to 19th centuries around Lake Chad, expanding under rulers like Mai Idris Alooma who acquired firearms from Turkish merchants.

Hausa City States:
- Location & Economy: Established in Northern Nigeria (including Kano, Katsina, Zaria, and Daura) as southern terminals of the Trans-Saharan trade specializing in crafts, agriculture, and slave provisions.
- End: Absorbed into the Sokoto Caliphate following a jihad led by Uthman Dan Fodio starting in 1804.

Spread of Islam in West Africa:
- Introduction: Began as early as the 8th century through trade connections with North Africa, taking early root in Tekrur, Ghana, Mali, and the Hausa city-states.

5.5. CENTRAL AND EASTERN AFRICA
The East Coast, Islam, and Trade:
- Zanj / Swahili Coast: A coastal strip stretching from Somalia to Mozambique characterized by a rich blend of Bantu Africans, Arabs, and Persians forming independent trading city-states like Mombasa, Malindi, and Kilwa.
- Trade Networks: Thrived on seasonal monsoon winds connecting the coast across the Indian Ocean to Arabia, Persia, India, and Ming China (visited by Admiral Zheng He's fleet in the 15th century).
- Portuguese Disruption: Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1497-1498 initiated Portuguese control attempts, contributing to the decline of the Swahili city-states.

---

5.6. SOUTHERN AFRICA
Great Zimbabwe:
- Evidence & Builders: Stonewalled palaces, towers, and enclosures built by Bantu-speaking Shona peoples between 900 and 1500 A.D., reaching its peak around 1300 A.D.
- Economy & Fall: Prospered via gold mining and Indian Ocean trade links channeled through Sofala and Kilwa, before declining due to overpopulation, civil war, trade shifts, and Portuguese incursions.

The Khoi Khoi and San:
- Khoi Khoi: Pastoralists and cattle herders who expanded into Southern Africa by the 15th century.
- San: Indigenous hunter-gatherer bands who inhabited Southern Africa for thousands of years, utilizing small bows, poison-tipped arrows, and organized family bands.

---

5.7. RELATIONSHIPS AND EXCHANGES AMONG DIFFERENT REGIONS OF AFRICA
Trans-Saharan Caravan Trade:
- Inception & Peak: Active from 500 to 1590 A.D., reaching its height between the 14th and 16th centuries during the Mali and Songhai empires.
- Commodities & Routes: Utilized camels across three main Saharan divisions to exchange North African salt, cloth, and horses for West African gold, slaves, kola nuts, and animal hides.
