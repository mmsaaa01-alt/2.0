UNIT FOUR: GLOBAL POPULATION DYNAMICS AND CHALLENGES

4.1 THE GROWTH OF WORLD POPULATION
Introduction to Global Population Dynamics:
- Rapid population growth in recent decades has strained the worldwide ecosystem, potentially leading to inadequate food supply, insufficient non-renewable resources, overexploitation of renewable resources, and environmental degradation.

The Demographic Transition Model:
- Stage 1: High fertility and mortality rates with relatively stable population growth and a triangular-shaped population pyramid.
- Stage 2: Decreasing death rates (especially among children aged 0 to 5) while birth rates remain high, resulting in significant population growth.
- Stage 3: Decreasing birth rates driven by socioeconomic changes such as urbanization and widespread contraception use, leading to slower population growth.
- Stage 4: Low birth and death rates resulting in a slowly growing population.
- Stage 5: Rebounding birth rates while death rates remain low, leading to stable or low population growth.

Global Population Growth Trends:
- The global population increased dramatically from 1 billion in 1800 to 7.7 billion in 2019.
- Projected to reach 8.5 billion in 2030, 9.7 billion in 2050, and 10.7 billion in 2100.
- Sub-Saharan Africa is expected to account for more than half of the world's population growth between 2019 and 2050, adding an estimated 1.1 billion people (a 99% increase).
- India was projected to surpass China as the world's most populated country around 2027.

Population Trends in Less Developed Countries (LDCs):
- Significant post-WWII progress in medical facilities, disease management, piped water, improved housing, and education led to a decrease in death rates.
- Rapid population increases can hinder poverty eradication, improving quality of life, combating hunger and malnutrition, and enhancing health and education systems.
- Rural-to-urban migration puts immense strain on cities, causing overcrowding, traffic congestion, inadequate sanitation, and the growth of informal communities.
- Populations in at least 55 less-developed nations are expected to shrink between 2019 and 2050 due to low fertility and emigration.

Population Trends in More Developed Countries (MDCs):
- MDCs face challenges related to low population growth, population stagnation, and aging.
- A reduced potential support ratio (the number of people aged 25-64 years compared to those aged over 65 years) creates economic pressures to maintain public health care, pensions, and social protection for the aged.

Factors Responsible for Accelerated Population Growth:
- Agricultural advancements and organized agricultural communities ensured human security and food production.
- The Industrial Revolution increased food sources, leading to higher nourishment levels and decreased vulnerability to diseases.
- Medical advancements and public health improvements drove down death rates, creating a mortality transition.
- Other contributing factors include migration, illiteracy, and cultural attitudes in rural societies toward family planning.

Measures of Fertility:
- Crude Birth Rate (CBR): The basic measurement of fertility, calculated as annual births divided by the mid-total population multiplied by 1,000, though it ignores age and sex structure.
- Total Fertility Rate (TFR): Estimates the total number of children a woman will have throughout her reproductive career based on age-specific fertility rates.
- Gross Reproductive Rate (GRR): Similar to TFR, but counts only female births to determine if a population is replacing itself.
- General Fertility Rate (GFR): Measures live births per thousand women of reproductive age (15-49 years).

Measures of Mortality:
- Crude Death Rate (CDR): The ratio of total registered deaths in a year to the total mid-year population, multiplied by 1,000.
- Infant Mortality Rate (IMR): The number of deaths of infants under the age of one year for every 1,000 live births in a given year.

International Migration:
- International migration is an economic and political process driven by push factors in source countries and pull factors in destination regions.
- Types of migration include free migration, forced migration, human trafficking, and temporary labor force relocations.
- Measures of migration include the immigration rate, emigration rate, and Net Migration Rate (NMR).

Population Policies:
- Government declarations, choices, and measures aimed at influencing population size, growth, distribution, and composition.
- Objectives include promoting economic development, social and individual welfare, managing urbanization, and addressing environmental sustainability through family planning programs.
