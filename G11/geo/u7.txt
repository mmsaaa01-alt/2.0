UNIT SEVEN: GEOGRAPHIC ISSUES AND PUBLIC CONCERNS

7.1 POPULATION DYNAMICS AND RELATED CONCERNS
Population Growth in Developing Countries (DCs):
- Developing Countries experienced their highest population growth rates between 1960 and 1990, and Sub-Saharan Africa is projected to face the most rapid population growth in the coming decades.
- Rapid population pressure and urbanization lead to severe socioeconomic and environmental challenges, including land degradation, resource depletion, biodiversity loss, chronic poverty, malnutrition, and the expansion of slums.
- Remedial measures include human capital investment, promoting healthy aging, sustainable urban planning, supporting vulnerable populations, migration management, and improving girls' and women's education.

Family Size, Education, and Income:
- Households with large family sizes are forced to spend more on education and healthcare, which can diminish quality, lead to lower investments per child, and perpetuate generational poverty.
- Global mean household sizes range from under 3 individuals in Europe and North America to over 5 individuals in Africa and the Middle East.
- Income distribution is severely uneven: wealthy countries account for 55% of the world's total income, while DCs contribute only 1%, with mean incomes in North America and the EU significantly larger than in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Population and Economic Growth Views:
- Malthusian and Neo-Malthusian Views: Thomas Malthus argued that population grows geometrically while food production increases arithmetically, leading to inevitable resource scarcity, poverty, and positive checks like war and famine.
- Anti-Malthusian Views: Marxist views attribute poverty to capitalist accumulation and a created "surplus population," while Boserupian views argue that population growth stimulates technological innovation and agricultural improvements to keep pace with demand ("necessity is the mother of invention").
- Demographic Dividend: Slowing population growth by lowering fertility rates can increase the working-age population relative to dependents, creating economic opportunities if matched with proper human capital investments and sound policies.

Unemployment and Underemployment:
- Unemployment reflects joblessness for active working persons, with DCs experiencing the highest rates due to rapid population growth and a lack of economic structures beyond agriculture.
- The Economic Dependency Ratio (EDR) relates the number of non-workers (unemployed, underemployed, and dependents) to the number of employed workers.

7.2 LAND DEGRADATION AND DESERTIFICATION
Forms and Causes of Land Degradation:
- Land degradation involves the long-term reduction of land productivity, ecological integrity, and human value through physical, chemical, and biological processes.
- Physical degradation involves compaction, crusting, and structural changes; chemical degradation includes nutrient loss, acidification, and alkalization; biological degradation includes deforestation, overgrazing, and loss of biomass.
- Causes include natural factors like volcanic eruptions and floods, alongside human factors such as population pressure, improper farming practices, rapid urbanization, and weak policies.

Desertification:
- Desertification is land degradation specifically occurring in dry, arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas, driven by aridity indices, population pressure, inappropriate land use, and human-derived climate change.
- Strategies to minimize desertification include utilizing Indigenous and Local Knowledge (ILK), water harvesting techniques (micro-basins, planting pits), farming diversification, and growing drought-resilient crops.

7.3 RECURRENT DROUGHTS AND FAMINES
Drought Characteristics and Types:
- Drought is a prolonged deficiency of moisture and water resulting from abnormal rainfall shortages, categorized into meteorological, hydrological, agricultural, environmental, and socioeconomic droughts.
- Mitigation measures include improved drought monitoring, better water and crop management, early-warning capacities, and Soil and Water Conservation (SWC) practices.

Famine:
- Famine is persistent and acute hunger or starvation caused by food and water scarcity, severe malnourishment, and death, often triggered by recurrent droughts, floods, locust invasions, wars, and resource inequalities.
- Mitigation strategies involve humanitarian aid, community rebuilding, strengthening local food system resilience, and promoting long-term economic diversification.

7.4 DEFORESTATION
Ecosystem Services and Causes:
- Forest ecosystems stabilize local climates, control flooding, filter wastes, minimize soil erosion, store carbon stocks, and provide habitats for terrestrial species.
- Deforestation is driven by underlying causes (population pressure, flawed government policies, poverty) and proximate causes (commercial logging, clearing for agriculture, fuel-wood collection, and overgrazing).
- Consequences include carbon stock reduction, loss of biodiversity, disruption of hydrological cycles, and the acceleration of global warming.

7.5 THE WORLDWIDE DIGITAL DIVIDE
Definition and Disparities:
- The digital divide refers to the gap between individuals, households, companies, and geographic areas regarding access to Information Communication Technologies (ICTs) and the internet.
- Key disparities are driven by income shortages, poor connectivity, low levels of education and digital literacy, and geographic location, with access lowest in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa.
- While the digital divide exacerbates income inequality and cyber security risks, digitalization offers powerful advantages such as e-learning, telemedicine, financial services for the unbanked, and real-time weather and market information.
