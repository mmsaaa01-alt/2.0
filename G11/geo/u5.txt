UNIT FIVE: GEOGRAPHY AND ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT

5.1 EFFECTS OF GEOGRAPHIC LOCATION ON DEVELOPMENT
Economic Growth and Development:
- Economic Growth: An increase in goods and services or gross domestic product (GDP) over a specific period, focusing primarily on quantitative growth of national per capita income without fully regarding the standard of living.
- Economic Development: Encompasses both qualitative and quantitative development of a country's economy, focusing on the sustained development of society's material well-being.
- Human Development Index (HDI): A quantitative measure used to assess human well-being and development based on three dimensions: longevity (life expectancy), access to knowledge (literacy rates and years of schooling), and standard of living (real per capita income).

Geographical Determinism vs. Possibilism:
- Geographical Determinism: Assumes that a country's socio-economic development stage is exclusively determined by its geographic environment, such as location, climate, and geomorphology.
- Possibilism: Argues that the geographic environment is not the sole determinant; development is shaped by the interaction between geographic location and human factors, with human ingenuity modifying environmental conditions into usable opportunities.

Impact on Specific Sectors:
- Agricultural Development: Dictated by environmental resources and land use patterns, as demonstrated by von Thünen's Hypothetical Circular Land Use Model, which organizes agricultural yield into concentric zones around a central market based on transport costs and crop perishability.
- Industrial Development: Governed by Alfred Weber's Least Cost Triangular Location Model, which emphasizes transport costs, labor costs, and agglomeration economies, classifying industries as bulk-reducing (weight-losing) or bulk-gaining (weight-gaining).
- Settlement and Service Centers: Explained by Walter Christaller's Central Place Theory, which utilizes a hexagonal pattern based on marketing, transportation, and administrative principles to understand how urban settlements space out and exchange goods.

5.2 CLIMATE EXTREMES AND POVERTY
Climate Extremes and Their Impacts:
- Climate extremes refer to excess temperature and rainfall events—such as heat/cold waves, floods, droughts, and landslides—that negatively impact agricultural yields and human livelihoods.
- Frequent climate shocks trap vulnerable households in cycles of poverty by forcing them to consume or sell productive assets, undermining their capacity to adapt to future hazards.

Flooding Events and Poverty:
- Flash floods destroy human settlements, physical infrastructure, and agricultural fields, leading to widespread food insecurity, displacement, and the breakdown of informal social support networks.

Poverty Traps and Critical Thresholds:
- Poverty traps occur when repeated extreme climate events push poor populations into sustained, long-term destitution.
- Sub-Saharan Africa and South/Southeast Asia experience high concentrations of extreme poverty and vulnerability to climate-induced hazards.

5.3 DISADVANTAGES OF LANDLOCKED COUNTRIES
Characteristics and Disadvantages:
- Landlocked countries (LLDCs) do not possess any seacoast and comprise about one-fifth of the world's nations.
- They face severe economic impediments, including high international transport costs, isolation from major global economic centers, and complete reliance on the transport infrastructure of transit neighbors.
- Political and administrative barriers—such as border fees, transit licenses, security bonds, and regional conflicts—cause severe transit delays and reduce international trade competitiveness.

5.4 INTRAREGIONAL TRADE IN AFRICA
Trends and Drivers:
- Driven by population growth, rising incomes, and urbanization, intra-African agricultural trade has experienced steady growth, led heavily by Regional Economic Communities (RECs) like SADC and COMESA.
- South Africa, Egypt, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania serve as major regional agricultural market players and exporters.

Challenges to Integration:
- High tariffs, non-tariff measures (NTMs), administrative bottlenecks, and poor infrastructure heavily restrict formal intra-African trade and create strong incentives for informal cross-border trade.
- Improving regional agri-food value chains requires strategic investment in transport corridors, processing facilities, and harmonized trade policies.
