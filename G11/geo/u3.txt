UNIT THREE: NATURAL RESOURCES AND CONFLICTS OVER RESOURCES

3.1 THE FUNCTIONS AND MANAGEMENT OF LAND
The Importance and Functions of Land:
- Land is the ultimate resource and foundation for all human activity, serving as a physical commodity, an economic asset, cultural wealth, and an ecological environment.
- Its primary functions include production, waste disposal, economic value, and cultural/religious significance.

Land Management:
- Refers to the proper use and protection of land resources to provide optimal benefits to growing populations while maintaining ecosystem services.
- Prime objective: To control land degradation and balance resource exploitation with regeneration through soil, water, and vegetation conservation.

3.2 RESOURCES UNDER PRESSURE
Soil Resources:
- Facing degradation from soil erosion, nutrient loss, reduction of biological components, and topsoil removal.
- Divided into physical (crusting, compaction), chemical (salinization, acidification), and biological degradation.

Water Resources:
- Vital for life, agriculture, and power generation, but heavily threatened by domestic and industrial pollution, plastic bags, water hyacinth, and oil spills.
- Rapid population growth, climate change, and high water demand are accelerating water scarcity, potentially leading to regional and international water crises.

Forest Resources:
- Provide subsistence, income, climate stabilization, flood control, carbon storage, and habitats for terrestrial species.
- Under pressure from deforestation, large-scale plantation agriculture, livestock ranching, biofuels, and mining, requiring conservation strategies like afforestation and participatory management.

3.3 LAND RESOURCE DEPLETION AND DEGRADATION
Concepts and Causes:
- Resource degradation is the overall loss in quality and quantity of land resources, driven by underlying causes (such as rapid population growth, poverty, and government policies) and proximate causes (like intensive farming, overgrazing, and poor land use).

Consequences and Mitigation:
- Leads to biodiversity loss, desertification, lower agricultural yields, hunger, and poverty.
- Can be checked through land management practices including afforestation, reforestation, area closures, terracing, check dams, mulching, shelterbelts, and crop rotation.

3.4 TRANSBOUNDARY RIVERS
Distribution and Significance:
- Transboundary rivers are watercourses shared by two or more riparian countries, making up about 60% of global freshwater flows.
- They foster socioeconomic integration and regional development, but can also become sources of contention and political friction.

3.5 REGIONAL COOPERATION FOR SUSTAINABLE USE OF SHARED RIVERS
- Shared river basins require regional institutions and bilateral or multilateral agreements to harmonize relations and ensure equitable resource utilization.
- Examples include the Nile Basin Initiative (NBI), the Lesotho Highlands Development Authority (LHDA), and the Zambezi River Authority (ZRA).

3.6 POTENTIAL AND ACTUAL USE OF WATER IN ETHIOPIA, EGYPT, AND THE SUDAN
- Ethiopia, Egypt, and Sudan share the Nile waters, but historical treaties (such as the 1959 bilateral agreement between Egypt and Sudan) heavily favored downstream countries while excluding upstream nations like Ethiopia.
- The construction of projects like the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) has sparked intense debates and contentions over water allocation and regional development rights.

3.7 CONFLICTS OVER RESOURCES
Concepts and Sources:
- Resource conflicts are disputes arising over accessing, controlling, and using natural assets due to asset scarcity, competing interests, or mismanagement.
- General types of resource conflicts include secessionist conflicts in resource-rich areas, disputes over new national laws, grievances over standalone infrastructure projects (mines and dams), and cumulative localized resource pressures.
