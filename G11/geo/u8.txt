UNIT EIGHT: GEO-SPATIAL INFORMATION AND DATA PROCESSING

8.1 REPRESENTATION OF RELIEF FEATURES ON TOPOGRAPHIC MAPS
Basic Concepts of Relief and Topographic Maps:
- Relief represents the difference in elevation between the highest and lowest points on a landscape, while a topographic map provides a detailed, two-dimensional illustration of natural and man-made three-dimensional surface features.

Methods of Representing Relief:
- Hachures: Short disconnected lines drawn in the direction of the ground slope, used to show slopes, escarpments, and depressions, though they do not indicate exact heights.
- Contours (Isohypses): Imaginary lines connecting places of equal elevation above a datum or mean sea level, colored brown and drawn at regular intervals (e.g., 20 m on 1:50,000 maps).
- Contour Properties: They never cross or branch (except at vertical cliffs), and their spacing indicates slope steepness (closely spaced means steep, widely spaced means gentle).

Contour Patterns for Specific Landforms:
- Hill: Represented by concentric circles with increasingly smaller closed contours at higher elevations.
- Valley: Illustrated by "V" or "U" shapes where the closed end points upstream or toward high ground.
- Ridge: Formed by U-shaped or V-shaped patterns with closed ends pointing away from high ground.
- Depression: Shown by closed circular contours with tick marks pointing inward toward the low ground.
- Plateau: Represented by roughly rectangular closed contours with wide, closed top surfaces.
- Spur: Illustrated by U or V shapes directing away from high ground.
- Cliff: Represented by merged or extremely close contour lines where the last line has tick marks pointing toward low ground.

Drainage Representation:
- Drainage basins and watersheds can be identified on contour maps by tracing ridge lines, saddles, and the "V" shapes of tributary streams.
- Common drainage patterns include dendritic (homogeneous rock), trellis (alternating hard and softer rocks), radial (flowing outward from a high feature like a volcano), and centripetal (flowing inward to a central basin).

8.2 BASIC CONCEPTS OF GEOGRAPHICAL INFORMATION SYSTEM (GIS)
Definition and Importance:
- A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a computer-based system for capturing, preparing, storing, retrieving, manipulating, analyzing, and displaying geographically referenced data.
- It supports decision-making in environmental science, social science, resource management, and urban planning.

Five Key Components of GIS:
- Hardware: Technical equipment including computers, high-capacity processors, plotters, and GPS devices.
- Software: Tools required to input, store, manage, and analyze geographic information, such as ArcGIS (ArcMap, ArcCatalog) and open-source alternatives like QGIS.
- Data: Divided into raster data (cells or grids representing continuous layers like elevation and temperature) and vector data (points, lines, and polygons representing discrete spatial features), alongside non-spatial attribute data.
- People: GIS users and specialists who manage, analyze, and apply the system to real-world problems.
- Methods: Well-designed plans, models, and operating rules unique to each organization.

8.3 ARCMAP AND MAIN TOOLS
ArcMap Document Management:
- ArcMap document files (.mxd) store map elements and pointers to file locations, meaning they refer to data rather than storing the data directly.
- Views: ArcMap provides a Data View for geographic exploration and real-world coordinates, and a Layout View for arranging map elements like titles, north arrows, and scale bars on a page.

Main Tools and Components:
- ArcToolbox: An integrated application containing logically organized geoprocessing toolsets such as the Analysis Toolbox, Cartography Toolbox, and Data Management Toolbox.
- ArcCatalog: Used for file management and developing metadata for GIS data.
- Data Layer Order: In the Table of Contents (TOC), point features sit on top, followed by lines, polygons, and raster datasets at the bottom.
